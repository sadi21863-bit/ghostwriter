# The Cartographer of Small Hours

_Novel · linear · story type: linear · genres: urban quiet fantasy, mystery · 8446 words_

## 1. The Small Hours

The city gave up its extra streets at 3:04 most mornings, and Noor Haddad had eleven minutes to get them on paper before they changed their minds.

She worked at the night desk on the fourth floor of the Municipal Survey Office, a room with too many lamps and not enough windows, where the heating pipes knocked twice an hour like a man asking to be let in. Her tools were unremarkable: a rotring pen, a straightedge, a stack of gridded sheets stamped PROPERTY OF THE CITY — NOT FOR CIRCULATION. The city did not know it owned these streets. That was part of the arrangement.

At 3:00 the ink on her ruler went cold. It always did.

The paper in front of her showed a corridor that had not been there when she'd set it down. Not a street yet — streets were a later, more confident stage — but the suggestion of one, the way a bruise suggests a blow. It ran east off Vell Street between a shuttered pharmacy and a shuttered notary's, and it was twelve feet wide, and it smelled faintly, impossibly, of cut hay.

Noor measured it. Twelve feet. She noted the smell in the margin, because the margins were where you were honest.

By 3:11 the hay-smell had gone and the corridor had narrowed to nine feet and acquired a slope. She drew the slope. She had learned not to argue with the small hours; you recorded what you were given and you did not ask the street to justify itself. Couriers did the same with signatures. Coroners with livers.

At 3:19 she heard the lift.

The lift should not have been running. The lifts in the Survey Office were shut down at eleven, chained by a porter who took his duties and his hip flask equally seriously, and the building's night wiring ran only the desk lamps and the cold-water tap in the washroom. But she heard it: the long groan, the cable's complaint, the doors opening on her floor with a sound like a polite cough.

She put the pen down and slid the gridded sheet into the drawer, face down, which was the procedure and also good manners.

Elio Voss came in carrying two paper cups and the particular carefulness of a man who had already broken something tonight.

"Night desk," he said, as he did. "I've brought you the wrong tea."

"You always bring me the wrong tea."

"I bring you the tea you drink." He set a cup on the one clean corner of her desk, avoiding the map drawer as if it might be warm. "It's the tea I get wrong."

He was fifty-one, though he looked like a document someone had folded too many times. Wool coat, good shoes, no umbrella even in weather that required one. He had been coming up on the wrong lift for eight months, roughly, always between three and four, always with two cups, always with the air of a man waiting for a train he knew had been cancelled. Noor had asked him once, in the fourth month, why he didn't come during the day.

"Nothing's here during the day," he'd said, and drunk his tea.

Tonight his hands were worse than usual. He held the cup with both, which he did when he wanted them to stop.

"There's a room," he said.

"Rooms I don't do. You know what I do." She uncapped her pen to make the point, and found her fingers had already left it.

"It's not far. Two streets over, the way you count it."

"Elio."

"It has a south window. Cream curtains. There's a crack in the wall shaped like—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, as if reading from a card he'd written in a language he'd forgotten. "There's a room where my wife went. Two years ago. You could put it on a map."

Noor drank the tea. It was the wrong tea, and he was right that it was the tea she drank.

"I map streets," she said. "I map the streets between three and four. They have names, most of them, and numbers, and by dawn they're gone and the city doesn't remember them, and I'm the only one who ever knew. That's the whole trick of it. Streets leave things behind — a curb, a stain, a drainage grate the sewer crews find in 1974 and can't explain. A room doesn't leave anything. A room's a private thing. It belongs to whoever walks into it."

"She walked into it. She didn't walk out."

"Then it's not a room anymore. It's a hole. I draw surfaces." She set her cup down, gentler than she meant to. "I'm sorry. I can't help you."

He nodded, slowly, the way a man nods when he's already decided something and is waiting for the conversation to catch up.

He put his own cup on her desk — untouched, still lidded — and reached into his coat and laid a folded square of paper beside it. Not much larger than a playing card. Creased so many times it had gone soft as cloth at the folds.

"Just look at it," he said. "You don't have to do anything. You're the only person I know who'd believe the size of it."

She should have refused. She had refused, once, in a manner of speaking, and refusals of this kind are like drains — you clear them once and they stay clear, or you don't and the water finds its own way.

He was already at the door. The lift coughed for him, patient as a dog.

"Sector nine," he said, not turning. "Brume's got the file. He's had it two years and he's never once been able to draw the room either."

The doors closed.

Noor sat with the tea going cold and the folded paper where he'd left it, and did not touch it for a full minute and eleven seconds, which took her all the way to 3:41.

Then she unfolded it, and saw the south window, and the cream curtains, and the crack in the wall shaped like something she couldn't name, and in the bottom corner, in Elio's small square hand, a compass rose he'd drawn himself. He'd got the north wrong. He'd got the north wrong by about the width of a room.

## 2. The Regular

He came back on the Tuesday. He came back on the Wednesday, and the Thursday, and after that Noor stopped being surprised and started being punctual — she had the visitor's chair turned toward the desk before the lift coughed, which was a thing she elected not to examine.

The tea was always wrong, and the wrongness varied. A cardamom thing that tasted of soap. A bitter green she drank out of politeness and then out of thirst. Once a chai so sweet her teeth ached until five. He drank his own standing, coat on, and he never took the coat off, and somewhere in the second month she had decided not to ask him about it.

He gave her one detail a night. Never more. It was like watching a man pay off a debt in coins — he would not be hurried and he would not be short.

She drew what he gave her. This was not the procedure, and she did it on the backs of the gridded sheets so the night's real work stayed clean on the front. A kitchen. Her scale was 1:48, which was the scale for interiors, and it suited the smallness of the thing.

"Two doors," he said, the first night. "One at each end. Facing."

She drew them, and the nine feet between them. "Windows?"

"None."

Her pen stopped. She took the folded card from under the corner of the blotter, where she had left it because she had not decided what else to do with it, and opened it, and smoothed it with the side of her hand. South window. Cream curtains. A crack shaped like something she hadn't named.

Elio looked at it for a long while. He turned his cup a quarter turn, then another quarter.

"That's the other room," he said.

"Elio."

"There's the room you go into and the room you're in. Some nights they aren't the same room." He said it flatly, as if it were a clause in the building code.

She wrote it in the margin, because the margins were where you were honest. Later, when he'd gone, she measured his compass rose with the dividers. He'd put north off by nine feet. A room's width, and the room was nine feet.

The details came one a night, doled out. A stove with four rings and one lit. A formica table, chipped at the northeast corner. Four chairs, three pushed in and one pulled out, and the pulled-out one drawn back far enough that whoever left it had left in a hurry or a temper. A calendar two years out of date, still showing a photograph of a reservoir.

"The lino's worn," he said on the fifth night. "You can see the path."

"Path to the table?"

"Path to the other door." He set his cup down. "It never goes to the table."

She drew the wear as a dotted line, because there was a symbol for wear and she was not going to invent one. Then she sat back and looked at what she had made, and it was a kitchen the way a held breath is a breath — the shape of one, with the thing itself missing.

"I'm not drawing the room," she said. "I want that understood. I'm drawing your account of it."

He nodded, slow, and drank his tea, and did not say what they both already knew.

On the ninth night he gave her the name.

"Ilse," he said. "Ilse Voss."

"Ilse Voss," Noor repeated, because she read back what she recorded, always, out loud — an old clerk's habit, and a courtesy, and a way of checking the spelling without asking.

Her ruler went cold.

She looked at it lying there on the sheet, brass edge, and she did not touch it. Outside, a delivery van came up Vell and stopped and started again, and the radiator clanked its one note, and Noor sat very still with a name in her mouth that she had tasted before.

The wife. Elio had a wife, and the wife had a name, and the name had been in her mouth before.

"Say it again," she said.

"Ilse Voss."

"Ilse." She turned it over. It was not a name from the Office, not a courier or a coroner or a claimant. It was older than that. It was older than the small-hours shift. "I know that name."

Elio's hands closed around his cup, both of them, the way he did when he wanted them to stop. "I know," he said. "That's why I picked you."

"That's not—" She stopped. She was aware of the drawer beside her knee, and the sheet in it, and the corridor off Vell running east between the pharmacy and the notary's, nine feet wide and sloping, and she was aware of a thing she could not yet put a shape to, the way you are aware of a stair in the dark.

He left at 3:47 without being asked to. The lift coughed for him. The doors closed.

At 3:52 she took the back stairs to records, three flights, the handrail cold through her sleeve, and let herself in with the key that was not supposed to be copied and was.

Sector nine, demolition surveys, the year she'd been twenty-five and junior and had spent her days hatching out buildings nobody would miss. The ledger was where it should be. The entry was where it should be, in her own hand, small and square and unmistakable — north side, between the pharmacy and the notary's, unsafe, no entry, recommendation: clear.

Tenant of record: I. Voss.

The plan sheet behind the entry had been cut out. Not torn. Cut, with a blade, close to the binding, so the stub looked almost intentional. In the corner of the stub, in ink that was not hers and not the Office's, a single initial.

Noor stood in the cold of the records room and put her palm flat on the ledger, and remembered. She remembered the hatch marks. She remembered standing in a kitchen with two doors and no windows and telling herself that a kitchen without a window is not a kitchen, it is a cellar, and the survey said above ground — and so she had drawn it as a corridor, because that was a thing a corridor could be.

She had been drawing that room for years, and calling it a street.

## 3. The Sealed File

The cross-reference card was in the pocket at the back of the ledger, where the pocket was meant to hold a plan and instead held a card, and the card said: *Voss, I. — B/2291/CL — SEALED — Brume.*

Sealed files at the Office were not locked. That was the joke of them. They lived in the held cabinet with a paper band around them and the closing officer's name on the band, and any key in the building would lift the band. The seal was not the lock. The seal was the signature, and the signature meant that lifting it put your name in a ledger beside his.

She went up three flights at 4:40 with the handrail cold through her other sleeve and sat at her own desk with the lamp off, and thought about it the way she thought about contours: where the ground falls away, and how fast.

Then she did the thing that made her want to be sick, which was the correct thing. She filled in a Records Transfer slip.

There was a box on the slip for *Reason for inspection*. She wrote *survey currency* and did not underline it, because underlining was how you told a lie you were proud of. She signed, dated it, and dropped it in the tray instead of the night hatch, so that it would be handled at eleven by a person rather than at five by a machine, and so that there would be a person who remembered her face.

The file was on her desk when she came on at ten the next night, banded, thirty-one pages, and heavier than paper.

She put her tea down without drinking it and did not turn around to see whether anyone had stayed to watch.

The first page was a missing person report, four years old, in the flat hand of an officer who had not been interested yet. *Voss, Ilse, thirty-four. Reported by husband, Voss, Elio. Last seen the eleventh of March.* Habitual address given as a street Noor had surveyed and could not now put a number to.

The second page was a statement. A woman from the floor above, eighty-one, deaf on the left, who had heard, some time after two, a door at one end of a room and then a door at the other end, and then nothing, and who wanted it recorded that the second door had not been opened in years. She described the room to the officer because the officer asked her to. Two doors, facing. No windows. A stove with four rings, one of them lit. A path worn in the lino, and the officer had written *path to?* in the margin, and the woman had said, *away.*

Noor turned the page with one finger and found that her other hand had gone to the drawer by her knee and was resting on it.

The exhibit list was on page nineteen. Items one through three: a coat, a handbag, a calendar. Item four, described in the flat clerical way that removes a thing from the world even as it records it: *Four sheets, municipal survey, sector nine, grease pencil and ink, in the hand of N. Haddad, junior surveyor. Returned to Office.*

She read it twice. The radiator clanked its one note and she let it.

Page thirty was Brume, and Brume's handwriting was small and level and did not hurry, and it said that the premises described by the witness did not appear on the register, on the rates book, on the valuation list, or on any deposited plan; that the survey of the sector had been carried out by this Office and showed no such interior; that there was no address to search and no landlord to interview; and that the recommendation was no further action, the file to be held, the band to be signed.

Below it, in the same even hand, one line that had not been struck out and had not been explained. *Surveyor to be spoken to.*

Noor sat with that line for a while. She did not remember being spoken to. She remembered the year: twenty-five, junior, hatching out buildings nobody would miss, and a run of weeks in the spring she could not lay flat — a gap in her own memory the shape of a room.

She turned to the inside of the band. The sign-out card was pasted there, and there were four lines on it, and the fourth was hers, dated yesterday, and the third was Brume's, dated four years ago, and the second was Brume's too, and the first had been scratched out so hard the pen had gone through the card.

The lift coughed.

She did not turn around. She had learned that much from the job — that turning around tells the other person you were doing something.

Inspector Brume came as far as the corner of the desk and stopped, and stood with his hat in his hand, and looked at the open file the way a man looks at weather he has already been out in.

"Your slip said survey currency," he said.

"It's a survey."

"It's four sheets of one." He turned the file a quarter turn with one finger, and then another quarter, and Noor felt the back of her neck go cold, because she had seen a man turn a cup that way nine nights running. "I closed it on your evidence," Brume said. "I want you to understand that. Not in spite of it. My lads had nothing. Your sheets were the best work in the file and they said there was no room there, and I have never in twenty-six years had a better reason to close a case."

"Somebody cut the plan out of the ledger."

"I know." He said it without weight. "I've known four years."

She looked down at her own square small hand on page nineteen, the hand of a woman who had believed a corridor was a thing a corridor could be, and she was aware of the two of them standing either side of the desk and the nine feet of dark between the lamps.

"The eleventh of March," she said. "Every year he comes in the first week of March and sits in the corner with his tea."

"He's early, then."

"He's early." She closed the file. "Inspector. Why are you here at eleven at night to tell me you knew?"

Brume set his hat down on the corner of the sheet and left it there, over the nine feet, and did not take his hand away.

## 4. Two Doors, No Windows

Brume lifted his hat off the sheet and put it on his head, and the nine feet of dark between the lamps was still there, and would be, she suspected, for the rest of her career.

"You'll want the address," she said.

"I know the address. I've stood outside it." He was at the door before he said the rest. "Take the lamp. Sign for it."

Elio was on the step with his collar up and a flask in his coat pocket and the particular stillness of a man who has already decided not to ask for anything twice. He had been early in March for four years. She had never once asked him why, in four years, he had chosen a Records Office at midnight to drink his tea in.

She signed out a survey lamp and a tin of brass pins, and entered the numbers in the book, and initialled them, because the difference between a mistake and a crime was a legible hand.

The city at three was doing its small private business. A bread van with its sidelights on, idling outside a shutter that had not yet gone up. A man hosing the pavement in front of a restaurant because the pavement had been dirty since the evening and would be dirty again by noon. The tram, empty, lit, going to the depot with the resignation of something that knows it is not needed. Noor kept her lamp off. The street did not want it yet.

Elio counted doors. He did it silently, moving his lips, the way some men count stairs. She had noticed it in the third week and had not asked, and tonight she understood that he was checking the street against itself, and that the street was passing.

"It's here," he said.

It was a doorway between a printer's and a shuttered unit with a faded sign for a dancing school. The printer's had a number. The dancing school had a number. This had a lintel, and a step, and a handle, and no number, and Noor knew the street, and knew she had surveyed it, and that her own sheets said the printer's was next to the dancing school, wall to wall, and no gap.

She wrote it down anyway. That was the job. You wrote down what was there.

The stair went up two flights in the dark and turned at a landing where somebody had once painted the rail green. On the second floor there was one door and it was open. She put the lamp on and went in ahead of him, because that was also the job.

The kitchen was ordinary, and it was not. There were two doors, facing each other across six feet of floor, and no windows, and the room had the proportions of somewhere that gets its light from the street and had lost it. A stove with four rings. One of them lit, blue, small, patient. Lino with a path worn in it, and the path went from between the doors to the middle of the room and stopped there, and did not lead to the stove, or the sink, or anywhere a person would go.

The air tasted of tea. Not the smell of tea — the taste, on the back of her tongue, the taste of the tea in the chipped cup he set on the corner of her desk, and which he had never named and she had never asked about.

Noor stood in the doorway and did her job. She counted paces between the doors, and paced the lino, and noted the stove, and the single ring, and the absence of a window, and the worn path and its terminus, and she wrote it all in the small square hand she had been taught, because a room that appears on no ledger can still be surveyed, and a surveyor is only ever a person who wrote down what was there.

Elio stayed in the doorway. He did not look at the second door, the one at the far end, the one the neighbour said had not opened in years. He looked at the floor.

"She used to say it opened," he said. "At night. She said she could hear the room on the other side of it."

"Did you ever hear it?"

"I never stayed." He turned the flask over in his hands. "I brought her tea. In the mornings. That's what I could do."

She chose the east wall, the one with no door in it. She took a brass pin from the tin, and set its point against the plaster, and pushed with the heel of her hand, expecting the resistance of lath, and it went in the way a pin goes into a wall that wants one. She left an inch and a head showing, and it was there, and it was a mark, and tomorrow, whatever else, she could say she had marked it.

"Three fifty," she said.

They stood. The ring clicked off, and the room was darker for it. Then there was a door at one end of a room and a door at the other end, one after the other, close as breath.

At four she was on a landing in the dark with a lamp in her hand and Elio beside her, and the rail was green, and there were two flights down and a printer's and a dancing school and a wall between them. She looked for a pin. There was plaster, and the flattening of the tram tickets pasted there and never claimed.

She walked back the way she had come and got in at four forty and did her returns, and signed the lamp back in.

The tin had five pins in it. She had drawn five and left one in a wall. She tipped them out onto the desk and looked at them for a while, and the radiator clanked its one note, and she let it.

## 5. The Pin

The drawer stuck the way it stuck in winter, and Noor put her shoulder into it the way she did in winter, and the pin was in with the pencil sharpener and the spare staples.

She had signed it into a wall on Tuesday. It was Wednesday.

The pin was bent at the shoulder, forty degrees off true, in the manner of a pin that has been drawn out sideways and thought better of the angle. Beneath it, a slip of paper — office issue, the thick kind, torn from the pad in the third drawer — and on the slip, her own hand. The small square capitals she had been taught at nineteen by a man who said that a letter which can be read two ways is a letter that will be.

*Pin recovered. Bent at shoulder. Position per grid.*

Six figures after that, and her initials, and the date.

She read the date twice. She read the grid reference four times. She turned the slip over, and there was nothing on the back, which was the only thing about it that behaved.

The ledger was where it lived, on the shelf under the returns box, and the ledger said five drawn, five signed back, and her own initial in the column, and the pin count in the tin had been five when she tipped it out at four forty and looked at it and let the radiator clank.

She put the bent pin in the ashtray that held paperclips and set the slip under the corner of the desk blotter, and went out with the survey lamp at eleven the way she did every night, and the street was ordinary, and the pin was in the drawer when she came back at four fifty, and so was the slip, and neither of them had moved, which she found she minded more than if they had.

Inspector Brume came at five twenty, when the office was grey and the radiator had given up.

He did not sit. He stood with his back to the window and his hat in both hands and looked at the shelf of ledgers as though counting them.

"Your returns are the cleanest in the building," he said. "That's how I know. Nobody's returns are that clean."

"They're clean because I do them."

"Tuesday night you drew five pins and returned five, and your sheet says you left one in a wall." He turned the hat a quarter turn. "I've read a lot of returns. In thirty years I've never read one that corrected itself."

She said nothing. The chipped cup was on the corner of the desk where it went on the mornings Elio came, and this was not March, and it was empty, and she had set it out anyway.

"The room you surveyed isn't in my file," Brume said. "That's not the same as it not being known. There are eleven surveys in that file going back to nineteen fifty-one and every one of them is by a person who did the job properly." He looked at her then, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "Two of them are dead of being old. One of them is in a home in Ostend and doesn't know her own name. The rest stopped writing things down. That's the whole of it. They stopped writing things down."

"I'm a cartographer," Noor said. "Not writing things down isn't on offer."

"I'm not telling you to stop." He set his hat on his head, and then took it off again. "The file's sealed. You'll have heard that. What you won't have heard is where. The seal isn't on the cover. It's on the fourth page, and it's my own hand and my own stamp, and I don't remember putting it there." He put the hat on. "I've been a policeman since I was twenty-two. I know my own hand."

He was at the door when he said the rest, and he said it to the door.

"The man with the flask. He's not a witness. Grief makes a person sincere and sincerity is not evidence." A pause. "But he's been early in March for four years and you've never once asked him why, and I'd like you to notice that you've never once asked him why."

When he had gone she took the slip from under the blotter and set it on the large sheet, and got the scale rule and the dividers, and did the job.

The six figures fell in the block she knew. Printer's, dancing school, wall to wall. She plotted the point and ruled the street north from it in 2H, and the line ran eleven inches and stopped.

She set the pencil in the margin and made a test line, and the paper took it. She sharpened. She ruled again, from the south, and the line ran and stopped in the same place.

The stopping had an edge to it. Not a smudge, not a lift of the hand — an edge, clean and ruled, the way a corner is trimmed, and past it the paper was paper and the ink was in the pot and nothing was wrong except that eleven inches of street had ended and there was nothing on the other side to end against.

She measured the blank with the dividers. Then she measured it again, and wrote the figure in the corner in the small square hand, and looked at the figure for a while.

Six feet.

## 6. The Blank

She stepped off the kerb at the six-foot mark and the street went on.

Not far. It went on the way a held breath goes on — everything in its place, everything accounted for, nothing arriving. The lamp at the corner was lit the way it was lit at three. The bakery on Fenwick had its shutters up and its trays full and the woman behind the glass reading a newspaper with her lips moving, and the headline said the same thing twice.

Noor walked north with the lamp in her left hand and her footsteps came back off the wall half a beat late, which is a thing she had read about in a surveyor's memoir and had never once put in a report.

The tram at the top of the street stood at the stop with its doors open and the driver reading a folded page. She passed it, and did not turn, and passed it again. He was on the same page. The indicator ticked. It had been ticking a long while, and it never turned.

The clock over the dancing school said three fifty-one. She stood under it and counted to three hundred in the way she counted, and it said three fifty-one, and the second hand had not moved so much as the width of a hair.

Between the printer's and the dancing school the wall had a door in it that she had drawn eleven times and never seen.

She went up. The stairs smelled of the same dust as the stairs at the office, which was not a comfort.

The room had a table and a lamp and a window with the curtain half drawn and a woman in it mending the cuff of a shirt.

She was warm. That was the first thing, and Noor had not expected it, and it made her angry in a way she filed for later. The woman had her hair down and her sleeves rolled and a cup at her elbow going cold, and she looked up without surprise, the way a person looks up when the kettle has finished.

"You'll want to sit," she said. "There's only the one chair and I'm in it, so you'll want to want to."

Noor stood. She got out the notebook. The woman watched her do it and did not object, which was worse than objecting.

"I know what you are," the woman said. "You're the one he brings tea to."

The pencil stopped.

"He's early," the woman said. "In March. He's always early in March."

"Four years," Noor said.

The needle went into the cuff and stayed there a moment. "Four years," the woman said, and then she went on stitching, and her stitches were neat and slightly too long, the way a person sews who has stopped caring whether it will be seen.

"You could come out," Noor said. "The door's open. It's been open since I came up."

"I know. It's been open a great deal longer than that." She bit the thread. "The first year I stood at the top of the stairs with my coat on. Two years, perhaps. There's no telling in here. Then I took the coat off, and after that it was a different question, and the different question was easier."

"Easier."

"Out there I'm a woman who disappeared. It's a whole occupation. Every hour of the day I'd be doing it — at the shops, at the post office, in the way people would look up and then look down." She smoothed the cuff. "In here I'm a woman reading. I'd rather be that. You can write that down, if you like. It's not a tragedy, it's an arrangement."

Noor wrote it down. Her hand was steady and she disliked it.

"There's a file," Noor said. "Inspector Brume's. The seal is on the fourth page and it's in his own hand and he doesn't remember putting it there."

The woman's needle paused at nothing. "He was very polite," she said. "He had a fountain pen and a stamp and he asked me a great many questions in a particular order, and then he went down and the stairs took it out of him the way stairs do." She turned the shirt. "He left his signature. He'll have that. The room is generous with the shape of things and stingy with the rest."

Noor put the notebook down on the corner of the table, and squared it, and then said the thing she had come to say and had not known she would say. "I have to draw this. It's the position. I've got a blank on the sheet six feet across and I'm the only person in this city who can fill it."

"Yes."

"Then you understand why I can't promise."

"I understand that you can't promise," the woman said. "I'm asking anyway." She set the mending in her lap. "Because a map is a promise that a thing can be found, and finding is not the same as fetching, and there will be men with fountain pens and there will be an order to the questions, and after that I'll be a page. I've been a page. It's warm in here and I've been a page." She looked up. "Leave the room off. You can leave a thing off. It's a decision, not an accident. You of all people know the difference."

The lamp on the table guttered and did not go out.

Noor took the pin from her coat, the office pin, and walked to the turn of the stair, and drove it into the plaster at the shoulder, forty degrees off true, and left it there.

"That's not a map," she said.

"No," the woman agreed. "It's a pin."

At four fifty she was at her desk with her coat still on. The street outside was ordinary. The clock over the dancing school, which she could not see from the window but could see from the step, was going.

She tipped the tin out and counted. Five. She counted again, and put them back, and the bent one lay on top with its shoulder wrong.

Then she got the scale rule and the 2H and ruled the street north from the dancing school, and the line ran, and went on running past eleven inches and past twelve, and closed over the place like water.

She wrote the return in the small square hand. *Position per grid. Nothing at the position.* And beneath it, because a return is a return and she had never in her life signed a page she had not measured: *Six feet, occupied.*

## 7. The Choice

Brume had been in the other chair long enough to leave a ring in the dust. His hat sat on the desk beside the file, which was thick and soft at the corners and had a seal on the fourth page in his own hand.

"Draw the room," he said. "Grid, north arrow, scale. I'll take it down to the register myself and I'll initial it."

"And then."

"And then the file goes in the stove. Four years of it. Nobody climbs that stair again, nobody sits in a kitchen asking a man to go over the Tuesday for the ninth time." He turned his hat a quarter inch. "I'd have done it in the first year if I'd had a sheet to put in front of the board. A thing that isn't on a map isn't a place. It's a complaint."

"You want to knock the wall down."

"I want to send men with hammers instead of men with questions. Hammers are finished when they're finished." He said it without heat, which was the worst of it. "You've been up."

Noor did not answer.

"There's a pin in the plaster at the turn of the stair," Brume said. "Drove it in at a slant, like a boundary mark. I got as far as the turn on Tuesday. The stairs took it out of me the way stairs do." He looked at his hands. "I stood there a while and looked at your pin and went back down. So. You've been up."

"Yes."

"And you'll want to know what I'd do about her." He shook his head slowly. "Nothing. She's a witness who won't be sworn and a complaint that won't sign. I've had four years of her and I've got a drawer of paper and no page with a body on it. If the room is on the map, the room is a room, and I close the file on the room. She isn't in it."

"No."

"No. She isn't in it." He put his hat on. "I'm at the desk till six. After six I'm a man with a hat on, going home."

He got as far as the door and stopped, and said, to the frame rather than to her, that he had been very polite up there, and that he remembered the pen and the questions, and that he could not for the life of him remember the fourth page, and that he was sixty-one years old and had begun to think it was the room's doing and not his.

Then he went down, and the stairs took it out of him.

---

She took the return out of the drawer and read it once. The street north from the dancing school, ruled true, past eleven inches and past twelve, closing over the place like water. Her own small square hand. *Position per grid. Nothing at the position.* And underneath, because a return is a return and she had never in her life signed a page she had not measured: *Six feet, occupied.*

She lit the corner with a match and held it over the tin ashtray, and the flame went along the ruled street the way water had gone, and the six feet went last, and she turned the ash with the end of the pencil until there was nothing on it that could be read.

---

Elio came at twenty past five with two cups and his hat under his arm and the rain in his shoulders.

"You found the blank," he said.

"I found the blank."

He set the cups down and did not sit. "I've been thinking about it all night. If it's on a sheet with a grid on it, then it's a place. Then a person can go to it." His voice was careful, laid out like cutlery. "I'm not asking you to fetch her. I know the difference. I'm asking you to find."

Noor looked at her own hands. Then she took the notebook and put it in the drawer and shut the drawer, which was the only honest sentence she had.

"I can't draw it," she said. "And it isn't the position. The position I'd draw. It's that a map is a promise, and the promise is that you can come back."

"I don't need to come back."

"That's the part I'd be drawing," she said. "That's what would be on the sheet."

He stood there with the rain in his shoulders and his mouth working, and she thought, this is the minute, this is where I decide what kind of mapmaker I am, and she found that she had already decided, at four fifty-one, with a pin.

"She's at peace," Noor said.

He did not move for a moment. Then he sat, and put his hand flat on the table, and let out a breath that had been in him four years. "You saw her."

"I saw the room."

"She's at peace."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, and then again, faster, and then he looked at the window with the curtain half drawn and said, "She used to do my cuffs. Sit up half the night over them, hair down, sleeves rolled, telling me they were only cuffs and I'd want them right." He drank his tea. "That's all. I wanted somebody to have seen her being herself. It's a stupid thing to want."

"It isn't stupid."

"No." He turned his cup a quarter inch, the way Brume had turned his hat. "She'd rather have been reading. That's what she'd have said, if you'd asked her what she'd rather." He said it lightly, as a thing long known, and Noor's hand closed around the pencil hard enough to print the make.

He left the second cup. She heard him go down.

---

She took a fresh sheet and squared it and ruled the street north from the dancing school, past eleven inches, past twelve, and where the blank had been six feet across she hatched it in the conventional way, close and even, and wrote in the small square hand at the corner of it: *Not surveyed.*

Her signature went under it before she could think about it further.

The clock over the dancing school, which she could see from the step, was going. The pin was still in the plaster at the shoulder of the stair, forty degrees off true, and it was the only mark she had made all night that she was prepared to stand behind.

## 8. Morning

The streets stopped coming on a Tuesday.

Not all at once, and not with any announcement. She trimmed the lamp and sharpened the pencils and squared the ward sheet under the straightedge, and between three and four the city was only the city. The grid held. Everything lay where the survey had put it, eleven inches to the chain, and nothing came up through the paper that had not been there at midnight.

She walked it at four to be sure. The dancing school was dark. The stair was a stair. The pin was in the plaster at the shoulder of the turn, and she did not touch it.

The second night the same. The third night she brought a second lamp and a fresh nib and sat until five, and the page stayed a page. The small hours had gone ordinary on her, the way a word goes ordinary when you say it too many times in a row.

On Thursday she put in for days.

---

The day room had west light and four other desks and a woman named Petronella who kept a jar of boiled sweets and said *good morning* as though it were a measurement. The day hands measured in metres now and went home at five and did not look at the clock over the dancing school, which was wrong by four minutes and had been wrong when she started and would be wrong when she finished.

She spent the morning on Fenwick Lane, where the bakery had moved its awning post fourteen inches east without telling anyone, and the afternoon on a wall by the canal that had been rebuilt a foot and a half inside its old line by a builder who had gone into the sea air and stayed there. She drew both. Her hand was steady. The sun was on the paper and showed her thumbprint in the margin and she left it.

It was the same work. It was exactly the same work, with the sun on it.

Brume came by on the Friday and stood at the end of the desk with his hat in his hand and did not sit.

“The room’s been struck from the small-hours returns,” he said. “I did the striking. The drawer’s gone down to the basement. That’s where the city puts a file when it has decided the file was never a file.”

“And the room?”

“The room is a room.” He turned his hat a quarter inch. “There’s a young man comes in of an evening and sits at the table and doesn’t ask me anything. That’s the whole of it now. A man who doesn’t ask.” He put the hat on. “You look well. Days suit you. I’d not have said so.”

“Nobody would.”

“No,” he agreed, and went, and did not look at the clock either.

The plate room sent her own sheet back that afternoon with a docket pinned to it, and she stood at the window and looked at it a while. Where she had hatched six feet across, close and even, and written *Not surveyed* in the corner in her small square hand, the plate had taken the hatching as land. It was drawn in the same weight as the walls. It was property. It had a boundary and a boundary is a thing that can be conveyed, and somewhere in the basement a man with a drawer would have said, if anyone had asked him, that a thing with a boundary on it is a place.

She did not correct it.

---

She found the pin on the Monday, in the coat she had not worn since, in the seam of the pocket where it had worked itself through the lining and stood at an angle to the cloth. It was bent. It had been bent when she drove it in at four fifty-one, forty degrees off true, and it was the only mark she had made that year that she was prepared to stand behind, and she had pulled it out of the plaster with her fingers and put it in her pocket and forgotten it entirely.

She held it up. Bent, blunt, four inches of iron, worth nothing, the sort of thing that falls out of a joist and lies in the dust under a floor for a hundred years.

On Friday she came in at a quarter to seven, in the daylight, before the small-hours shift would start.

The night desk was as it was. The lamp was not lit. The chair with the bad caster, the tin ashtray, the drawer with the notebook in it, shut, and nobody had asked her for the key. The table where Elio sat was bare except for the ring his cup had made in the varnish, which was old and would not come out.

She pushed the pin into the soft pine at the same forty degrees, and it stood.

There was no note. A note would have had her name on it. The pin was already in her hand.

She looked at it a moment standing there, and then she put her hat on and went down, and the stairs took it out of her the way stairs do, and outside the clock over the dancing school was four minutes wrong and going.

---

She slept until seven and did not wake at three.

In the morning she made tea in the cup with the make worn off it and stood at her own window over her own street, which she had never surveyed, which had been ruled onto the ward map in a stranger’s hand in 1898, and which was precisely where she had left it. The map was a promise and the promise was that you could come back. She had come back. The city had put a wall where the hatch was, and the wall was a wall, and the street went north the way the street went north.

She drank her tea and did not go down to look at it again.

Somebody would set a cup on that table before six and find it standing in the pine, and would have to decide, with no sheet to put in front of a board, what it was.
