# The Glass Orchard

_Screenplay · linear · genres: quiet sci-fi mystery + romance · 3704 words_

## 1. Tuning Fork

INT. GREENHOUSE - NIGHT

Rain on glass.

A flashlight beam rakes a gravel aisle — iron pipe, rain barrel, a bench of clay pots — and climbs.

Glass fruit hangs from espaliered frames in rows. Pears, mostly. A few apples. The vines they hang from are dead: brown, split, not a leaf left on them. The fruit doesn't seem to mind. Clear ones take the beam and pass it on. The amber ones give it back thick as honey.

VERA LINDQVIST, 58, wedges the flashlight against a pot, aimed at the roof. The house comes up grey-green. Wool coat over a nightdress. Rubber boots, no socks.

From the coat she takes a felt roll and lays it flat: seven tuning forks in stitched slots. She picks the fourth — steel, wooden handle, the grip worn pale where a thumb has been.

A rap on the bench edge. A clean A fills the air.

She carries the fork to the nearest pear and sets the handle against its stem.

Glass hangs. The note fades.

Nothing.

Down the row: rap, stem, wait. Rap, stem, wait. The fifth gives her a sound like a chair dragged in another room, then quits.

VERA
That's the boiler pipe.

She keeps going, talking to the fruit as she passes it.

VERA
There's a man coming Thursday. He wants the field behind the house. June's bringing the papers. She thinks I don't know she's bringing the papers.

Rap. Stem. Nothing.

VERA
I said I'd think about it. You know what I mean when I say that.

At the seventh pear her hand stops before the note has died.

VERA
Anders.

The pear turns a little on its wire.

VERA
Second row. The one you said got the afternoon shade.

Rap. Handle to the stem.

ANDERS (V.O.)
— quarter turn, no more than that. There. Now it's —

Silence. Rain comes back.

Vera holds the fork against the stem a beat longer than she needs to. Lowers it.

VERA
Say it again.

Nothing.

VERA
Say it again, Anders.

Rap. Stem. The pear gives back the same four seconds, note for note. Quarter turn, no more than that.

She plays it a third time and lifts the fork away before the sentence ends. The voice stops mid-word — cut clean, sealed back in.

VERA
Three years. Three years and you're in the glass.

She sets the fork down as though it might go off, and wipes her mouth with the back of her wrist. Walks the aisle with her hands behind her back, reading the fruit the way she once read a customer's piano: which keys had been played, which hadn't. Her boot heels knock the gravel. Near one pear her palm goes warm. Near another, cold. Two of them are silent in a way the others aren't.

At the bench: a green one. Small, hard, milky, nowhere near ripe, the stem still sappy where she works it free with her thumbnail. She holds it in both hands, close, the way you'd hold a microphone you didn't trust.

VERA
My name is Vera Lindqvist. The fourteenth of March, eleven at night, give or take. I'm in the greenhouse.

The glass stays cloudy. What she's putting in is going in and not coming out.

VERA
If anyone finds this — the second row from the door is the loud one. Start there. And don't let June touch it.

She takes a spool of garden wire off the bench and twists a length around the green stem. Three turns, the end tucked under, the way you'd mark a graft.

She sets the pear back among the others. It hangs there looking exactly like the rest, and not at all.

Flashlight up. The beam runs the bench to the far end, near the door, where the fruit is oldest.

One pear there is amber-dark, nearly brown. On its stem, above the tie, a twist of wire. Thin. Black with age. Three turns, the end tucked under.

Turned the other way.

Vera leans in close enough to fog the glass. Her thumb finds the wire. Brittle. It gives.

She doesn't take up the fork. The amber pear comes off its stem into her hand. She weighs it a moment, then unzips her coat pocket, puts it inside, and zips it.

Back at the green one, she crouches until her eyes are level with it.

VERA
And there's wire on the north end that isn't mine.

One more quarter turn to be sure. The stem creaks.

Boots to the door. The forks go back in the felt roll, all but the fourth, which she keeps in her fist.

The latch lifts. Rain swells. Behind her the beam swings off the bench, and the green pear hangs in the dark with its twist of wire, and holds.

## 2. What June Won't Touch

EXT. GREENHOUSE - DAY

Gravel dark from last night's rain. Past the rhubarb, a hatchback sits nose-out, driver's door open, engine ticking as it cools. JUNE, thirty-one, headphones around her neck, lays a leather folio on the roof and checks her face in the wing mirror — her own face, briefly.

She crosses to the greenhouse and stops. One boot on the mat. One on the stone.

Inside the glass, VERA sits on a stool at the near bench, the fourth fork upright in her fist. She isn't striking it. She's letting it hang.

June lifts her chin at the fork.

JUNE
You going to do that all afternoon?

VERA
You going to stand out there all afternoon?

JUNE
Surveyor's at two. Drainage, the well, the whole list. You don't have to be in the house for that part.

VERA
Door's open.

JUNE
It's been open since I got here.

Vera sets the fork down on the bench. Not gently.

VERA
Come in, June.

JUNE
No.

The word is out before the sentence is. Her hand finds the doorframe and stays there, fingers spread on the paint.

JUNE (CONT'D)
I've read the whole file. Twice. The boiler's a write-off, the roof's gone in at the north end, and Halloran isn't asking you to fix any of it. He's asking you to walk away and let him eat the loss.

VERA
I know what he's asking.

JUNE
Sixty-two an acre over valuation. It's obscene. Sign it and you never have to open this door again.

VERA
I opened it this morning.

June breathes out through her nose. There's something of her father in it. Nothing of the woman on the stool.

JUNE
Dad stood in here at two in the morning with the lights off. I'd come down for water and there'd be a man in a glass box talking to nothing.

VERA
Not nothing.

JUNE
I used to stand on this mat.

She looks at the mat. At her boot, and the line of wet stone it isn't crossing.

JUNE (CONT'D)
Eight years old. An hour and a half, once. He never turned around.

Vera says nothing. June's grip shifts on the frame. Her shoulder comes through the doorway, and the folio comes with it, extended at arm's length, held out like something on a tray.

JUNE (CONT'D)
Just take the papers. Read them at the kitchen table. That's all I'm —

The corner of the folio clips the nearest tray.

Glass tips. A pear rolls off the end of the bench. The fall is shorter than the sound of it — a hard bright crack on the concrete, then a slide, then a slow scatter of pieces coming to rest.

June goes still.

From the pieces, a hum. Thin, rising, a wet finger finding the rim of a glass.

JUNE (V.O.)
Dad. Dad. If I tell you a secret in here — does it keep it?

The shards tick against each other. A thread of light moves in the amber curve of one, the way heat moves under a lamp.

KARL (V.O.)
(far back in the same moment, half-turned, busy with something)
Depends which one you're standing next to.

Static. The hum falls away. The pieces lie there gleaming like spilled sugar.

June's hand is still out, holding nothing. Her headphone cable swings against her chest and settles.

VERA
Don't move.

June doesn't. Her eyes are on the concrete.

VERA (CONT'D)
Your weight's on the mat. You'll grind it in.

June steps back. Both boots on the gravel now. Then she crouches, and reaches in — an arm, a wrist, a hand entering the greenhouse for the first time since her father died. She gathers the shards the way you'd lift a broken record. Two of them are still warm in her palm.

She doesn't put them down.

VERA
There'll be more of them in the second —

JUNE
I know which row.

She stands. Folds the pieces into her coat pocket. Holds the pocket flat against her hip from the outside.

JUNE (CONT'D)
The well's got a cracked head. That's in the survey, not the file. Don't let him tell you it's a new thing.

VERA
June.

JUNE
Two o'clock. He'll bring a pen, not a contract. He signs first, so it looks like a favour.

She picks up the folio. The corner is bent where it struck the tray. She squares it against her forearm, and her eyes go past Vera, down the length of the bench, to the oldest fruit at the far end — brown, quiet, no light in it at all.

JUNE (CONT'D)
How many of them have me in them?

Vera's hand goes back to the fork. She doesn't lift it off the bench.

JUNE (CONT'D)
Thursday, then. Have an answer Thursday.

June walks to the hatchback without hurrying. Every step a step she'd have taken anyway.

She slides into the seat, sets the folio down on the passenger side, leaves the door standing open. The key goes into the ignition. She doesn't turn it.

Her palm presses flat to her coat pocket. Inside, the pieces have gone warm as a held hand.

She sits in the ticking quiet and waits for them to do it again.

## 3. The Man Who Knows the Rows

INT. GREENHOUSE - DAY

Halloran is three rows in by the time Vera finds him. Gravel from the drive has come in on his boots; it grits on the tiles each time he shifts his weight. Coat folded over one arm. No folder. No pen.

VERA
Two o'clock was the arrangement.

HALLORAN
I'm early. One of my two bad habits.

A thumbnail flicks out and taps the shoulder of a pear at eye height. The note comes back small and true, still ringing a half-second after the tap has finished.

The next one gives a click and nothing else. He walks past it without slowing.

HALLORAN (CONT'D)
Not that one.

VERA
No.

Across the aisle he skips two and taps a third with his knuckle. It goes off like a wet finger on a rim.

VERA (CONT'D)
Left, right, skip. You did it at the door, too.

HALLORAN
You were counting.

VERA
I tune things. Counting is most of it.

He works down the row at a steward's pace, keeping to the outside where the light comes flat and sideways. Ripe fruit gets a knuckle. The silent ones get nothing at all — not a glance, not a pause to be polite about it.

VERA (CONT'D)
June says you'll bring a pen.

HALLORAN
June's usually right about pens.

VERA
She says you sign first.

HALLORAN
So it looks like a favour.

VERA
It isn't one.

HALLORAN
No.

He stops at a pear that still wears a twist of wire at the stem. Green, hard, weeks off anything.

HALLORAN (CONT'D)
Yours.

VERA
Mine.

HALLORAN
It'll keep.

He moves on. Vera stays where she is a moment longer than the remark deserves.

VERA
The survey puts the boundary twelve metres past the fence.

HALLORAN
You've read the survey.

VERA
I've read it.

HALLORAN
Twice, I'd guess. There's a well head in the second photograph. Cracked at the collar.

VERA
That's been cracked since before I met him.

HALLORAN
He photographed it from the good side.

VERA
He photographed everything from the good side.

She sets her bucket down. Water slops once against the rim and steadies.

VERA (CONT'D)
Which row gets the morning?

HALLORAN
(without looking up)
Third from the wall. The rest of them take the afternoon and blame the glass for it.

His hand comes off the bough. Something in the answer has caught up with him.

VERA
You've been in here before.

HALLORAN
I've been in a lot of glasshouses.

VERA
Not like this one.

Down at knee height a pear hangs dark as a boot heel. Halloran cups it, tilts it clear of the stem, and puts his ear against it the way you'd put an ear to a wall in another man's house.

THE FRUIT
(a young man's voice, quick, close)
— Sten. Sten, if you go back in there tonight, I'm not coming after you.

The ring of it dies off into the glass and keeps dying.

Halloran lowers the pear, stands it upright on the bench, and squares it with two fingers so it keeps company with the others. His mouth stays shut.

VERA
That's your voice.

Halloran turns the coat over. Gravel patters onto the tiles.

VERA (CONT'D)
You knew him when you were that young.

He walks on down the row. The silent ones pass him by.

VERA (CONT'D)
He never mentioned you.

Halloran keeps walking.

## 4. The North Row

INT. GREENHOUSE - NIGHT

A storm lantern sits on the floor at the north end, wick turned low. Its light reaches three benches and stops. Condensation beads on the glass overhead. A drip keeps time somewhere in the dark.

Vera moves between the rows in a man’s coat, sleeves past her knuckles. A tuning fork in one hand. A coil of white wire hangs from her wrist. A slate board swings from her neck on a string, three chalk marks already crossed out.

She strikes the fork against the iron pipe. The note spreads through the glass. Pears hang close as fists. None answers. She waits. At the far end, a pear trembles once on its stem, then stills.

Vera crosses to it. The fork goes to the skin. The fruit takes the note and returns it lower, a hum that runs up into the stem. She twists a length of wire around the stem — not the fruit — and moves on.

Second bench. Fork struck. A pear answers with a man’s laugh, cut off at the first breath. She chalks the slate. The next stays quiet. The one after gives back only the note itself, thin and useless. She passes both.

The fork’s ring fades into the glass. Vera holds it to the next pear, then the next, without striking again. Nothing. She hits the pipe harder. A row of pears shivers. One near the floor answers with a low male voice counting — “four, five, six” — then stops. She marks it and moves on.

Third bench. A pear has split at the crown; the flesh inside shows dark. She leaves it. The next responds with a woman humming two bars, then nothing. Vera marks it, crosses the mark out, and moves on.

At the ninth pear, her wrist slows. It hangs lower than the rest, almost black, a hairline crack running through the skin. She strikes the fork and sets the stem of the fork against the crack.

The lantern hisses. The glass ticks.

STEN’S VOICE
(recorded, low)
After the funeral. Not before.

HALLORAN’S VOICE
(recorded)
North row first.

STEN’S VOICE
North row first. Then the rest.

HALLORAN’S VOICE
You’re asking me to burn a thing you built.

STEN’S VOICE
After I’m dead. That’s the difference.

HALLORAN’S VOICE
Not to her.

STEN’S VOICE
No. Not to her.

The pear holds the silence after. Vera’s breath fogs the skin. She turns the fork a fraction; the recording drags, then steadies.

HALLORAN’S VOICE
She’ll know it was me.

STEN’S VOICE
She’ll know it was me. That’s the point.

HALLORAN’S VOICE
And when she asks why?

STEN’S VOICE
She won’t ask. She’ll tune it. She’ll hear what it wants her to hear.

HALLORAN’S VOICE
That’s not an answer.

STEN’S VOICE
It’s the only one I’ve got.

The fork rings down. Vera shifts her grip. The pear gives a soft click and begins again farther in.

STEN’S VOICE
Paraffin’s under the potting bench. Two cans.

HALLORAN’S VOICE
You’ve thought it through.

STEN’S VOICE
I’ve had time.

Vera’s hand tightens around the fork. The pear ticks. Then, quieter:

STEN’S VOICE
Vera.

HALLORAN’S VOICE
Don’t put her name in it.

STEN’S VOICE
She must never hear the rest.

The fruit stops. No fade. Just cut. Vera stands with the fork against the crack until her arm aches.

VERA
Sten.

No answer. The pear hangs still.

She sets the fork on the bench. From her pocket she takes a red twist of wire — the one she uses for the ripe ones — and ties it around the stem. The pear swings once and settles.

Both hands lift it, careful of the crack. She lowers it into the basket at her feet. The tuning fork goes in beside it. She picks up the lantern. The light swings across the row, waking a dozen small reflections in the glass.

At the end of the north row, past the last bench, one more pear hangs against the window. It is green, unripe, the same hard green as the one she marked the first night. Vera stops.

She takes the fork out of the basket and strikes it on the iron pipe.

Before the note reaches the fruit, the pear begins to hum.

## 5. The Signing

INT. KITCHEN — MORNING

Frost on the window, thick at the corners. Two cups steam. Between them the sale papers sit squared. A fountain pen lies across the top page, cap off, the nib already wet.

HALLORAN stands behind his chair. Coat buttoned. Hat in both hands.

VERA carries the north-row pear to the table in both hands and sets it in the empty bowl. Red wire twisted at its stem. The crack in the glass runs clean from crown to base.

HALLORAN
That's off the north end.

VERA
Sit down.

He sits.

VERA
Drink your coffee.

HALLORAN
Vera—

She takes a tuning fork from her apron pocket and strikes it against the table edge. Both cups shiver. Then the pear takes up the note.

STEN'S VOICE
—after. Not before.

HALLORAN'S VOICE
How long after.

STEN'S VOICE
A season. Long enough that nobody comes asking. Then the paraffin, both cans, and you light it from the south wall so she can't—

A flat palm comes down over the fruit. The note dies. The kitchen is very quiet.

Halloran sets his hat on the table. His hands stay on it.

VERA
You want to tell me it's not what it sounds like.

HALLORAN
It's exactly what it sounds like.

VERA
Then say it plainly.

HALLORAN
Which row?

VERA
North.

HALLORAN
Which bench?

VERA
Third from the window. Where the pipe runs warm.

He nods, slow.

VERA
You knew the warm ones before I did.

HALLORAN
I knew Sten before you did.

She lifts her cup. Puts it down without drinking.

VERA
You were going to burn it.

HALLORAN
I was going to buy it and burn it. That was the arrangement. He didn't want it standing empty and he didn't want it standing with you in it.

VERA
And the money.

HALLORAN
Mine to lose. He said a man doesn't promise a hard thing for free.

Vera reaches for the papers, draws them across the table, and lays them face-down on the chair beside her.

HALLORAN
You can sign it and still hate me.

VERA
I don't hate you.

HALLORAN
Then sign.

VERA
No.

He looks at the chair. At the papers. At her.

HALLORAN
It's the last of the offer. There isn't a second one.

VERA
I know.

She goes to the sill, lifts a jam jar, and sets it in the middle of the table: dark soil, water, a green stem, root hairs drifting pale through the glass. Two fingers push it toward him.

HALLORAN
What's this.

VERA
What does it look like.

He lifts the jar to the light and turns it. Soil slumps against the glass and settles.

HALLORAN
It's green.

VERA
It's ripe when it says something worth keeping.

HALLORAN
That's not how it works.

VERA
You learned the first half from him. You'll learn the rest from me.

He sets the jar down. Lifts it again. A crease shows between his brows that she has not seen there before.

HALLORAN
I've nowhere to put it.

VERA
You've a kitchen window.

HALLORAN
Three years before it fruits.

VERA
I've tuned pianos for thirty. I can wait on a tree.

He slides the jar into his coat pocket. Glass knocks once against something metal in there.

At the door, hand on the latch, hat under his arm, he does not turn around.

HALLORAN
The pear past the last bench. The one that never ripened.

VERA
How do you know about that one.

HALLORAN
It was there before yours were.

Vera's fingers close around the fork.

VERA
It hums. Before I strike anything. It hums on its own.

HALLORAN
I know.

VERA
Halloran. What's in it.

HALLORAN
He made one promise straight and one promise crooked. The crooked one's in that pear.

The latch turns. Cold comes off the doorframe and moves along the floor.

VERA
And the straight one?

HALLORAN
That you'd never have to hear it.

He steps out. The door clicks shut. Through the glass his shape crosses the yard. The gate hinges complain. Then there is only the frost and the cups going cold.

Vera stands a moment with the fork in her hand. The papers stay face-down on the chair. She takes her coat from the hook, pockets the fork, and pulls the back door open.

From the greenhouse, faint through two panes of glass, a single note holds. Steady. Patient. Struck by nothing.
