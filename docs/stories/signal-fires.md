# Signal Fires

_Web Series · linear · genres: sci-fi thriller · 4952 words_

## 1. Dead Air

COLD OPEN

The kettle clicks off at 02:40. Nobody pours it.

Mara Quinn has her boots on the wastebasket, log pad open, pen capped. Through the window the harbour light swings its arm over the water. Buoy bell past the Ness. The set hisses at itself, the way it does on a clear night.

Then it stops hissing.

“—station, station, station, this is Sanderling, Sanderling, Sanderling—”

Her boots hit the floor. The handset is up before the chair stops spinning.

“Sanderling, Harbour Station. Go ahead, over.”

Carrier. Open-mic breathing. She calls twice more. Switches to the spare antenna. Calls again. Nothing.

Playback, then.

The deck’s stamp reads 14th, 04:12. Her watch says the 13th, 02:47.

“—three aboard, three aboard, we’re taking water, bearing zero-seven-four, two miles off the Ness—”

The door bangs. Theo shoulders in sideways with the tide tables, rain in his hair. He stops cold, hand still on the handle.

“That’s you,” he says.

ACT ONE

Four playbacks. Mara sits with her chin on her knuckles and lets the voice come at her sideways, the way you have to. Flat vowels. The hard K she puts on the end of a word when she’s scared and trying not to sound it. The way it says Sanderling — three times, because that’s the drill. Because somebody taught her that, and she taught it to every volunteer in this room.

“Salt-and-pepper hiss under the voice,” Theo says. “Deck’s out. That’s the tell.”

“I know what the tell is.”

“Then it’s not the deck.”

She stands, pulls the cassette, holds it against the window light. Magnetic tape in a plastic shell. One of forty in the drawer. The deck has been running double shift since the digital logger went out in April and the council stopped answering emails about it.

Theo reads from the pad. “Sanderling. Zero-seven-four. 04:12. Three souls. Recorder clock’s synced to the phone. Phone’s synced to the harbour master.”

“Read me the bearing again.”

“Zero-seven-four. That’s the Skerry bank. Two off.”

She knows what’s two miles off the Ness on zero-seven-four. Sand. A reef under it. Eleven feet of water at low tide. She has pulled two men out of that water.

“There’s no Sanderling out of this harbour,” she says. “I know every hull on this quay down to the paint.”

Theo taps the pad. “So where’s it broadcasting from?”

ACT TWO

Ostrander comes in at 03:20 with diesel on his jacket and rain running off his cap. Mara lets the tape run because he needs to hear it. He listens with his hands behind his back, all the way to the end. Then he laughs once.

“Inversion,” he says. “Some trawler out of Arklow. Bounce off the warm layer, lands in your lap with a bad stamp on it. Happens in the spring.”

“Stamp’s off the deck, not the ionosphere.”

“Then your deck’s sick. Get it fixed.” He picks up her mug, sniffs it, puts it down. “Mara. Six boats go out Saturday with paying guests on them. The festival’s in nine days. I have one story in this town, and I’d like it to be about the mackerel.”

“You want me to check the registry?”

“I want you to check the registry.”

They check it. The harbour’s licensing database, three years of it. Then a phone call to the county office, where a woman named Deirdre reads out every vessel over eight metres with a mooring in the district. No Sanderling. Not this year. Not last.

Theo writes NOT REGISTERED in the margin and underlines it twice.

Ostrander puts his cap back on. “Dead air and a tape warp. That’s the story. Am I right?”

“You’re half right,” Mara says.

After the door shuts, she stands a while with her hand flat on the deck, feeling the hum of the set. Then she opens the drawer and takes out the bulk eraser.

ACT THREE

Muster at six. Four volunteers, cold hands around mugs, rain on the roof like gravel.

“Overnight log,” Mara says. “Two-forty. We took a weak transmission. Garbled. No station ID. No response to challenge.

## 2. Counterflow

COLD OPEN

Channel 16 crackles open at 06:04 with a voice too calm for what it is saying.

“Pan-pan, pan-pan, pan-pan. Bridie May, potting off the Carrick rocks. Two men in the water, one conscious. Life ring has a name on it. Sanderling. Repeat, Sanderling.”

Mara’s chair rolls back into the radiator and rocks there.

“Say the name again,” she says.

“Sanderling. We’re hauling them now. We need the lifeboat alongside.”

Theo has both palms flat on the chart table. Neither of them reaches for the mic.

ACT ONE

Seven-twenty, the station smells of wet wool and burnt toast. Theo sets the handheld recorder on the desk like it might go off.

“I run it every shift,” he says. “My own. Not the deck.”

“You told me the deck eats tapes.”

“It does. So I stopped trusting it.” A carpenter’s pencil sits behind his ear; he takes it down, turns it over twice. “It’s timestamped. It’s not been near anything.”

He plays it.

Static, then a voice, flat and fast, riding over the top of the carrier: *“Any station — any station. This is Sanderling, calling from bearing one-one-zero, two point four miles off the harbour mouth. We are taking water. Two aboard. Position —”*

The seal breaks. The voice goes under. Then Mara’s own voice climbs out of the noise, and she has heard it before, and it does not get easier.

*“Sanderling, Sanderling, this is Cloghnagh Harbour Radio. Steer one-one-zero. Hold on. I have you.”*

The recording ends at 02:42 and eleven seconds.

Mara lays the parallel rules on the chart. Runs the line from the harbour mouth: one-one-zero, two point four nautical miles, the western edge of the Carricks. She says nothing for a while.

“That’s the position the Bridie May called from,” Theo says. “I looked.”

“I know what it is.”

“And the registry has no Sanderling on it. Ostrander had Deirdre read the whole district. I wrote it down.” He turns the log toward her, the pencil line under NOT REGISTERED still angry in the margin. “So how does a boat that doesn’t exist sink at two-forty in the morning?”

Mara opens the laptop, pulls the county register, and searches the district by hull number instead of name. It takes nine minutes. A twelve-metre trawler, green hull, sold last October, renamed on the papers, new owner, licence lapsed in January. The new name is Naomh Pádraig. Nobody repainted the life rings.

“There she is,” Mara says. “She exists. She just stopped being legal in the spring.”

Theo’s pencil stops moving.

ACT TWO

Ostrander comes in without knocking, coat dripping, cap under his arm.

“I’ve had the lifeboat on the phone since half six,” he says. “Two men in the water, one of them singing. You want to tell me what the station knew and when?”

“We knew at two-forty,” Mara says. “Same as you’d have known if you’d been listening.”

“Don’t.”

“Twelve metres. Green hull. Lapsed licence.” She watches him. “You knew the boat before the Bridie May said a word about it, Chief. How’s that?”

His jaw works once. “I know every hull in this harbour.”

“You know every *registered* hull. You signed the sheet that says there’s no Sanderling.”

He sets the cap down, precisely, brim to the door. “Here’s what I know. Six boats go out Saturday with paying guests. The festival opens in eight days. Insurance man’s coming Thursday, and he’s bringing a clipboard and a nose.” He leans on the desk. “A volunteer station that logs a sinking before it happens is a story I cannot unfuck. You follow me?”

“I follow you.”

“Give me the tapes. All of them. Quiet, and it’s a sad accident off the Carricks and we all get on with our summer.”

“Station tape’s blank,” Mara says. “You were right. Sunspot noise.”

Ostrander looks at the handheld sitting between them. Nobody has moved it.

“That’s not station property,” Theo says.

“That’s a recording of a distress call received in this district. That’s mine.” Ostrander picks up his cap. “I’ll be back at six with paper. Have it on the desk.”

ACT THREE

The phone rings at four. Lifeboat coxswain, three minutes, no small talk.

“The conscious one’s talking,” he says. “He keeps saying a woman answered them. Gave them a course. Told them to hold on.”

“Answered them on what?”

“Radio. Half two, he says. Before anyone knew they were gone.”

Mara sets the phone down. Then she logs into the coastguard archive with credentials she has not used since she traded the uniform for a lanyard, and pulls the automatic transmission log for Cloghnagh Harbour Radio’s call sign.

There it is. 02:41:19. Channel 16. Thirty-eight seconds. Confirmed transmitted, not received. Her call sign, her station, her voice.

She had not touched the key.

At six, Ostrander comes back with a form and a cardboard box, and Theo stands in the doorway with his arms folded and his pencil behind his ear.

Mara signs. The deck goes in the box — the machine, the logbook, the spool drawer. She hands it all over because the station tape was erased at three in the morning and there is nothing in that box worth having.

Ostrander puts the box under his arm. He does not leave.

“There’s a thing I’m obliged to raise,” he says. “Coastguard logger’s got a transmission off this desk at two-forty-one. Thirty-eight seconds on sixteen. That’s a distress acknowledgement, and it isn’t in your logbook, and it isn’t on your tape.”

Theo’s arms come unfolded.

“So I’ll ask you both the once,” Ostrander says. “Who was on the air at two-forty-one?”

## 3. Dead Reckoning

COLD OPEN

Water sheets off the Naomh Bríd's stern and Deckie Rusk watches it go, hose in one fist, the VHF muttering to itself in the wheelhouse behind him. Charter season opens Saturday. Six rods, four Germans, one cooler of mackerel he has not caught yet.

At 05:52 the muttering stops.

"—bearing two-one-five, Cloghnagh Sound. Time, sixteen minutes past six. Repeat, sixteen minutes past six."

A woman's voice. Level as a shipping forecast. That flatness is what lifts the hair on his arms — somebody out there in trouble does not sound like that.

Deckie ducks under the wheelhouse frame, wipes his palms on his trousers, keys the handset. "Station calling Naomh Bríd, say your position, over."

Carrier hiss. Then the voice again, from the top, word for word. Tape, or something worse than tape.

"...sixteen minutes past six."

He writes it on the whiteboard under the charter bookings. 06:16. 215. Then, because the name came last and quiet, the way a person says a thing they'd rather not be overheard: FUREY.

By 06:16 he is standing on the pier with a coffee going cold, and there is nothing out there but gulls, one orange pot buoy, and a flat grey seam where the sea meets the rain.

ACT ONE

Ostrander brings two boxes. The second is for the logbooks, back to March.

"Receive-only until the licensing board sits," he says, laying the receipt on the desk like a bill. "No transmit. No logging. I'll take the microphone now."

Theo fills the doorway with his arms crossed and a pencil behind his ear and does not move out of the way.

Mara signs. "The desk was empty at two-forty-one."

"Logger says different." Ostrander coils the mic cable, unhurried. "Logger says thirty-eight seconds on sixteen, off this station, your call sign. That's not weather. That's a person."

"Then the logger's wrong."

"Loggers aren't wrong. That's the entire point of them." He sets the box against his hip. "Charter association's asked me to keep this off the pier till Saturday. Three boats booked, forty jobs riding on the back of them. One story about voices on the radio and we're a harbour that can't be trusted with a bank holiday weekend." His eyes cross the room to Theo and stay there. "If it reaches the pier, I'll know where from."

The door bangs shut. The transmitter room is locked now, and the lock is his.

Theo takes the pencil from behind his ear, looks at the point, and puts it back without a word.

ACT TWO

Diesel and wet rope, that's the boathouse. Mara hauls the old ICOM off the shelf, blows the dust out of the speaker grille, clips it to a tractor battery, and runs coax up to a dipole strung between the roof beams with two cable ties and no dignity at all.

"Tin roof, salt air, twenty metres of cheap coax," Theo says. "You'll hear the ferry and a fridge."

"We'll hear what we hear."

He sets his notebook on an upturned fish crate, opens to a clean page, writes the date across the top, presses hard enough to score the paper.

"You write nothing down," Mara says.

"Somebody has to."

"Somebody did, and it's in Ostrander's boot."

"Then I'll write it where he hasn't got a key." Click of the pen. "You want a witness or a witness statement. Pick one."

They wait. Seven o'clock. Nine. Half ten brings the ferry, the weather, two trawlers arguing about a pot line. At 21:04 the receiver gives them a woman's voice and Mara's hand stops halfway to her mug.

"—bearing one-nine-zero. Time, four a.m."

Then, softer, half off the mic: "Alma Rusk."

Theo's pen is already moving. Call sign, time, bearing, word for word. Then he flips back two pages, to the volunteer duty roster he copied out by hand because Ostrander took the original.

His finger stops on a line.

ACT THREE

Three slips on the corkboard now, plus Deckie's whiteboard photographed on Theo's phone.

02:41. Bearing 215. No name.
06:16. Bearing 215. Furey.
04:00. Bearing 190. Rusk.

"Don't look at the bearings," Mara says. "Look at the gap."

Theo does the arithmetic out loud. "First one lands twenty-four hours after it's spoken. Second lands nine. Third lands three." He sits back on the crate. "It isn't coming earlier. It's getting late."

"Something's running out."

"Or someone's getting closer." He taps the page. "Furey's on this roster. Rusk is on this roster. That's not a coincidence, that's a list."

She takes it off him. Duty roster, Cloghnagh Harbour Radio, volunteers, in the order the sheet rotates: Furey. Rusk. Marsh. Nolan. Quinn.

Her thumb is flat on the paper before she has decided to move it.

Theo reads it upside down. "You're on this."

"So are you." She folds the sheet once, and keeps folding. "Nobody answers that rig tomorrow but me. Whatever comes over that speaker—"

The receiver cuts her off.

Not ferry. Not weather. A woman's voice, calm as a shipping forecast, and the voice belongs to Mara, and it gives a bearing that puts the caller inside the harbour wall.

"Time," it says, "two-forty-one."

Theo's head comes round. "That's the first one. That's where we came in."

The voice says a name. Carrier swallows the middle of it; what comes through is a shape, a length, a first letter, and then hiss.

"Say it again," Theo says. "Say it again."

Hiss.

He turns the notebook round, runs his finger down the copied roster. "Who's after Rusk on this?"

## 4. The Quiet Frequency

COLD OPEN

The boathouse heater ticks. Diesel, wet rope, the receiver already warm at 04:47 because Theo has been here since three with a pencil pressed so hard the paper has gone shiny under the last line.

Static. A click. Then the voice, calm as a tide table.

"Time, zero-five-twelve. Bearing zero-nine-zero."

He writes it down. Zero-five-twelve. Zero-nine-zero.

"Name," the voice says. "Theo."

The pencil stops.

Four seconds back, run again, and the same shape comes out of the speaker — a name with the middle chewed off by the carrier, but the front of it is there and the back of it is there and both of them are his.

The door bangs. Mara comes in out of the rain with her coat open and the torch still in her hand, unlit.

"You're early," she says.

"The call was early." He turns the pad round. "Read it."

She reads it twice. Her jaw works once, side to side.

"Play it."

ACT ONE

They play it eleven times. Mara stands with both palms flat on the chart, hair dripping onto the Donegal sheet, and does not look at him once.

"From here," she says, "zero-nine-zero runs out past the bar and up the north channel."

"Where the charters go."

"Where the charters go at slack water. Which is—"

"Zero-five-twelve. I did the arithmetic." He sits on the upturned crate. "Furey came twenty-four hours before. Rusk came nine. Mine came six."

"Six."

"It's closing."

She takes the dividers and walks them across the chart without measuring anything.

"Three calls," Theo says. "Furey. Rusk. Me. But the sheet goes Furey, Rusk, Marsh, Nolan, Quinn." He taps the roster he copied in pencil. "Marsh and Nolan got skipped. It's not reading the list anymore. It's choosing."

Hail comes over the roof in one hard pass and turns back to rain.

"You're off the water," she says.

"I'm at the desk tomorrow."

"That's what I said."

Theo looks at the receiver, at the spool turning slowly for no reason, and puts the pencil behind his ear.

ACT TWO

Boots on the slip, then Ostrander in the doorway, rain running off the peak of his cap, and he does not knock because he has never once knocked.

"Your car's at the harbour wall, Mara. Lights off. Two in the morning." He looks at the receiver, at the tape. "I'd like that off."

"You've had a busy night."

"I've had the regional office on since Monday. Log discrepancy, Cloghnagh, an irregular transmission on the sixteenth." He sets a folder on the chart table, squared to the edge of it. "They'll send an inspector. He'll find a volunteer station with one tape recorder and no answers, and he'll close you. I can carry that folder into the tide and lose it. I'm offering."

"What do you want for it."

"That." A nod at the tape. "And a safety exercise. All craft alongside tomorrow, four until six, no exceptions. I'll put it out myself as an exercise. Your boy stays dry, nobody's frightened, the charters don't lose the week."

Theo's hand has gone still on the receiver case. "It's not an exercise."

"It'll do the same job as a warning."

"A warning gets people looking. An exercise gets people tied up." Theo leans forward. "Chief — if it's at the bar at five, and every boat in this harbour is tied up, then there's nobody out there for whoever it is."

Ostrander's eyes come round slowly to Mara. "And if you go on Channel 16 tonight and tell four hundred people a radio from tomorrow says so, what happens? Nothing, because they'll say you cracked. Or everything, because they'll believe you, and then they'll believe the next one, and the one after. Which is it?"

The rain fills the gap. Mara wipes her palms down her thighs.

"Both," she says. "So I don't tell them where it came from."

ACT THREE

She writes it out on the back of a passage plan, ballpoint, in the harbour's own flat language: unmarked obstruction, four cables east of the harbour mouth, on the zero-nine-zero line. All craft advised to keep clear between 04:30 and 06:00. Nothing about voices. Nothing about tomorrow.

Theo reads over her shoulder.

"That's a lie."

"That's a notice to mariners."

"It's a lie and it only works if there's nothing left saying different." He points at the tape. "He'll take that. Not tonight — he'll take it in the morning, and then it's an inquiry exhibit and every word I've logged since Tuesday goes with it."

Mara presses the stop. The spools settle.

"Then he won't get it," she says, and pushes the erase arm across.

The tape hisses. Six days of a calm woman's voice go out of it in eleven seconds, name by name, bearing by bearing, and Theo watches it happen with his shoulders up around his ears and does not say a word until it is done.

"Right," he says. Quietly. "Do your broadcast."

She keys the handset on 16 at 05:03, and her voice comes out level, coastguard-flat, the way it did for nine years.

"All stations, all stations, all stations. Sécurité. Cloghnagh Harbour. Unmarked obstruction, four cables east of the harbour mouth, bearing zero-nine-zero. Avoid between zero-four-thirty and zero-six. Out."

Three times. Then the acknowledgements start, small boats out of the marina, a trawler off the point, a yacht at anchor who thanks her for the sharpness of it.

Then Ostrander's own voice, clipped and official, two minutes later: "Harbour patrol acknowledging, proceeding to the position to investigate."

Theo looks up from the receiver. "He's going out there."

"He's doing his job."

"He's going out to the bearing." His hand is at the pad again, the pencil found from behind his ear without looking. "That's not what we wanted. We wanted nobody out there."

The receiver clicks.

The spool begins to turn, the way it has no business doing with the tape she just wiped and the erase head still humming.

"Time," says the voice, and it is the same voice, and it takes a second for what it says to become a number. "Zero-five-twelve. Bearing zero-nine-zero."

Theo's pencil hovers. Nine minutes. Not tomorrow. Nine minutes, and the harbour patrol is already underway, and there is nothing on any tape in this county that says the radio ever spoke.

"Name," says the voice, plain this time, no carrier in it at all.

"Ostrander."

The heater ticks. Theo writes it down.

"Say it again," Mara says, to the speaker, to the erased tape, to the rain. "Say it—"

Hiss.

## 5. Signal Fires

COLD OPEN

Channel 16 has forty people on it at once.

"—Cloghnagh, Cloghnagh, this is *Seánie B*, we're—"

"—say again, there's a boat on the bar, there's a—"

"—my husband took the *Máire Óg* out at half three, has anyone—"

Mara keeps the volume at three, squelch open, one ear free. The pen has not left the paper in twenty minutes. Names. Positions. Headings.

Ostrander cuts through it like a bow wave. "Silence on the channel. *Silence.* This is Harbour Patrol. One at a time. Vessel and position."

He gets his silence. It lasts four seconds. Then a lifeboat coxswain reports three hulls aground inside the bar, all crew accounted for, nobody in the water; a fourth boat standing off with a fouled prop; and a yacht with a dead depth sounder motoring for home on a position she gave at 04:44.

"Three aground," Theo says. Second desk, headset clamped on, log open at a fresh page. "Because the whole fleet came in at once. Because we told them to."

"We told them there was something in the water."

"We told them there was something in the water and not what it was." He turns the page. "Twenty-two names. Times we have. Bearings we have. Reasons, none."

Between them on the desk: the pen, the pad, and Theo's small tidy hand, half the names struck through, half still waiting on a clock that hasn't stopped since the kettle clicked off nine days ago.

ACT ONE

Water in the gutter, running. Nora Ffrench comes up from the shop with a flask and four cups and no questions at all, and in twenty years Nora Ffrench has never brought a cup without a question. The cups sit.

"Deckie's got the *Naomh Bríd* against the north quay," she says. "He's fine. He's furious. Says something told him at half four to come in."

"Did it?"

"It didn't tell him that."

Mara takes a cup. "No."

At 04:51 the coastguard asks Cloghnagh Coast Radio, formally, for the source of the warning broadcast. Mara holds the key down and says, "Standing by," and then stands by nothing at all for a long moment, and Theo watches her not do it.

Ostrander comes in at 04:53 hard enough that the door hits the wall, smelling of diesel and cold, holding Thursday's impound order creased along the same fold.

"You've got the whole harbour out at low water," he says. "Three boats on the bar because your voice on an open channel scared the wits out of them."

"Three boats on the bar because the bar is unlit and the fleet all came home at once. Nobody's drowned. That's the part you say next."

"That's the part I'll say at the inquiry." He takes the pad. She takes it back. Theo's headset stays exactly as it is and his pencil keeps moving and neither of them looks at him. "I gave you a lawful direction. The recordings go to the harbour office, sealed, this morning."

"Then take them." Mara sets her cup down. "Take the tapes, take the log, take the whole desk. There's twenty-two names on that list and every one belongs to a boat or a body in this harbour, and you want me to build a nice quiet room where nobody has to hear it."

"*Mara.*" He steps in close and lowers his voice, which is worse. "The charter season opens in nine days. Nine. This town gets a name for being on the end of a haunted radio, the boats don't come, the pier doesn't eat, and none of your tape does a damn thing about that."

The speaker hisses.

The spool begins to turn.

ACT TWO

04:59. Theo's pencil stops.

"Time," says the voice. "Zero-four-fifty-nine."

Mara stands very still. It is her voice. It has always been her voice. It has stopped sounding like an accident.

Ostrander says, "What."

"Bearing three-four-five. Range: nil."

The range finder on the wall is brass, old, the gift of a widow. Mara walks to it the way you walk in a dream and lines it and does not need to. Three-four-five. West. Not four cables. Not the harbour mouth.

The transmitter hut.

"Name."

Mara holds her own breath.

"Cloghnagh Harbour Coast Radio," says the voice, and the carrier drops, and everything in the room is suddenly very loud — rain on the tin, the kettle cooling, Ostrander's belt radio ticking.

Theo is already reading off the sheet. "A call names a person, we warn the person. A call names a boat, we warn the boat. This one names us." His voice has gone thin and steady at once. "What do we do with a call that names us?"

"You do nothing," Ostrander says. "Not one thing. That tape goes in a bag."

"Ninety seconds," Theo says. "Time mark to spool dry. It's on the pad."

Mara takes the microphone off the hook. Not the link. The desk mic. The big one. The red key.

"Take the key off her," Ostrander says, to Theo. "That is not a request."

Theo looks at the key. He looks at the pad, at twenty-two names, at his own name struck through at the top with a time beside it that came and went at 04:40 and left him standing exactly where he is.

"No," he says.

ACT THREE

She keys it at seventeen seconds.

The carrier click goes out through the whole harbour — a trawler in the roads, a lifeboat off the bar, forty sets, a patrol RIB coming up the channel at full.

"This is Cloghnagh Harbour Coast Radio," Mara says. "Cloghnagh Coast Radio, answering. Calling Cloghnagh Coast Radio."

Hiss. The clock eats nineteen seconds while she works out what the call wants. Every call named someone. Every call waited to be answered. The station has no boat and no crew and no chart. A station's name is the person at the desk.

"Dara Quinn," she says. Her mother's name. The one on her papers. The one on her first ticket at nineteen. The one nobody in this town has used in fifteen years. "Dara Quinn, duty operator, Cloghnagh Harbour Coast Radio, standing watch."

The spool holds.

Then it runs out. The reel goes round and round with nothing coming off it, the erase head's hum takes over, and the speaker goes properly dead — no guard hiss, no channel, nothing. The first true silence on this frequency in nine days.

Mara stands with the key still down. Nobody speaks. Rain.

Ostrander crosses the room and lifts the spool off the deck with both hands, the way you lift a bullet, and sets it in the middle of the table.

"Now it's mine," he says. "Whatever that was, this harbour's answer to it is not going to be a woman reading a list on sixteen."

He takes the bag off the shelf. Puts the spool in. Pulls the bag open again, sets it down, puts the tape back on the table.

"Play it," he says.

Theo presses it.

Her voice comes back out of the machine — the carrier in it, no hurry in it at all. "Time. Zero-six-hundred. Bearing three-four-five. Range: within the harbour. Name—"

The hiss cuts clean.

"Ostrander," says Theo.

Ostrander's hand is flat on the table. On his belt his radio has come alive — a bare carrier, no voice, held open by something that is not a thumb.

Mara takes the pen. She writes the time, and the bearing, and the name, and slides it across to him.

"Six o'clock," she says. "I'll want you at this desk, Chief. Whether you believe it or you don't."

He picks up his cap. "And if I'm not here?"

Theo re-threads the tape. "Then you'll be at the bearing."
