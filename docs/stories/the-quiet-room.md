# The Quiet Room

_Screenplay · linear · genres: quiet sci-fi + horror · 4498 words_

## 1. Residual Noise

INT. PETROV SOUND — RECEPTION — NIGHT

A key turns in a lock that sticks. NIA PETROV, 34, shoulders the door open, a file box stacked against her hip.

The smell arrives before the light does: dust, cold foam, cigarettes thirty years old.

She finds the switch. Fluorescents stutter on down a corridor of fixtures, half of them dead.

The room assembles itself: a rental counter. A wall of demo cassettes in a case, hand-labeled. A velvet chair gone bald at the arms. On the back wall, in a warped frame, a man in his sixties with a mustache, arm around a teenage girl holding a soldering iron like a trophy.

Nia sets the box down. Turns the photograph to the wall.

Out of the box: a legal pad. A single line already on it, in ballpoint — LIST PRICE — CALL TERRY MON.

She sets a pen beside it, squares the pad to the counter's edge, and walks the room at the pace of someone measuring for a coffin.

At the end of the corridor, a heavy door under a red bulb, dark. A brass plate: QUIET ROOM. Below it, a half-peeled sticker: RATE ON REQUEST.

INT. PETROV SOUND — CONTROL ROOM — NIGHT

Nia drops into a chair that exhales. The console is analog, enormous, faders like piano keys. She runs a finger down a channel strip; it comes away gray.

She hits the power. The board lights in stages. A fan wakes somewhere, then thinks better of it.

Through the glass: the booth. A small dead room, foam-wedged walls, one stool, one microphone on a stand.

Nia pulls the headphones over her ears. Leans to the talkback.

NIA
Testing, one. Testing.

Her voice comes back warm and flat, swallowed by the foam. No tail. No room.

The silence after it is enormous. She listens to it longer than she needs to. Pulls the phones off. Rubs her ear.

A knock on the glass behind her. She turns.

EMIL PETROV, 28, in a paramedic's jacket, two coffees in a cardboard tray. He grins through the glass like an apology.

INT. PETROV SOUND — RECEPTION — NIGHT

Emil sets the tray on the counter beside the legal pad. Reads it upside down.

EMIL
Terry Mon's a shark.

NIA
Terry Mon's a buyer.

EMIL
Forty years, Nia. Dad built this place with his hands.

NIA
And it's worth more as a building than as a business. That's not a decision. That's arithmetic.

Emil lifts the photograph she turned. Carries it back to the wall. Hangs it level.

EMIL
You remember the night he let me record a message for Mom? I was nine. He cut it in.

NIA
I remember.

EMIL
You come by the station, they still play the old exposures. Your stuff.

NIA
That was a long time ago.

EMIL
It was four years ago.

Nia lifts her coffee. Drinks. Says nothing. Emil watches her not say it.

EMIL (CONT'D)
You want me to come by tomorrow? Help you box the tapes?

NIA
I want you to not be a paramedic on the nights I'm here alone.

EMIL
That's not a thing I can do.

NIA
Then I'll manage.

He nods. Takes his coffee. At the door, half out:

EMIL
Dad never sold this place.

NIA
Dad never sold anything. He died.

The door eases shut. Nia stands in the fluorescents with her coffee going cold. Then she turns to the corridor.

INT. PETROV SOUND — QUIET ROOM — NIGHT

Nia pulls the heavy door shut behind her. It seals with a soft, total compression. The room goes still in a way that has texture — her breath loud in it, the fabric of her jacket whispering.

She sits on the stool. Sets a reference meter on the floor. Checks the microphone. Clips a fresh reel onto the old Studer in the corner.

Into the mic:

NIA
Nia Petrov. Calibration, Quiet Room, night one. Room tone from zero.

She lets the reel run. Holds very still. Ten seconds. Twenty. Her pulse arrives in the mic, faint, from the throat.

She stops it. Rewinds. Puts on the headphones.

Tape hiss. Room tone — that flat, dead nothing. Then a click.

Her hand stops on the transport.

Over the headphones, low and close and badly recorded, a voice that is not hers:

VOICE (FILTERED)
— Nia.

She pulls the phones off. The room is silent. She looks at the microphone. It looks back, dumb.

She puts the phones back on. Rewinds. Plays.

Silence. Hiss. Another click. Then:

VOICE (FILTERED)
— tell her — the water —

Nia's breath stops in the phones. A man's voice. Maybe seventy. Each word scraped slow, the way people talk when breathing costs.

VOICE (FILTERED) (CONT'D)
— Nia — Nia, the water —

She cuts the transport. The reel stops with a small mechanical sigh.

She sits on the stool in the foam room, hands flat on the console, and she is very still, and she does not rewind it again.

Her cell phone lights on the stool beside her. A missed call from Emil, four minutes ago.

Nia picks up the reference meter. The needle has not moved. She checks the cable. Checks it again. Then she lifts the reel off the Studer, holds the plastic in both hands like it might be warm, and reads the hand-written label her father left on it years ago, in his own block capitals:

ROOM TONE — DO NOT ERASE.

She stands there with it.

NIA
(to the room)
Who's going to answer that.

She sets the reel on the console — not back in the archive — and reaches for the light.

Her hand stops on the switch.

From the other side of the sealed door, from the empty reception, the front bell rings once and quits, as if let go.

Nia looks at the red bulb above the door. Still dark.

She picks the reel back up.

## 2. Playback

INT. STUDIO - NIGHT

A desk lamp, a cold mug, the reel face-up on the console. Nia types one-handed, phone jammed between shoulder and ear.

The laptop shows a county news page. A photograph of a lock gate, water sliding over the cill.

NIA
Eleven hours. Give or take a few minutes.

EMIL (V.O.)
I'm not supposed to talk about calls.

NIA
You're not a priest. Did your crew run it?

EMIL (V.O.)
We ran it. Weir off Lock Row. He'd been in the water an hour, maybe less.

NIA
Name.

EMIL (V.O.)
Why do you need a name?

NIA
Because I've got a voice in this room and nothing to hang it on.

On his end, a tap runs and shuts off.

EMIL (V.O.)
Kell. Arthur Kell. Seventy-one.

On the pad: ARTHUR KELL, underlined twice.

NIA
Cause.

EMIL (V.O.)
Drowned. Except there's no water in him. Lungs came up dry as a paper bag. Coroner's writing drowning anyway. What else is there to write.

Her pen rests. The Studer's counter reads 06:41. The wall clock reads something else entirely.

NIA
What time did they call it in?

EMIL (V.O.)
Six forty-one.

She sets the pen down and writes nothing.

NIA
Anything on him?

EMIL (V.O.)
Wallet. Keys. No phone. Why?

NIA
Thanks, Em.

EMIL (V.O.)
Nia—

She hangs up. Fridge hum, filament buzz, a pipe ticking behind the plaster. The room's own tone, loud now that nothing covers it.

The landline handset comes off the cradle. Her thumb finds the non-emergency number taped above the shelf, and dials six of the eight digits before the cradle takes the handset back.

From the recycling she pulls the morning paper, works the obituary page loose, and cuts a single column free with the studio scissors.

The booth door sighs open on its seal. She sets the metronome on the stool and winds it. Sixty beats. The obituary goes on the music stand, weighted with a spare reel hub.

Through the glass, in the control room, she drops the Studer into record. Phones on.

NIA
Test one. Room tone with metronome, sixty beats. Subject is a stranger. She died Tuesday.

The needle on the reference meter lies flat. Nia leans to the microphone.

NIA (CONT'D)
Ruth Fenwick, ninety-one, of Ash Row, died Tuesday at home. She is survived by no one.

Tick. Tick. Tick. A minute of it. The needle does not lift. Nia watches the capstan turn, counting under her breath, then kills the transport and rewinds.

She opens the session log — her father's old book, half its pages full — and prints on a clean line in block capitals:

RULE ONE — A TAPE IS PLAYED ONLY IN THE ABSENCE OF THE LIVING IT NAMES.

She looks at the sentence for a while. Then she plays the tape.

Tick. Tick. Tick. Room hiss underneath, the old hiss, the one she grew up inside of. Then, at the edge of audibility, an old woman's voice, close to the grille:

RUTH (FILTERED)
— turn it off, love, it's cold in here —

Nia's hand goes to the transport and stops short of it. The metronome ticks on. Hiss. Nothing else.

The reference needle swings up three decibels and settles back down.

NIA
(quietly, to the glass)
Ruth. That's Ruth.

She lifts the phones off her ears. Her cell buzzes against the console — a photo message from Emil. She opens it: a steel table, an evidence bag, a business card inside it.

She pinches the image wider.

N. PETROV — STUDIO B, and a phone number that has been dead for six years.

A voice note follows. She plays it on speaker.

EMIL (V.O.)
It was in his coat. Inside pocket, no wallet marks on it, like he carried it on its own. It's Dad's card, Nia. There's writing on the back.

The photo again. Her thumb drags the image sideways. No back-of-card shot yet.

NIA
Send me the other side.

She sets the phone face-down. Her chair rolls back, squeaking, and she crosses to the archive cabinet, pulls the bottom drawer, the one with the session logs stacked spine-up.

Dust lifts. She flips the second book open to the middle.

Her father's hand, block capitals, page after page. Dates. Durations. Subjects.

Halfway down a page dated twenty-six years ago:

A. KELL — 4 HRS — SEALED.

Nothing in the notes column. No engineer's initials. A line drawn under it, twice, hard enough to dent the paper.

Nia checks the shelf number in the margin, walks her fingers along the reels in the cabinet, and finds the gap — a clean dust ring on the shelf board, the plastic cold where the missing reel sat.

Behind her, the cell buzzes again on the console. It buzzes a second time. A third.

She does not turn around yet.

## 3. The Client

INT. STUDIO B — NIGHT

The booth door stands open six inches, wedged with a folded square of oak. VOSS sits at the console. Coat over the spare chair. Shoes side by side on the carpet. Hands folded. The calibration sheet lies in front of him, turned around, right way up.

Sixty-odd. Gray at the temples. He does not look at the door.

NIA comes in from the hall with two mugs. She stops for half a step, then keeps walking.

NIA
Outer door was locked.

VOSS
It was.

NIA
Then how are you at my desk.

VOSS
Your father kept the alley fire door on the latch for tape deliveries. Matchbook in the strike plate. It's still in there.

She sets a mug near his elbow. Steam bends toward the vent. He doesn't reach for it.

NIA
You're Voss.

VOSS
And you're the daughter. I booked the booth.

NIA
By letter. No phone number.

VOSS
I'm old.

He slides a cream envelope across the desk. Nia opens the flap with her thumbnail, tips out a cashier's check, reads the figure. Her jaw shifts once.

NIA
The rate's nine hundred a night.

VOSS
Nine hundred is the rate. That's the arrears on the building. To the cent. Your father refinanced in the spring of his last year. The note came due in April.

NIA
Nobody has that number.

VOSS
The bank has that number. I bought the note in March. You've been paying me six weeks and I've been patient about it, which is more than the bank was.

She lays the check flat and squares its edge with the desk.

NIA
Two hundred a night off. Four months. That's the deal.

VOSS
That's the deal. Eleven to five, one night, and no one in the building after midnight. Not the cleaner, not your brother, not you.

NIA
It's my studio.

VOSS
Which is why I'd rather you were somewhere else when it starts.

Nia pulls the calibration sheet back her way. Runs a finger down the pencil marks. Above them, the air handler kicks on and the room's hum thickens by three decibels.

NIA
What are you recording? Voice, instrument, room tone?

VOSS
Room tone.

NIA
That's a long night for room tone.

VOSS
Your high-pass is set at thirty.

She looks up. Nothing on the sheet says thirty in words. It says thirty in pencil, in her own hand, in a box she drew herself.

NIA
How —

VOSS
Take it to nineteen. The room won't null until you do.

NIA
Nineteen hertz is under the fundamental of everything. There's nothing down there but rumble and the fridge.

VOSS
Then you won't miss it.

Nia swivels to the console, punches up the tone generator, sends a sine to the booth's talkback speaker. The meter leans into the red.

NIA
Thirty. That's thirty.

She walks the dial down. Twenty-six. Twenty-four. The tone sags in pitch, then thins, then at twenty-two it seems to move behind the wall. At nineteen the monitors go quiet. Not quiet — gone. The VU still swings. The booth is still full of a tone the room has swallowed whole.

Nia leans in. The hair on her forearms lifts.

NIA
That's not a null. That's a hole.

VOSS
Your father hung a second wall in there in ninety-eight. Never wrote down why. Nineteen is where the two walls agree.

She kills the generator. Room tone rushes back in — the hum, the air handler, the compressor's gain light blinking once and holding.

NIA
How do you know what he did in ninety-eight.

VOSS
Because I paid for the lumber.

He lifts the mug at last. Drinks. Sets it down on the wood without a sound.

VOSS
One more thing. I'll want the shelf empty.

The cabinet sits behind her. Second drawer, spine-up. A clean dust ring where a reel has been missing for a quarter of a century.

NIA
What shelf.

VOSS
The one with the gap in it. What you do with the rest of the reels is your business. That slot needs to be nothing but dust by eleven.

Nia crosses her arms. Her thumb finds the seam of her sleeve and works it.

NIA
Who's Kell?

He looks at her properly for the first time. Something in his face settles, the way a meter settles when the signal stops fighting the gate.

VOSS
A man with a bad heart and better manners than mine. He lasted four hours.

NIA
Four hours in a room, doing what?

VOSS
Listening. He was very good at it. That's what killed him.

The fire door in the alley ticks against its frame, the matchbook softening. Nia reaches over and flips the calibration sheet facedown.

NIA
You'll take your tape at five and I'll take the check at five.

VOSS
You'll take the check now. I don't like to owe people.

She folds the check, puts it in her back pocket. He rises, takes his coat, folds it over his arm rather than putting it on.

VOSS
Eleven to five. Nothing in the building after midnight. And Miss Petrov — leave the monitors off once the latch closes. Whatever it puts in your headphones, you can hear it tomorrow.

NIA
And if I listen tonight?

VOSS
Then it will know you're listening.

He goes to the hall. At the doorway he stops with his back to her, one hand flat on the frame.

VOSS
Four hours was all Kell had. I've booked six.

The fire door closes. Its spring pulls the latch until it half-catches, and half-catches again.

Nia sits. Her hand goes to the high-pass dial and turns it down, past twenty-two, and stops at nineteen. The meter swings. The room says nothing at all.

## 4. Dead Air

INT. CONTROL ROOM — NIGHT

The wall clock reads 2:41. A dry click every second. Under the click, the capstan turning. Under that, the HVAC breathing through the floor grate.

NIA PETROV at the console, headphones around her neck, a log open under her pencil. 2:10 — NO SIGNAL. 2:20 — NO SIGNAL. 2:30 — NO SIGNAL.

Through the glass, the booth is lit. VOSS in the chair, hands folded, coat over the armrest, eyes open and not moving much.

The meter needle rests on the floor. High-pass at nineteen.

Nia writes 2:40 — NO SIGNAL. Presses the talkback.

NIA
Still nothing.

VOSS
(through the glass, thin)
Then we're doing it right.

She lifts her finger. The room comes back in — tape hiss, a faint wow in the capstan, traffic three floors down.

The hiss thickens.

The pencil stops.

VOICE (FILTERED)
Emil Petrov. Oh-four-forty-seven.

A click. Hiss again, flat and even. The way it was.

In the booth, Voss has not opened his mouth.

Nia's hand goes to STOP. The reels slow, tape chirping against the head stack. Then she pulls the breaker down at the wall.

The console lamps die. The meter drops. The booth stays lit on its own circuit. Voss sits in it a moment longer, unhurried, then pushes the door.

VOSS
You stopped the machine.

NIA
You heard that.

VOSS
I heard the room speak. There's a distinction, and it's the whole of our business.

NIA
What did you give it?

VOSS
Forty grams of restraint and nothing else. I've been sitting on my hands for three hours.

Nia turns the log face down and pushes it away.

NIA
We're finished.

VOSS
We're at two-forty. The clock is the arrangement.

NIA
The arrangement was that I'd record nothing. That thing used a name. My brother's name.

Voss takes his watch from the armrest, straps it on, doesn't look at the dial.

VOSS
It gave you a time as well. Most people only ever get the one.

NIA
Get out of my building.

Coat over his arm rather than on. At the top of the stairs, without turning:

VOSS
Whatever you do to that tape, do it before the hour. Rooms like this don't repeat themselves for free.

The street door pulls shut. Its latch catches on the first swing and holds.

Nia stands with her palms flat on the console. The building's hum climbs back through the wood into her wrists.

Boots. Fast, on the stairs. Two at a time.

EMIL PETROV comes in still in his jacket, radio on his shoulder, satchel across his chest. Twenty-eight and moving like he's already on shift.

EMIL
Where is it.

NIA
Where's what.

EMIL
The alarm. You said the CO alarm was going and you sounded like you were standing in the road.

He takes a small grey monitor from the satchel, switches it on, holds it to the floor vent. One chirp. Zero. He moves it along the baseboard, past the door, past the console.

EMIL (CONT'D)
It's a reading of one. That's air, Nia. That's what air reads.

NIA
It went off. It might be a sensor going.

EMIL
It's an electric panel. It's got no gas to sense.

NIA
Then it's the sensor going.

He sets the monitor down. Looks at the dead console, the hand-thrown breaker, the lit booth beyond the glass with its empty chair.

EMIL
Power's off on the desk and on in the room.

NIA
I'm rewiring.

EMIL
At three in the morning. In the dark. With the door unlocked.

Nia's mouth opens. The clock clicks. She reaches into the drawer and slides a folded paper across the counter, a pen on top of it.

NIA
I need you to sign something. The sale. It needs a witness who isn't me.

Emil unzips his jacket, reaches inside for a pen, stops. His hand comes out empty.

EMIL
You called me at two-fifteen and told me to come quick and breathe into a machine.

NIA
And you came. That's probably the whole of it.

EMIL
That's not the whole of it.

He picks up the monitor, checks the reading out of habit, clips it to his belt. At the door to the hall he turns back.

EMIL (CONT'D)
If you're going to lie to me, lie about something I can't check in thirty seconds.

NIA
Sign the paper, Em.

EMIL
Put it in the post.

Boots down the stairs, slower this time. The street door opens and closes. Then only the HVAC and the clock.

Nia lifts the reel off the machine. Writes on the box in grease pencil — VOSS 11:00–2:41 — and sets it on the top shelf, spine out, where she can see it from the desk.

She opens the log to a clean page. Writes: PETROV, N. — SESSION. Under hours: 3:20 to 5:00. Her pencil lifts. In the margin, small: 04:47.

Then she reaches over and throws the breaker up.

The console wakes with a click. The clock reads 3:19.

She threads the blank reel, presses the transport into gear, watches tape run across the heads for the length of one slow breath. Sets the high-pass to nineteen. The meter swings once and settles.

Her hand finds the booth door handle. Cold, and not quite still.

## 5. The Quiet Room

INT. QUIET BOOTH — NIGHT

The door pulls shut and the world goes in order: the HVAC hum, then the street, then the hall clock's tick. Foam wedges floor to ceiling. Nia's breathing fills what's left.

A shelf at shoulder height carries a Revox. Beside it, a bulk eraser the size of a brick.

She sets the VOSS reel on the spindle. Through the glass, the control room glows amber. Voss sits at the console, jacket off, sleeves rolled, both hands folded on the log like a man waiting on a platform.

She presses the talkback.

NIA
I'm going to wipe it.

VOSS
(through the speaker)
You could. You'd be erasing the container.

NIA
That's all a tape is.

VOSS
Then you've learned something since eleven o'clock.

Her thumb finds the eraser switch. It travels halfway. Her other hand comes up and hits record.

The transport clunks. The needle kicks and falls back to zero.

NIA
(into the dead room)
Nia Petrov. Thirty-four. Fourteen Aldergate, second floor. It's three twenty-two in the morning and I'm standing in here because I said I would.

The needle doesn't move. She counts her own breaths — four in, four out.

NIA (CONT'D)
If you're going to take something, take the address. I don't want it.

Nothing. She sits on the stool. Stands up. Puts her palm on the shelf edge and leaves it there until the metal warms.

Then the hiss changes.

A grain enters it, steady, sand poured onto a drum skin. Her hand comes off the shelf.

The grain resolves into a room she can't see. Wind. A strap creaking. Underneath it, a voice she has known since it cracked.

EMIL (V.O.)
— can you hear me? Ma'am. Ma'am, can you hear me.

The stool goes over. She doesn't hear it land.

EMIL (V.O.) (CONT'D)
Okay. Okay. Stay with me, I've got you, I've got —

A click. Fabric shifting. Quieter, mouth pressed to a shoulder radio.

EMIL (V.O.) (CONT'D)
Two victims. One's not —

The hiss swallows the rest.

EMIL (V.O.) (CONT'D)
Tell my sister the papers are in the —

Silence. The needle drops off the scale.

In the control room, Voss's hand flattens on the console. He doesn't reach for the talkback. Above the glass, the clock reads 04:47.

Nia crosses the room in two steps and hauls on the handle. It turns a quarter inch and seats. She puts her shoulder into the frame. The gasket holds.

She hits the glass with her palm.

NIA
Open it.

VOSS
(through the talkback, mild)
I haven't touched anything.

NIA
Open the door.

VOSS
It opens outward. The seal has to equalize. Give it a minute.

She hits the glass again. Stops. Her hand stays flat against it and she makes herself breathe slow, the way she'd talk somebody down off a ledge.

NIA
How long.

VOSS
A minute. Two.

She turns her back to the glass and slides down it, sits on the floor among the wedges.

NIA
You knew what it would say.

VOSS
I knew when.

NIA
That's the same thing.

VOSS
It isn't.

She looks up at the clock. It hasn't moved since the tape stopped.

NIA
The papers. He said the papers are in the —

VOSS
You'll find them.

NIA
How do you know.

VOSS
Because he knew you'd be looking.

Nia's jaw works. She looks at the floor, at her own hands, at anything that isn't the glass.

NIA
You booked eleven to two forty-one.

VOSS
That session was mine. Yes.

NIA
Then who booked this one.

Voss turns the logbook around so the open page faces the glass. Her own handwriting. NIA PETROV — SESSION. 3:20 to 5:00. Below it, a line ruled and empty.

NIA (CONT'D)
What's the line for.

VOSS
The witness.

NIA
Yours is blank too.

VOSS
It gets filled at the end.

The gasket sighs. The door eases open an inch and the control room breathes in — HVAC, street, a car idling at the light.

Nia doesn't get up.

NIA
You're not going to erase my tape.

VOSS
No.

NIA
Don't.

She rises, steps past the bulk eraser, and presses stop on the Revox instead. The reel slows and halts. Somewhere in that ribbon Emil's voice lies folded flat between an hour of nothing and an hour of nothing.

The pencil comes off the shelf. She turns the reel box over and writes.

NIA (CONT'D)
Emil. Four forty-seven.

VOSS
You understand what the room does with a name written on it.

NIA
It keeps it.

She sets the box down spine out, where she can see it from the doorway.

NIA (CONT'D)
Seven o'clock. You'll be in that witness line, and you'll be in this room when I play it.

Voss's pen lifts off the log. It stays off.

VOSS
I've never heard one from inside.

NIA
Then take notes.

She pulls the booth door wide and holds it, and waits.

NIA (CONT'D)
Bring your coat. It's cold in here.

Voss closes the log without filling the line.
