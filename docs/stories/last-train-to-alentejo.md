# Last Train to Alentejo

_Screenplay · linear · genres: noir romance · 3643 words_

## 1. The Borrowed Name

INT. SANTA APOLÓNIA STATION - NIGHT

Rain ticks on the vaulted glass like a fingernail. Under the shed, one overhead lamp burns a wet yellow circle on the tiles. The rest of the platform is dark.

INES VALE, 34, comes out of the downpour in a navy raincoat too heavy for the season, shoulders black with water, one small leather case in her hand. Her heels strike the tile in a rhythm she keeps having to correct.

At the ticket window, a clerk sleeps upright. Ines slides the passport under the grille. He wakes, thumbs it open, takes his time with the photo page. The name there is MARIA DO ROSÁRIO. The clerk looks at Ines. Ines looks at the departures board. He stamps the ticket and pushes it back.

The last carriage. SENHOR DUARTE, 60s, uniform pressed and ancient, a brass punch hanging from his wrist.

DUARTE
Last carriage, senhora. Only one other passenger tonight.

INES
That's fine.

He glances at the ticket, then at her, then steps aside. She climbs. He watches her ankles disappear.

INT. TRAIN CARRIAGE - NIGHT

The corridor sways. Compartment four holds a single passenger: RAFAEL SOMBRA, 46, back to the window, hands folded on a walking stick, gray at the temples, a face built for quiet rooms.

Ines slides the door open. He does not look up.

INES
You don't mind.

RAFAEL
I've been waiting for someone to mind. Please.

She lifts the case to the rack. Sits. The carriage lamps dim and settle. Outside, the platform begins to slide.

Rafael taps the stick twice against his shoe.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
Maria.

She goes still.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
Maria do Rosário. It's on the ticket. The clerk held it up to the light — the window makes a fair mirror.

The last platform lamp slides off the glass. Black now, and rain running in diagonal threads.

INES
You have the advantage.

RAFAEL
I have the disadvantage. I know what you're not. That's a poor sort of knowing.

INES
Then I'll get off at the next stop.

RAFAEL
Entroncamento. Three hours. The agent there is a friend of mine. He'd remember a woman traveling under a dead woman's name.

She looks at him fully for the first time.

INES
How much?

RAFAEL
That isn't what I—

INES
Everyone wants something. How much.

He turns the stick upright, rests his chin on it. Rain fills the pause.

RAFAEL
Fifteen years ago I inspected the line through Alentejo. Night train, two in the morning, last carriage, the rails gave. Nine people. I wrote the report. Everyone told me what to write and I wrote it. Afterward they found a young inspector's name on every inspection sheet for that stretch. Twenty-one years old. Nobody could find her to ask her why.

Her hand has drifted to her ribs, to something under the raincoat.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
You signed what I dictated. I've had a long time to wonder what you'd look like now.

INES
You have the wrong person.

RAFAEL
I have the right person and the wrong name. That's the trouble we're both in.

The train grinds around a curve. The lamp above them swings. Her case slides half a foot along the rack and stops.

INES
You want the ledger.

His eyebrows lift a fraction. He didn't say ledger.

RAFAEL
I want to know what's in it.

INES
No.

RAFAEL
Then say my name before Palmeira. Because when I tell the conductor that the woman in compartment four is carrying a passport surrendered at a funeral in Alcácer do Sal, your night ends earlier than Alentejo.

Silence. The lamp keeps swinging.

INES
Sombra.

RAFAEL
That's the beginning of something.

The compartment door slides open. Duarte in the corridor, punch raised, taking them both in with the flat patience of a man who has watched every shape of darkness come through this carriage.

DUARTE
Tickets.

Ines hands hers over. Duarte reads the name without moving his mouth. He punches twice. Hands it back.

DUARTE (CONT'D)
Senhora do Rosário.

The rain says something in the gap.

DUARTE (CONT'D)
It's going to be a late night. The two of you should be civil.

The door slides shut. His shape moves away down the corridor.

Rafael leans forward. Almost gentle now.

RAFAEL
He comes back at Palmeira. He always does. So tell me now — when he does, do I say the name you're using, or the name you left behind?

Ines sets the case on her knees. Opens it. A folded coat, a small tin, nothing else.

INES
At Palmeira you can say whatever you like.

She takes out the tin, works it open, lifts out a thin book bound in green cloth. She holds it where he can see it, not where he can reach it.

INES (CONT'D)
We both know it isn't my name you want. So. What's it worth to you.

Rafael's stick goes still. Outside, the rain has not stopped, and ahead of them Alentejo is still just a word the night hasn't reached.

## 2. The Inspector's Signature

INT. COMPARTMENT FOUR - NIGHT

A brass latch, worn pale where thumbs have been. Ines keeps two fingers on it. Beyond the glass the river runs black, and the rain lays a shine on it.

RAFAEL
That side's the river. You'd want the corridor.

Her hand comes off the latch. Into her lap, next to the tin.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
Sit down, Inspector.

INES
I'm not.

RAFAEL
No. Tonight you're Senhora do Rosário. I heard Duarte say it.

The stick lifts, comes down soft against the floor. He settles back into the cushion and lets the silence take its time.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
Eleven years ago the night train out of Barreiro went off at the curve past the cork oaks. A kilometre short of the bridge at Vale de Figueira. Four in the fourth carriage. A fifth man died on the Thursday in Beja with his boots cut off.

No hurry in it. He could be reading a timetable.

INES
I know the derailment.

RAFAEL
You know the newspaper of it. Three columns and a photograph of the engine on its side like a dropped stove.

Her thumb finds the edge of the tin and works at it. Stops.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
The inquiry wanted a cause, and the cause wanted a signature. The sleeper had gone. Anyone walking that stretch with a hand lamp would have seen the split — the report said so, in a hand that sloped to the right. Signed by a junior inspector, twenty-three years old, eleven days before the wreck.

INES
And?

RAFAEL
She was never found. The inquiry sat nine days with her chair empty, read her name into the record, and moved on to the pension payments.

Rain goes sideways across the glass. His reflection hangs in it, the weather coming through his face.

INES
You were at the inquiry.

RAFAEL
Back row. I've a copy of the report in my coat, creased to where I can open it in the dark. There's a line in it that isn't in the version filed with the court.

Ines's palm goes flat against her ribs — one quick press, covered by smoothing her coat.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
You can put your hand down. I've already seen where you keep it.

She doesn't. She leaves it there, holding her own side like a woman with a stitch.

INES
Tell me what happens at Palmeira.

RAFAEL
At Palmeira a man from the Guarda comes aboard with a folder under his arm and a sergeant's patience. He doesn't walk this far down the corridor without a reason.

INES
And you'd give him one.

RAFAEL
I'd have to be annoyed.

The carriage swings over points. She reaches for the latch again — a reflex — and catches her own hand in the air. Looks at it. Lowers it to her knee.

INES
What do you want.

Something moves at the corner of his mouth that isn't a smile.

RAFAEL
Say that again.

INES
You heard it.

RAFAEL
I heard it. It's the first thing you've handed me tonight that didn't come wrapped around a lie.

He leans forward. The stick lies across his knees, put down on purpose.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
She kept a book. Not the report — the book the report came out of. Ruled pages, green cloth. Every inspection she ever walked, in ink, with the weather written at the top of the page.

Her fingers find the tin's lid and stay there.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
The Guarda turned her room over in Beja and came out with a hairbrush. Nothing else.

INES
You think I have it.

RAFAEL
I think it's in that tin. I think it's been against your ribs since Santa Apolónia. And I think you'll want the rest of it before you decide what to do about that.

Ahead the line runs downhill. The wheels let up their noise for a few seconds — small mercy — then take it back.

INES
Then say the rest.

RAFAEL
There's a signature above hers on the certificate. Senior inspector. The one who was owed the walk.

His thumb moves along the stick. Stops.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
Mine.

She gives him nothing. It costs him to go on.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
I signed it at a desk in Barreiro. Never put my boots on that stretch in my life. Eleven years I've carried it, and tonight a woman gets on my train with a dead girl's name in her mouth and her fingers on the wrong latch.

He picks the stick up. Sets it down again.

RAFAEL (CONT'D)
Ask me your question once more. I'll answer it properly.

Ines takes her hand off the tin. She sits back. Her eyes come up to his face and stay there, level — the way a person looks when they've decided to spend something they can't get back.

INES
What do you want, Sombra.

RAFAEL
A name on a page. Whose hand pulled the fishplates at Vale de Figueira — written in hers, not in mine.

## 3. The Ticket Punch

INT. NIGHT TRAIN, COMPARTMENT SIX — NIGHT

Corridor lamps slide across the window in slow strokes, light the glass, let it go dark again.

The door runs back on its runner. DUARTE fills the gap. Cap low. The brass punch hangs off his little finger by a leather loop, swinging a half-inch with the rocking of the car. Rain on his shoulders, already gone to steam in the carriage heat.

His eyes travel the compartment like a man counting coins. Her collar, wet at the seam. The square shape under her coat at the ribs. The walking stick hooked over Sombra's knee. Two coats on the hooks, both men's wool, neither of them his.

DUARTE
Tickets.

Rafael folds his out of his breast pocket. The punch bites. The paper jumps. A small circle of confetti catches in Duarte's cuff. He hands the ticket back without looking at it.

Ines works hers up out of her cuff, damp, softened at the corner. Before it has cleared her fingers, Duarte speaks. Eyes on her face. Not on the paper.

DUARTE (CONT'D)
Marta Caeiro.

Her hand stops. Below the floor, the bogies take a joint and count it.

INES
You read that before you had it in your hand.

DUARTE
It's a good name. There's a Caeiro in the churchyard at Vale de Figueira. Been there since the flood.

He takes the ticket at last. Punches it blind. Returns it. The hole sits dead centre, north of the date.

DUARTE (CONT'D)
Says you paid cash at Santa Apolónia. Cash is a good colour on a ticket.

INES
Is there a bad colour.

DUARTE
Green. Green means the office printed it, and the office remembers who asked.

He looks at the walking stick. Then at the man holding it.

DUARTE (CONT'D)
Sleeper's made up. Two berths, empty all the way down. Courtesy of the company.

INES
I'm fine here.

DUARTE
Nobody's fine here.

He turns his head, unhurried, to Rafael.

DUARTE (CONT'D)
Inspector.

RAFAEL
Not for eleven years.

DUARTE
Once is enough. That's what the certificate's for.

The punch swings on its loop, a small brass pendulum. Duarte touches two fingers to the brim of his cap and steps back into the corridor.

Then his hand comes to rest on the door.

He slides it shut. Thumb on the outside handle, a small practised turn. The latch drops. The click comes up through the floorboards.

Through the corridor window his back retreats — coat, cap, the punch swinging — and the lamps go out behind him, section by section, until the corridor is a long brown throat and he is the only lit thing in it, far down at the end, under the last bulb, where he stops.

He takes a folded slip of paper from his breast pocket. Unfolds it. Reads.

Ines reaches across and takes the inside handle. Turns it. The handle moves freely. The door does not. She tries it again, slower, as if the second time might be a different mechanism.

RAFAEL
It's latched from his side.

INES
There's a lever.

RAFAEL
There was. He's put the pin in it.

She sits back. Under her coat her thumb finds the corner of the tin and presses until the flesh whitens.

INES
Is that regulation?

RAFAEL
On this train it is.

Down the corridor, Duarte refolds the slip, squares it against his thumbnail, puts it away. He does not look up.

Ines watches him.

INES
He read my name like a man reading his own.

RAFAEL
He reads everything twice. It's a habit. He reads with his lips.

The train leans into a curve. Her shoulder goes against the window frame and stays there. Outside, fog stands in the fields, close to the rails, lit for a second and gone.

INES
Somebody told him my name before he ever saw the ticket.

Rafael shifts the stick an inch on his knee. His eyes have not left the far end of the corridor.

RAFAEL
Somebody told him more than that.

He leans forward, takes the blind by its cord, and pulls it down over the glass.

## 4. Two Coats in the Dark

INT. NIGHT TRAIN COMPARTMENT - NIGHT

The blind hangs level with the window frame. Rain ticks against it, steady as a fingernail. Rafael’s stick lies across his knees; one hand rests on the handle. Ines sits opposite, coat buttoned, the ledger’s edge a hard line under the wool.

RAFAEL
The ledger. You’ve carried it since Santa Apolónia.

INES
You’ve been counting my ribs.

RAFAEL
I’ve been counting the minutes. There’s a difference.

He turns his palm up. Waits.

RAFAEL
Hand it over, and when Duarte comes back I’ll tell him the name on your ticket is a clerical mistake. He’ll believe me. He’s believed worse.

INES
And if I don’t?

RAFAEL
Then I’ll tell him the truth. Or most of it.

Ines looks at the blind. At the door. Her hand finds the water carafe, lifts it, sets it down without pouring.

INES
You had a photograph in your breast pocket at the platform. I saw the corner.

RAFAEL
You saw a ticket stub.

INES
I saw a woman’s shoulder. White dress.

Rafael’s hand closes on the stick handle. A muscle ticks once in his jaw.

RAFAEL
The ledger.

Ines stands. The compartment is narrow enough that standing brings her knee against his. She braces one hand on the luggage rack. The other goes to the blind cord, not to him.

INES
Open my coat, then.

RAFAEL
What?

INES
You want what’s against my ribs. Come and take it.

He doesn’t move. She leans down and kisses him. Not tender. A transaction with teeth. His mouth is cold from the corridor; his breath goes out hard through his nose. One of his hands leaves the stick and finds her waist, fingers spread over the coat button. Ines slides her hand inside his lapel, past the worn lining, past the ticket stub, into the breast pocket.

Her fingers close on paper. A stiff photograph edge. She draws it out, breaks the kiss, and steps back against the door.

RAFAEL
Ines.

INES
That’s not my name.

She looks at the photograph. A woman in a white dress stands before a whitewashed wall, one hand shading her eyes. On the back, in ink: For Rafael — the last good day. A.

Ines turns it over. Her face does not change. Her hand does.

INES
This is her.

RAFAEL
Yes.

INES
You knew her.

RAFAEL
I knew her.

INES
Before or after she died?

He sets the stick against the seat and stands. Outside, a signal lamp slides red across the blind and is gone.

RAFAEL
You’re wearing her name. You’re carrying a ledger with her initials stamped inside the cover. You think I’m the one who should be explaining?

Ines holds the photograph against her chest, face out. Her thumb covers the woman’s hand.

INES
I think you’ve been explaining since Santa Apolónia. Just not with words.

RAFAEL
The ledger.

INES
Is this why you let me board? This photograph?

He steps closer. She does not step back.

RAFAEL
I let you board because Duarte had already seen your ticket. I let you sit across from me because I wanted to see how long you could carry her name without flinching.

INES
And?

RAFAEL
You lasted until Beja.

Ines looks at the photo again. The woman’s stance, the shade of the wall. She turns it face down and holds it out.

INES
Take it.

Rafael doesn’t.

RAFAEL
No.

INES
It’s yours.

RAFAEL
It was. Now it’s yours. You’ll want it when Duarte asks who you are.

INES
Why?

RAFAEL
Because I’m going to tell him you’re her daughter.

Ines’s hand tightens on the photograph. The train takes a curve; the stick slides along the seat and knocks against her shoe.

INES
She didn’t have a daughter.

RAFAEL
No. But you’ll say she did, and I’ll say I knew you when you were small. He’ll check the ledger. He’ll find the initials. He’ll believe the rest.

INES
You’re offering me a lie.

RAFAEL
I’m offering you a better one.

Ines looks at the door. The lever, still dead. Then at Rafael. She slips the photograph into her own breast pocket, inside the coat, against the ledger. The paper edges align.

INES
No.

RAFAEL
No?

INES
I’ll tell him I’m her daughter. But I’ll tell him the ledger is mine. Not yours to trade.

She sits. Crosses her legs. The coat falls open. The ledger catches the compartment light, then disappears as she buttons it.

RAFAEL
He’ll search you.

INES
Then he’ll find her photograph. And your name on the back.

Rafael looks at the blind. At the door. His hands hang at his sides. He does not reach for the stick.

INES
Sit down, Senhor Sombra. You’re blocking the light.

He doesn’t sit.

RAFAEL
There’s something else.

INES
Of course there is.

RAFAEL
The report wasn’t signed by a junior inspector. It was signed by me.

## 5. Alentejo, Before Dawn

EXT. PLATFORM — NIGHT

Fog pools under the single lamp at the end of the platform, then takes everything past it. Where a name board once hung there are two iron bolts and a rust stain shaped like the sign. The night train stands, steam sinking around its wheels. Above the track, the signal holds red.

The carriage door hangs open. INES fills it, coat buttoned to the throat, an oilcloth parcel flat under her arm. RAFAEL is already down on the stones, hatless, hair flat with rain.

Far along the platform a lantern swings: SENHOR DUARTE, walking the length of the train at the pace of a man who has already seen everything worth seeing.

RAFAEL
This halt came off the timetable in sixty-one. Signal's against us. Three minutes.

INES
Then talk fast.

RAFAEL
Give me the book, and the platform keeps its mouth shut.

INES
It's a platform. It has no mouth.

RAFAEL
Duarte has one. He was at the weighbridge in March. He remembers what the third column said before somebody wrote over it.

Ines doesn't move.

RAFAEL
I'm not asking for myself. The company wants its property back. You want to walk off this train with your name still in the clear. Both of those can happen inside two minutes.

INES
And if I say no.

RAFAEL
Then I do what I'm paid to do. You do what you've been doing since Lisbon. Neither of us sleeps.

Ines looks past him — down the platform, to the swinging lantern, and back. She unwraps the oilcloth. The ledger is thick, corners soft, the spine dark with handling.

RAFAEL
Ines.

She steps down to the stones, crosses to the iron bolts, and strikes a match on one of them. It gutters, steadies. She holds the flame to the spine.

RAFAEL
That's nine years of record.

INES
It's nine years of my hand.

The glue takes. She turns the book over and lets the fire climb down through the pages, and the numbers come up out of the paper one column at a time, orange, then grey, then nothing. Smoke goes sideways in the fog.

Rafael takes one step. His arm comes up. The burning book is between them, close enough to feel.

He puts his arm down.

Far off, the lantern has stopped. It holds still a long moment, then goes on swinging, away.

The engine sounds two notes.

Ines waits until the fire reaches her fingers, then opens her hand. What falls is not a book. She steps around it and walks into the fog, away from the train.

Behind her, his boots find the iron step, then the stones, then the fog, in no hurry at all.
