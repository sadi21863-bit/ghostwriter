# The Three Tides

_Novel · NON-LINEAR (three interleaved timelines) · genres: literary mystery + slow-burn romance + folk horror · 12780 words_

## 1. The Salt Index

The salt index for Bellamy Shoal arrived in a plastic crate that smelled of cold iron and old rope, and Iris signed for it at half past six because the regular courier had a dental appointment. Nobody else in the customs house archive was awake to argue the point.

Three notebooks, a brass gauge tag, and a sheaf of correspondence filed in the wrong decade. She set the kettle on the radiator and worked through the notebooks with the radio murmuring the inshore forecast. Soundings, soundings, soundings — the shoal recorded in patient pencil, four fathoms where the chart promised six, silt moving the way silt always moved, sandbars shrugging east and west along the estuary mouth.

Then, near the back of the second notebook, a page of numbers that belonged to no surveyor on the roster.

Depths of nought point four. A street.

She read it twice, thumb pressed flat to the paper, before she pulled the chart from the flat drawer. Where the notebook showed dry ground, the chart showed a shelf of blue six fathoms deep with a name printed across it in italic. HALLOWAY BANK. She checked the signature. E. Trewin, 1965. Then a second entry, same hand, same notation, 1995. Then a loose leaf laid in like a bookmark, pencilled, dated 2015 in a different and smaller hand and signed only *M.*

The arithmetic took her less time than finding a pencil. Thirty years. Thirty years. And then nothing at all — because the next one was due.

She wrote the depths on the back of her wrist and called Priya, who answered from a car park with the handbrake on.

"Tell me you're not going out there."

"There's a marked shoal where the chart says six fathoms and three sets of soundings that agree to the inch. I'm not going to sit in the archive and guess."

"You're an archivist. Guessing is the job." A car door shut somewhere behind her. "Iris. It's a spring tide tonight."

"Which is why the shoal will be dry."

"Which is why the channel patrol will be busy and nobody will come and get you." Priya sighed. "Take the dory. Take the radio. Take the flare gun out of the drawer that says flares on it, and if you see anything strange, be strange back."

Fog came down the estuary at eight, and the dory's outboard pushed her through it at five knots with the chart under a bungee and the notebook sealed in a bag against her chest. Water flattened. The smell changed from brine to something older — wet brick, cold cellars, a stone staircase full of rain. The sounder ticked down and down.

At twenty past nine the world went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with fog.

The prow nudged mud. Iris cut the engine, lifted it, and stepped out into six inches of water with her boots laced tight, and all around her the shoal was baring like a held breath let go — flat grey shelves of silt, a ribcage of roof beams, and ahead of her a street. Cobbles, laid in courses. A drain grate. Two walls standing to shoulder height with the plaster still on the inside, and a door frame through which she could see a second street and a third.

Her camera went up. She got four frames before the cold climbed over the tops of her boots.

Something moved the water behind her, a long sugh of it, and she turned and the channel had filled in a way channels did not fill, and the dory was gone out on the flood with its painter trailing. Iris ran the wrong way, then the right way, mud sucking at her shins, one boot lost to the ankle, and the water came up over her knees with a grudge in it.

A light swung across the street.

"Don't move," a man's voice said, very calm, from a survey catamaran sliding in sideways. "If you move you'll go into the drain."

She stood still. Cold to the thigh now. He came over the gunwale and into the water up to his chest, dry suit hood tight around a long face, and got a hand around her wrist and hauled.

"Other arm. On the rail. There."

They got her aboard. Her legs went out from under her and she sat down hard on a coil of rope. A blanket landed on her shoulders and she clutched it with both hands, shaking so badly the rails rattled.

"You're from the archive," he said. "Courier told me someone signed for the crate."

"Iris Vane."

"Rowan Baird. Tide surveyor." He pulled his hood back. There was a nip out of his chin that had healed crooked. "That was four minutes. Four minutes and I'd have been sounding a body."

"Then it's lucky you were out here." She got her teeth apart and kept them that way. "Why were you out here?"

He looked at the street going under behind her, and something crossed his face and was put away. "Same reason you were. The numbers don't fit." A hand went to a locker, came back with a thermos, and he poured a lid of tea and put it into her hands instead of giving it to her. "Drink that. Then tell me what you found."

"Soundings." The lid burned her palms and she liked it. "1965, 1995, 2015. All three in Trewin's hand until the last one. All three baring the same street. They're thirty years apart and the third of them is due this year, and nobody in that archive has flagged it, because it's filed under a shoal name that got removed from the charts in 1971."

"Nineteen seventy-one," Rowan said. "Interesting."

"Why?"

He crouched by the sounder and thumbed it awake. The screen lit his face green. "Because that's the year the coast authority stopped funding the tide gauge that recorded those numbers."

Iris set the lid down on the deck. "The gauge the readings came from isn't on the chart."

"No."

"So a dead gauge has been taking soundings for sixty years."

He didn't answer straight away. He spun the wheel, put the catamaran's nose toward the channel markers, and let the engine come down to a mutter before he said, "The nineteenth. That's the next spring tide. Twelve days." A glance over his shoulder, flat and unhurried, taking her in. "I've been out here four times since March. Every time the water bares her a little longer and a little wider, and every time the low comes a few minutes sooner than the table says it should."

The fog thinned enough to show the point, and the lamp on it, dark and pointless, and a gull crying over nothing.

Iris pulled the loose leaf out of the bag against her chest, unfolded it, and smoothed it flat on her knee. Pencil. A small neat hand. Depths to the inch, a row of them, and no title, no date but the year.

"Twelve days," she said. "I'll need a boat with a working sounder, a copy of the 1958 foreshore survey, and someone who can tell me a lie convincingly enough to keep the coast authority off the shoal before survey season opens."

Rowan put the throttle down. "You'll need a second blanket," he said. "And you should know I've already applied for the permit and been refused, which makes whatever happens on the nineteenth my fault, not yours."

## 2. Caul Water

The caul came away whole — a grey hood Maren lifted off the baby's face with two fingers and laid in the basin before the grandmother could get a look at it. Below the window, the highest spring tide of that decade was already in the lane, slapping the bottom step with a sound like somebody laying a table.

"Girl," Maren said. "Seven pounds if she's an ounce."

Nell Trewin had her chin down and her eyes half shut, and she didn't ask for the child, which Maren had learned to note but not to say. The grandmother stood at the foot of the bed with her hands wrapped in her apron, watching the basin.

"That what I think it is?"

"That's a bit of membrane and it's going out with the water." Maren wiped her hands, folded the towel, and put herself between the basin and the old woman. "Your daughter needs tea and she needs to sleep and she needs the pair of you to stop staring at me."

The lane outside had gone the colour of pewter. Every few minutes the tide knocked something loose — a bucket, a doorstep, once a whole crate that went past the window end over end and did not come back. Nobody in the parish had been born in a dry room since Thursday.

Nell's hand came up and caught Maren's wrist, and the grip was much stronger than the rest of her.

"It's counting," she said.

"Who is?"

"Halloway." The word came out flat, the way you'd say the name of a neighbour you didn't care for. "That's two this spring. It counts them with the hood on."

Maren had heard the word before, mostly from men who wanted to be funny about it. She had never heard it said indoors, quietly, by a woman who had just done eleven hours of work. "You're tired. That's all this is."

Nell let go and turned her face to the wall. "You write her down and it'll find her."

The knock came at the half hour, three blows and a pause and one more, which was the parish's way of announcing itself. Maren got the door open against the weight of the water and found Skerry, the clerk, in his good coat and borrowed waders, with his satchel held above his head like a man fording a river to bring a message from a king.

"Come through the churchyard," he said. "Half the graves are out of the ground. Mr. Pew's stone is lying flat in the road." He peered past her, into the room. "Is it done?"

"It's done and she's sleeping, and if you wake her I'll put you back out that door and let the tide have the satchel."

"Only need the particulars." He had his book out already, the calf-bound return, and a pencil that he licked. "Name of child. Time. Any mark or peculiarity at birth."

"Nothing peculiar. You can put that down. Nothing peculiar at all."

His pencil hovered. Down in the lane something turned over with a long wooden groan. Skerry looked at the basin on the sill, and then at the towel over it, and then at Maren, and his face did a small complicated thing — not surprise, she thought later, and not greed either. Recognition. A man arriving at a door he'd been told would be there.

"I'll take the paper," he said. "Whatever you've written. Save you the walk to the vestry."

"You'll have it when I bring it."

"Mrs. Poll—"

"When I bring it." Maren put her hand flat on the door and leaned. "Go home, Mr. Skerry. You're standing in six inches of the sea and your good coat is ruined."

He went, taking the water with him in long strides, and he looked back twice.

Maren shut the door and stood a moment with her forehead against the wood. Then she went to her bag, took out the leaf of paper with the birth on it in her own small hand — weight, time, the cord, the caul, all of it — folded it twice, and put it inside the lining of her bag where the seam had come away. The parish copy would get her neat versions. The neat versions would not have a grey hood in them.

At the vestry a week later, the water gone down and the mud scraped off the flagstones, she asked for the returns as she always did, to check her own cases against the register. Skerry gave her the book and stood too close while she read it.

Nine births she had attended in three years. Seven in the book. She turned a page back, as if she were uncertain, and did the sum again while he breathed on her shoulder.

"I'll want the older volumes," she said. "For the little one's pedigree. Her grandmother's set on a claim to the Fenwick land."

Skerry hesitated. Then he reached up to the shelf and put them down in front of her, four of them, their spines soft with damp, and went to see about the boiler.

Maren opened the oldest at the first page and worked backwards with her pencil making small marks in the margin where the arithmetic did not agree, and the arithmetic did not agree more often than a parish could explain by carelessness. Out in the channel the bell buoy was ringing and ringing, though there was no wind that afternoon worth the name.

## 3. Nine Dark Minutes

Thirty years before anyone thought to index it, the light at Carrow Point burned paraffin and turned on a clockwork that had to be wound every four hours, and Liss wound it at dusk with her sleeves rolled to the elbow because the lamp room was hot as a bakehouse. That spring the herring came thin, and the ebb went out past the last pencilled line of the chart, and she stood at the gallery rail with her tea going cold, watching water leave ground that had no business being ground.

The beam swung out over the shoal on its long arm and came back, and swung out again. Twelve seconds. She had never minded the rhythm before; it was the pulse you slept inside of.

On the third sweep it showed her a street.

Not a wreck’s ribs, broken and scattered. A grid — walls running parallel, a lane meeting another lane at a clean right angle, a square with a stub in the middle where a cross might have stood, and beyond it the square stump of a tower with the sea draining out of its arches in ropes. Chimneys. She counted forty before the light came round again and took them away.

Her tea went over the rail. Liss wiped her palm on her skirt and went down the spiral for the bearing compass, and by the time she got back the water was a hand’s breadth shallower and the street was longer, opening west as though a door had been tried.

Twelve seconds of village. Twelve seconds of nothing.

She wanted it in one piece. That was the whole of her thinking, and she has never once been able to make it sound like anything better than that. Down in the lamp room she turned the clockwork off its hook, lifted the mantle’s guard, and pinched out the wick between wet fingers, then the second, and the great lens went from white to a red coal to a black mouth, and the night came in close.

Nine minutes. She timed it by the deck watch, crouched at the gallery rail with the compass in her fist and her heart going like a gull caught in a chimney.

The grid lay open below her, and the sea had let go of it entirely, and there was a sound coming up off it — not voices, nothing so kind. A shutter banging, once, and then again a long while after, with the patience of a thing that had been banging for years. A door pulling on a hinge and not shutting. Small human noises in a place with no humans in it, and every one of them unhurried.

At six minutes she understood she was not alone at the rail, though the gallery was empty, and that whatever stood beside her was waiting, politely, for her to be finished looking.

She relit the lamp at nine minutes and fourteen seconds.

Water was already back over the chimney pots, and by the time the light had warmed, the shoal was a shoal and nothing else, and the buoy was ringing a note it had not been ringing before.

In the watch room, Liss opened the keeper’s log, ruled her columns, and wrote it down — the ebb, the bearing, the grid, the nine minutes, the shutter. Not the shape at her shoulder; a keeper’s log will not carry that and stay a log. Her hand was very steady, which she noticed and did not like.

Mr. Quist came out with the oil in the middle of the forenoon, his boy Tam perched on the cans in the tender with a rope in both fists and his mouth open at the height of things. Quist climbed the steps slowly, a man who had gone up them a thousand times, and set the first can down inside the door and stood there with his hat in his hand.

“You put the light out.”

“Nine minutes. Fouled wick.”

“Fouled wick.” He turned the log round on the desk with one finger, the way you turn a plate you do not intend to eat from. “You wrote me an essay, Liss.”

“It was a long nine minutes.”

Quist looked out at the water for a while. Below, the boy began to sing something tuneless to the cans.

“My grandfather had a rhyme about that shoal,” he said. “So did his. It’s not that people don’t know. It’s that knowing it out loud costs.”

“Costs what?”

“Ask the last man who kept this light.” He tapped the log. “Ask him in the old books, if they’re still in the press.”

Liss went past him to the corner cupboard, and the ledgers were there, soft-spined with damp, four of them, and she pulled out the oldest and carried it to the window. Quist did not stop her. He watched her the way you watch a person walk onto thin ice you have warned them about.

The entries for her date, thirty years prior, ran in a careful hand: light tended, wind nor’-west, nine minutes dark, wick fouled.

“That’s my line,” she said.

“No.”

“It’s the same words.”

“It’s the same night,” Quist said. “It comes round. And every keeper that’s written it down has stayed on this coast till they were carried off it, and I’d sooner not have that on my quarterly returns.” He put his hat on. “There’s a relief posting at the Dornoch light. I’ll sign the paper this week and no harm to your record.”

“No,” Liss said.

“Think on it. A week.”

“I’ve thought.” She held the old ledger open on her forearm so the page caught the light. “I’ll be wanting these in the lamp room. All four.”

Quist came back up the last two steps and stood over her, and he was not an unkind man, and his voice had gone down to the register he used for weather. “Lass. There’s men in this town would burn a book to keep it from being read twice.”

“Then they should see how fast I write,” Liss said, and turned the ledger round so he could read the thirty-year-old fouled wick in his grandfather’s time, and the one before that, and the one before that, each in a different hand and the same words, and under the last of them a name in iron-gall that she read twice before she would believe it was not her own.

## 4. The Ledger Under Glass

The seal was red wax over a paper band stamped with the parish crest, and it had gone ten years unbroken on a shelf in the diocesan strongroom. Iris broke it at a quarter to nine, in the reading room's particular cold, with a microspatula and the sensation of picking a lock she had permission to pick.

The wax cracked. The band came away whole, and she laid it flat on the blotter, because a broken seal is still evidence.

Inside was a midwife's book: a hundred and twelve leaves ruled for births, sixty of them filled by Maren Vasey — who had gone out of the parish in the flood year and had not been satisfactorily accounted for since — in a hand that leaned forward like a person walking into weather. Name of mother. Parish. Hour. Presentation. Whether a caul, and whether it came away whole, and whether the grandmother had asked to see it before it was laid in the basin.

Leaf sixty-one was not a birth.

A tide table had been tipped in along the gutter with flour paste gone the colour of weak tea. Iris turned the leaf, and her fingers went stupid with a cold that had nothing to do with the room.

The same grid. Bellamy Shoal, soundings in fathoms, the stipple fanning east into a shape she had spent two days arguing with a scanner about. Nine parallel lines. A cross lane. At the end of the lane a square that could be a church, or a market cross, or a well.

Maren had drawn it in pencil beneath the printed soundings, so faint the scanner would have eaten it and handed back a clean white page.

Behind her the door let in wet rope and diesel.

"You're early," Rowan said.

"You're earlier." She didn't turn. His jacket made a sound against the chair back. "There's an Admiralty chart on that table with pencil all over it."

"There's a photocopy of an Admiralty chart on that table."

"That's not better."

He sat down opposite, which was one of the things about him — he never came round to your side of a table to see what you were seeing. He waited to be handed it.

She handed it.

Soundings in grey. A shoal drawn where a shoal had been surveyed in 1971, and under it, in pencil, a second contour, walked with a lead line and written down by hand, sounding by sounding, in the rain. The village front was on the same sheet, the lane running up from the slip.

The heating clicked off. He read for a long time.

"Where's the raw data?" she said. "Not what you sent me. The raw."

"Unsent."

"Unsanctioned."

"Yes."

She turned the chart round and read the dates penciled in the corner margin. "February. That's a season you've been out there with a lead line and no warrant."

"The survey office wrote back in January. The shoal was stable, my concern was noted." Rowan flattened one corner with a thumb. "It stands a metre and a half prouder than that chart says. It has stood all winter. It's not a sandbank that moves the way a sandbank moves. It moves like something being uncovered."

"Moves how?"

"Deliberately."

The word sat on the table between them. Iris put her palm flat on the ledger and turned it, so the tipped-in page lay alongside his pencil contour, and watched him find it. The nine lines. The lane. The square.

He went still. Not shocked. Worse than shocked. Recognised.

"You've seen this shape," she said.

"On the water. At the ebb, in March, before the light went." His voice had dropped into the register men use for their own superstitions. "I thought I was tired. I thought it was the tide making a pattern in the sand the way it does."

"And you put pencil on a chart and told nobody."

"I told the survey office." A pause. "I'm telling you."

Iris turned to the last birth entry in Maren's hand. A boy, born in the flood year, in a lane the water came up; the mother's name inked and then gone over so hard the paper had furred. In the margin, small, upright, in a different hand entirely: *one uncounted.*

Below it, on the tipped-in table, Maren had circled the spring tide with a nail. Beside the circle, in the same upright hand, a date.

"Read that," Iris said.

Rowan read it. Then he took a diary out of his jacket and checked it twice, the way a man checks a bridge he doesn't trust.

"That's the next spring," he said. "Eleven days."

"She knew the date ten years ago. She knew the hour." Iris closed the ledger and did not put it back in its wrapper. "My accession number goes on your survey. Second observer. It's not sanctioned and it isn't going to be, and if the office wants to hang someone it can hang an archivist with a pension." She reached across and took the pencil out of his hand before he could offer it. "Eleven days. Thursday — your lead line, my camera, and you show me where the lane runs."

Rowan looked at the pencil in her fist. Something in his face came loose.

"You'll want boots," he said.

## 5. The Borrowed Name

Ten years before the archive, the light at Carrow Point ran off the mains, and the Board had taken the clockwork away in a van, and Liss kept the paraffin mantles in a tea chest under the stairs like a woman expecting the electric to blow over.

She answered the door with flour on her hands and did not offer to shake.

"You'll be the midwife," she said. "You've got the satchel."

"I've got a chart." Maren stepped over the iron nails set head-down in the threshold. "Two days old. A girl, seven pound four, caul over the face, and the tide in the lane before I'd cut the cord."

"And?"

"And the parish clerk was on the step before the water was off the flags. He had a paper written out ready and the session's seal in his pocket, and he wanted the return signed in his presence."

Liss wiped her hands on the roller towel, took her time about it. "Did you give it him?"

"I told him I'd left it in the bag and the bag was in the water." Maren set the satchel on the table, between the teapot and a barometer that hung crooked. "He's got a boy watching my door."

Flour went into the bin. The kettle went on. Liss set out two cups, one of them chipped at the rim, and her hand was steady as a bridge.

"You know the entry," Maren said. "Nineteen in the log. Nine minutes the lamp was out and the beam came back on the street."

"Who told you that?"

"The log's not sealed to the public. It's in the session room with the spine out."

"Then you'll have read what I wrote in the margin and no more." Liss poured. "There's men in this town who'd pay to hear me say the word Halloway, and I'd not give them the pleasure."

"I'm not paying."

"No," Liss agreed. "You're not."

Maren took the chart out and turned it round and pushed it across the table. The name was at the top in her own hand, blue ink, careful capitals: the hour, the weight, the caul noted and its disposition, and the mother's maiden name where the clerk had wanted the father's.

Liss read it twice. Then she put her finger on the name and left it there.

"That's a good name," she said. "That's a name with a whole family behind it. Grandmother on the hill, uncle on the boats. It'll hold."

"Hold what?"

"The counting." Liss took her finger back. "It takes a name every generation. Not a life—that's what the chapel preaches and the chapel's wrong. A name. It takes it and it keeps it, and the one it's taken from goes on walking about with a face everybody knows and no handle to hold them by. My mother's cousin was taken. I couldn't tell you what she was called. I'd know her in the street tomorrow."

The tea went cold on the table. Outside, the fog signal started its practice run, four seconds on, and stopped.

"The child's already counted," Liss said. "Born under the caul, it came up wearing a hood, and the hood's the village's mark. That's why the clerk had the paper written. Book's how it's done. Signed, sealed, filed, and the count closes."

"Then I'll not file it."

"It doesn't need the book. The book's a courtesy."

Maren put her hands flat on the table. "Come with me to the session. Bring the log. Stand up in front of them and say what you saw, and I'll have the chart, and between us—"

"No."

"Why not?"

Liss went to the window. The glass was old and warped and the sea in it stood higher than the sea is.

"I've not crossed the bridge in twenty years," she said. "Not for my sister's funeral. Not for the Board man when he came about the clockwork and I told him through the letterbox. I put my name in that log and it kept me the way a nail keeps a hinge." She turned round. "The harbor master's boy swims out to the shoal in his sleep. I've hauled him back twice. He's never once thanked me, because he doesn't remember, and I can't tell him. That's the work. Standing on the point with my hand on the switch. You take the log to the session and they'll want me to swear to it, and I'll swear to it here or nowhere."

"Then it's a pretty cage you've made."

"It's a lighthouse," Liss said. "There's a difference, and it's nine minutes long."

Maren put the chart back in the satchel. Her fingers were not steady. She was aware of the clock on the wall, and the kettle ticking as it cooled, and the smell of paraffin that would not leave the walls.

"Will you want more tea?" Liss said.

"No." Maren stood. "Will you show me the lamp before I go? I've never seen it lit in daylight."

Liss took her up. The stair was iron and narrow. The tower smelled of brass polish and salt. The lamp stood in its bed of rollers with the mantles grey and cold inside the glass, and the whole town lay out under it, small enough to put in a pocket. Maren looked at the town. Liss looked at the horizon.

Down in the kitchen again, Maren put the chart away and put something else in beside it. The log was on the shelf by the door in an oilcloth cover, its brass corners worn round as pennies, and it went into the satchel easy, the way a thing goes when it has been waiting to be carried.

Liss watched her do it and said nothing until the door was open.

"You'll bring it back," she said. Not a question.

Maren drove as far as the causeway and stopped where the tide was coming over the stones in a thin brown sheet. The log's back pages were a list, names in six hands, one to a generation, going back past the poor law to a line of ink gone brown as the cover. The bottom line had a date on it and no name after, and the date was a fortnight off, and the ink there was not Liss's and not any of the six.

She got out of the car with the log under her arm and the wind took the pages and she held them down with the flat of her hand, and stood there while the water came over the causeway and cut the road behind her. Then she got back in and drove to the lane, where the mother's door stood open and the pram was on the step.

"I'll need to write her down wrong," Maren said, from the gate.

## 6. The Bell Buoy

Thirty years before the archive got its hands on anything, the bell buoy off Bellamy Shoal rang to the swell and nothing else, and Liss could hear it from the gallery with her back to the wind. Tonight it stopped.

Not stopped. The tongue swung and found no throat to strike, and into that gap came a note from under the water, lower than the buoy could sound, one syllable drawn out long and flat like a word held at the end of a breath. Liss counted it. A keeper counts — that was the whole of the job, four hours and four hours and the sea in between — and by the time she caught herself she was at eleven.

Twelve. Thirteen.

She took the stair down with her palm flat to the iron, because the iron was cold and did not move, and fetched the key off its nail and slung the cord round her neck where the key lay against her breastbone, warm, the way it always did. Habit, that. She had never once gone out a door without it.

The ebb had bared the causeway and the weed hung off the stones like wet hair. Down past the slip the sand was firm enough to take her boots, and she followed the waterline out because the sound was out there, and the sound was patient.

A boy stood in it to the knee.

Colm Hask, sixteen, in his nightshirt, no boots on him, walking the way a man walks down a corridor he knows — steady, unhurried, one foot placed and then the other. The water was at his thighs and the shoal was a long grey band out beyond him and getting longer.

"Colm."

Nothing. Liss went in after him and the cold came up her legs like a hand closing.

"Colm Hask." She got a fistful of the nightshirt at his shoulder. He kept going and took her with him a step. "Your father's lantern is on the point and he'll have my lamp for this."

"Fifteen," the boy said.

His eyes were open. They were wet and open and fixed on the shoal and he had a bit of weed in his hair like a woman's comb.

"Sixteen."

"Look at me." Liss planted her heels and turned him. Salt water came over her boots and up her thighs. "Colm. What are you counting?"

"Seventeen." He came round easily enough, but he leaned toward the shoal the whole time, his weight going past her the way a door leans on a hinge. "Same as you."

She got him moving shoreward with her arm across his chest, walking backwards, walking crabwise, and the water pulled at her legs and pulled back and pulled again and somewhere in the middle of it the cord on her neck gave at the knot and slid. The key went down the front of her and into the ebb and she felt it with the sole of her boot, one hard second of it, and then the water had it and the water wanted it more than she did.

Eighteen.

"Hask," she said, to the dark, to the shore, to whichever of the family might be awake. "Hask, your boy's out here."

A lantern came bobbing down the slip and a voice came with it, thin with fear and then not thin at all. Hask waded in with his boots on and his coat over his arm and took hold of his son, and the two of them got Colm onto the shingle where he sat and was sick and shivered and came back into his own face.

"What in God's name," Hask said.

"Ask him."

"Nothing." Colm wiped his mouth on the wet shoulder of the nightshirt. "I was asleep."

"You were out to the shoal."

"I was asleep," he said, and then, quieter, "there was a bell."

Hask's lantern swung round on his own hand as he turned. The buoy showed itself on the swell and went under and showed again, dumb as a stone, and the thing about the buoy was not the sound. The thing about it was the shape of the silence hanging off it.

"The bell's got no clapper," Hask said. "The Navy took it in the war for the metal. That buoy has been dumb since before this boy could read."

"I know what it hasn't got," Liss said.

"Then you know what you heard."

She looked at her hands. They were wet and shaking and not from the cold, and her feet hurt, and she looked down at her feet and there was sand on them, sand up the ankles, sand between the toes, and she had walked out of the tower in her boots.

"Colm," she said. "How long was I standing out there before you came?"

The boy thought about it, which was worse than any answer he could have given. "You were at nine," he said. "You got to fourteen when I came down. I heard you say them."

Liss took the lantern out of Hask's grip because she wanted her hands to be doing something.

"Nineteen was when it stopped," Colm said.

The lantern threw their shadows toward the water.

"It can count," Liss said, and neither man asked her what she meant by it, and out on the tide the buoy found its tongue again and rang the ordinary way, once, like a thing clearing its throat.

## 7. The Drowned Street

Eleven days after the crate of tide logs arrived, Iris stood on the dry bottom of Bellamy Shoal with mud to the heel of her boot and the drowned village of Halloway laid out ahead of her like a plan drawing somebody had folded badly.

Rowan had killed the outboard a hundred yards short and rowed the rest, because she'd asked, and because a man with a survey licence and no permit develops a feel for silence.

"Forty-one minutes," he said. "Water comes back across that road there, and we'll be swimming with the tripod."

"Then stop talking and walk."

Mud gave under them in soft, obscene sucks. The street ran north-north-east, slate gone, walls standing to the eaves, and every third cottage had its door sealed with expanding foam and a steel band riveted across the jamb. Iris counted the numbers cut into the lintels. Nine, eleven, twelve. There was no thirteen. She took out her phone and photographed the gap anyway, then the numbering on the far side, because a missing thirteen was either a superstition or a demolition, and both were evidence.

"Who sealed these?" she said.

"County did. Back in the nineties, I think. Before my time on the books."

"Because nothing says coastal management like a thousand cans of foam."

"Because things wash in." Rowan set the tripod down, checked the level, and did not look at her. "That's the official line. Things wash in."

The ebb made a long, ticking sound against the wall ends. Somewhere out beyond the last cottage, gulls fought over something with the patience of a court case.

She found the first of it at eleven — a cottage with its banding rusted to lace and the foam picked out in chunks, a hole the size of a fist at the hinge side. Iris put her eye to it. Silt, a scatter of bladderwrack, the ribs of a chair. And a boot. Right foot. Oiled leather gone hard and translucent as a scute, laces cut rather than untied, and small — a four, a small five at most.

"Rowan."

He came, crouched, and was quiet a long moment.

"Old," he said. "That's old. That's decades in salt."

"Is it." She straightened. "Cut the strap."

Inside, the air was thick and cold and tasted of pennies. Her torch swung across walls that had once been papered; a scrap of it hung at shoulder height, roses gone brown as tea. The chair had a coat over the back of it, oilskin, stiff. Beneath the chair the silt had been disturbed. Iris crouched, put her hand flat over the mark, and felt the small, unmistakable approximation of a bare human foot.

Not deep. The water table hadn't been up long enough to fill it. Still damp at the toes.

"How fresh," Rowan said. He hadn't raised his voice.

"Hours. Maybe less than that."

"Tide cleared this at four this morning and came back at ten past eleven. There's a window in there nobody can stand in."

"There's a window in there of three hours," Iris said. "I've read the tables too."

He didn't answer straight away. Then: "People come out here. Kids. It's a—"

"Kids take their boots off to walk through a sealed cottage in seven inches of silt? Kids cut a rivet? Look at the foam, Rowan. It's been picked out from the outside with something thin. A screwdriver. A knife." She turned a slow circle with the light. "How many doors on this street?"

"Fourteen standing."

"Nine are open. I counted walking down. Nine open, five sealed, and I'll wager you the boat we came in that all five are in worse repair than this one." The light caught the coat, the chair, the small print of a foot. "Someone's been opening more than they've been sealing."

Rowan took his cap off and put it back on. "That's a maintenance log problem, Iris. That's not—"

"Say the rest of it."

"That's not a village coming up on its own." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I've surveyed this shoal for six years. Six. Every chart I've filed says it bares at spring low and drains dry for the period it drains dry and then it's gone. What I have never done is wonder whether the thing that comes up is the same thing that went down." A pause. "You can hear it on the hydrophone. Did you know that? There's a frequency at slack water that isn't a bell and isn't a boat and doesn't move."

The ebb ticked. The gulls stopped.

"You've heard it," Iris said.

"I've turned the gain down on it. Every time."

The mud under the floor made a sound like a lip pulled back from a tooth. Water, working in somewhere, finding its level.

Iris went down on one knee and put the boot in her bag. Then she took out her phone, photographed the footprint from three angles with the scale rule, and wrote in her field notebook the hour, the door number, the condition of the banding, the cut laces, the damp at the toes. Fifteen words. Nothing that would raise a question in a database.

Rowan watched her do it. "You're not going to file that."

"I'm going to file exactly what I just wrote, which is true." She stood. "And then I'm going to stop pretending I'm charting a shoal, because a shoal does not get up in the night and go and get a screwdriver." Brine was soaking through at the street's edge, thin as a knife blade, silver on the mud. "Whoever cut that rivet will be back before the next spring low. Because you don't keep a thing open like this for one tide. You do it every tide. You do it for years."

"And if they come back and it isn't just a screwdriver next time?"

"Then they'll be the first person on this coast who isn't surprised to find me standing here." She hiked the bag onto her shoulder. "Move the tripod. I want the north end before the water does."

## 8. What the Tide Kept

Ten years before the archive had climate control, the road out to Carrow Point was one lane of broken tarmac with a hedge on either side, and Maren drove it at two in the morning with the infant in a laundry basket on the passenger seat, wedged flat under her coat. Liss had the door open before the engine stopped ticking.

"Bring her in out of the wind."

"She isn't staying." Maren kept the basket against her hip and her boots on the step. "I've read your log cover to cover. There's a hand in the back of it that isn't yours, and the hand keeps a count."

"Every keeper keeps a count. It's called a tide table."

"Twenty-nine. Thirty-one. Twenty-nine." Wind came up the cliff and took the tail of her words. "It goes up by one every generation, and every time it goes up there's a caul born in this parish inside the month. I've attended three of them. I've read four more in the vestry cupboard."

Liss looked at the basket, then away, up at the light on the point turning its slow arm out over the water.

"You want the words," she said. "There aren't any. There's a way of holding the lamp and a way of not holding it, and you're ten years past the light."

"Then tell me what it takes."

"A name. Given freely, spoken aloud, on the ebb, and not the one it's owed." Liss's mouth worked. "That's the whole of it. That's why nobody says it. Saying it is halfway to doing it."

"Then I'll do it. I'll give it something else to count."

"It doesn't take what you choose," Liss said. "It takes what's nearest. Ask me how I know that."

Maren was already backing down the steps. "You could come. You know the ground."

"I keep the light. I don't go down there." The old woman stood in the doorway with her cardigan pulled tight, and the light above her swung round, and swung round. "Maren. Whatever it takes off you, don't stand there arguing with it. Come back up the shingle when the water touches your heel and don't look behind you for the sound of your own name."

The shoal lay out under a half moon with the ebb still running off it, water creasing away in long shivers toward the channel. Mud to the ankle in the low places. A smell coming up out of it like struck matches and wet sacks. Maren had the basket in one hand and Liss's log buttoned inside her coat, and she walked the ridge of harder sand the way she'd walk a corridor in the dark, by touch, until the street opened out ahead of her — a grid of walls a foot high, doorways, a lane running down to where a harbour had been.

No bell. Thirty years of it and the bell buoy at the mouth hung silent on a flat sea, and the silence was worse, because silence meant listening.

She stopped in the middle of the street and set the basket down on a doorstep and lifted the baby out, and the child lay in her arms with its eyes open and its hands loose, not asleep.

"My name is Maren," she said. "Maren Prosser, of the parish of Carrow. I'm giving you that. It's mine, and I'm giving it freely, and you can put it in your book and leave the child alone."

The gutters between the walls began to run. Not seaward. Uphill, thin threads of water climbing the tilt of the street toward the church end, and where they went a sound went with them, a low drawing sound, as if something under the mud had taken a breath and was holding it.

"Take it," Maren said. "Take the name. Go down."

Then she said the child's name. She said it loud and clear and she said it twice, the way she said it in the cottage when she got up in the night, and she heard her own voice go out across the street and go out and go out and not come back, and there was a moment when the sound of it was still in her mouth and the next moment when her mouth was open and empty and there was nothing in her throat at all.

She tried to say it again. Her jaw ached with the shape of it. No sound. Nothing came up past her collarbone but air.

The threads in the gutters stopped climbing. The mud settled. Whatever had been holding its breath let it go, and the smell of struck matches went off on the wind, and the ebb went on creasing away toward the channel as if nothing whatever had happened.

When the water touched her heel she was still standing there.

Back at the van, she got the baby into the basket with shaking hands and had to bite the strap to hold it steady. She tried her own name in her head and it was there, whole, Maren, Maren — she could think it fine. She tried the child's name and reached a place where it had been and found the surface of it smooth, like a sheet pulled flat. She went through the alphabet. She went through the naming book at the back of her mind, every Saints' day, every grandmother in the parish. Nothing caught.

The ledger was in the van where she'd left it, under the seat, and she hauled it out and opened it in the courtesy light to the page she'd written the night of the birth. The line was ruled and inked and there were letters in it.

The letters meant nothing. Her eyes went over them the way a hand goes over a hem and finds no thread. She knew the word had been a name. She could see where it stopped.

Maren sat in the driver's seat with the basket beside her and the log open on her knee for a long time, and out on the point the light went round and round with nobody in it but an old woman who would not leave the coast.

Then she found the pencil in the door pocket and turned Liss's log to the blank endpapers and began, in block capitals, to make a list: the four from the vestry, the three she'd attended, the one in the basket beside her who could not be entered in any book under any word at all. She numbered them. Beside each number she wrote a dash and left a space.

Up at the top of the page she wrote what she had worked out on the shoal: that you can't give a name you've been told not to give, and you can't hide a child on this coast, and the only way to keep one out of the count is to put a different name in the way of it — a name with nothing behind it, no cry in the night, no mother.

The churchyard at Carrow had eleven children in it who had died before they were named properly, and every one of them was past being called for.

## 9. The Third Light

Thirty years before the archive had a name for it, the sea over Bellamy Shoal lay flat as poured lead, and Liss rowed into it with a can of paraffin wedged between her boots. Halloway came up out of the dark the way a molar comes out of a gum — slow, and with the roots still in.

Chimneys first, then the roofs, tarred and shining. Sound carried strangely across that flat: her oars knocked, and knocked again, off nothing.

The keel touched mud. She shipped the oars, stepped out, and the shoal took her boot to the ankle and held it while she pulled.

The street ran away from her, cobbled and streaming. A hand-cart stood in it with the shafts down, as though the carter had gone in for his tea. Washing hung on a line and did not move. Somewhere a door worked gently on its hinges, in and out, the way a mouth does.

"Keep to the crown," a voice said. "Gutters are deeper than they look."

The harbour master sat on a mounting block with his hat on his knee. His boots were dry.

"You knew."

"I've been coming since Tuesday." He turned the hat a quarter turn and back. "There's a smell to it. Like a struck match."

"Why didn't you say so at the office?"

"Because you'd have come out here. And now you have."

She stood in the road with the paraffin can swinging. "There's a whole town under this water. Roofs. A cart. Someone's washing."

"There is."

"And the Board keeps a light on a point with no harbour."

"Now you've got it." He put his hat on. "The village paid for that light. Eighteen sixty-three, the herring went, and then the fever took the lower road, and the priest buried nine in a fortnight. So we voted. Moved the church up to Carrow, moved the graves that would come, and asked the Board for a light on the point and no harbour to go with it. The Board thought we were mad and took the money."

"A lamp keeps a village down the way a thumb keeps a cork in."

"It does not," said Liss.

"It does when it's lit right." He stood and walked ahead of her, gesturing at the roofs as if at sheep. "You've been keeper eleven years and never asked why the occulting's set at three and a dark. No chart on this coast carries a third light. Ships count two, steer by the second, and never count the third."

"They count the third."

"They count what the book tells them to count. And the book doesn't tell them."

She looked down the drowned street. At the far end, where the water still stood deep, something moved that was not the wind and was not the door.

"Why now?" she said. "Thirty years it's been quiet."

"Because nine minutes is nine minutes." His voice had no blame in it, which was worse. "The water counts them."

The lamp room hasp was cheap brass and gave to a marlinspike in four blows, because her own key lay somewhere in the mud of the shoal where she had dropped it three weeks ago, running after a boy who did not remember her in the morning. Inside, the paraffin mantles hung dead in their gallery, and the clockwork stood stopped with its vanes shut like a fist.

Taped inside the door was a card, printed and framed in tin, older than the current lamp. *Occulting. Three white, one dark. No variation.* Board lettering, and beneath it, in another hand altogether, pencilled and faded: *the dark is the weight.*

She filled the reservoir and pumped it up. Trimmed the wick. The first match guttered and died. The second took, and the mantle bloomed white, and the whole tower came awake with a soft roar.

Then she went to the clockwork and unscrewed the shutters, and took out one of the vanes — a strip of blackened tin that made the dark — and dropped it down the stairwell, where it rang and rang and stopped.

"Three white," said the harbour master from the doorway, out of breath, one hand on the jamb. "Three white and now no dark."

"A dark that comes when the clockwork says isn't a weight. It's a habit." She set the train and let it go. The beam swung out over the roofs, over the cart, over the washing, and the glare of it went flat and orange across the water.

Below them the street began, without hurry, to go under.

He was in the watch room when she came down. On the table lay a brass plate, unpolished, its four screws in a row beside it, and behind him the keepers' board, every name from 1863 to the last, and the dates beneath the names, and each date sat exactly thirty years after the one before, the way fence posts sit.

"Thirty years," she said.

"Thirty years." His finger found the last date. "It wears out. It's a hand on a wick, and a hand doesn't last."

"You've been carrying that plate in your coat."

"Three weeks now." He pushed it across the table. "I'd have cut it myself if I could. It won't take my hand."

The beam went round. On the third swing the water had reached the mounting block where he had sat with his hat on his knee, and the cart was up to its axle, and the door on the drowned street had stopped working.

Liss took the marlinspike from her belt and drew the plate to her and cut, letter by letter, L. TREVOSE. It went through the brass easily, the way it goes through a good year.

"Put the date," he said.

"I know to put the date."

"Not for the Board." The harbour master looked out at the turning light. "For the next one. They'll want to know how long it held."

She set the point and struck the 3, and struck a second time, and the water came over the crown of the road behind him without any sound at all.

## 10. Low Water

Lowest water came at twelve minutes past four, and by twenty to, Iris had Rowan's keys turning in the harbour office lock and the salt index open on the counter under the reading lamp. She had signed for the crate eleven days ago; tonight she meant to read what was in it out loud, on channel six, where every boat in the bay kept a watch.

The radiator ticked. Somewhere below the window, a halyard worked at its mast in a wind too small to name.

"You'll want to put that down."

The harbour master stood in the doorway with his coat half-zipped and his boots unlaced, and he did not look surprised so much as tired, the way a man looks at weather he has already been out in.

"Channel six is ship-to-ship," he said. "It's not a pulpit."

"It's the channel the lifeboat monitors, and the dredger, and the two boats still fishing out of this harbour." Iris turned the page. "That's most of the town, and the rest of them will hear it from the ones who are."

"You've been at the strongroom."

"I've been at the ledger. It's been ten years under a seal and no one has opened it, and in it a midwife copied the tide tables off Bellamy Shoal and ruled a grid over them in pencil. It matches the index in my crate. Line for line. Forty years apart, two sets of hands, the same nine days of the month."

He came in and shut the door behind him, which was worse than leaving it open.

"And what is it you think you're doing, then?"

"Low water at twelve past," she said. "Spring tide. It'll bare as far as the mounting block and hold for the better part of an hour. If we're all standing on the quay and there's a street out there, nobody in this town gets to call it a shoal again."

"You don't know what you're asking for."

"I know what I'm not being told. Nobody will say the name of it out loud, and yet the parish clerk has a drawer of returns that don't add up and every boat in the bay gives that mark a mile of water." She lifted the handset. "So say it with me or don't, but I'm about to be very loud."

He watched her thumb the key. His jaw moved once, as though he were counting something behind his teeth, and then he pulled out the chair on the other side of the counter and sat, and that was the closest thing to permission a harbour master ever gave.

"Nine days," he said. "It's nine days it stays up, after."

"You've seen it."

"My father saw it. He came home with a brass plate he'd had made for the lamp and would not say why." He nodded at the handset. "Go on, then. Before I remember my wage."

She read the index slowly, the way she would read it to a room of students who had paid to be there — the gauge, the hour, the fall in feet, the anomalies named and dated, and at the end the pencil grid and the note in a woman's hand that the seal had kept ten years: *nine dark minutes, and the street.*

By the second page, a light had come on over the chandlery. By the third, an engine turned over in the lane behind the pub.

They came down to the quay in coats over pyjamas, and they did not talk much, and they stopped at the top of the slip and stayed there.

The water went out further than any of them had seen it. It went out past the red can, past the mooring rings, past the black weed line that marked the ordinary end of things, and kept going with a long sucking sigh until there was mud to the horizon and a road in it. A street. Walls to knee height and walls to shoulder height; a gap where a shopfront had been; a lane going down between cottages with the tide still draining out of it, brown and quick.

Somebody's dog barked twice and then would not be moved past the mounting block.

"Christ," a woman said. "There's a lamp burning."

There was. Far down the new street, small and yellow and steady, a light not quite of the size a lamp should be at that distance.

Rowan's dinghy was not on the trailer any more. Iris had checked it at twenty past and the trailer had been empty and she had thought — she had thought he had taken it in before the low, the way he took everything in, methodically, out of the weather.

"Where's the surveyor?" she said.

The harbour master looked out at the street, and his face did a thing she had seen on men at the exact moment they understood the water was coming back wrong.

"Miss," he said. "Your tide table's out."

It was. Low water had come at twelve past; it was fifteen minutes after, and the mud at the foot of the slip was shining wet again, and the sound of it was not a gentle sound. Up the drowned lane, water ran backwards in the gutters, running uphill toward the village like something being poured.

He was in the street. He was in the street with his staff and his tape, halfway down, and he had stopped moving, and he was standing at a door in a wall that had no house behind it. His lamp was still on its tripod. He was unzipping his jacket. He was doing it in the slow, careful, obedient way of a man who has been asked to come in out of the rain.

The harbour master was already off the slip. "Get the pump engine," he shouted, and then, louder, to the whole quay: "Get the pump, get the lamps, we'll cut the lane at the neck and let it fill. Break the street and it goes down. It goes down the way it went down before."

Two men went for the tractor. A third had a spade off the wall.

Iris got in front of the spade.

"You cut that lane and he's behind the cut," she said. Her voice was very steady and she did not recognise it. "You put a wall of mud up and you shut him in it."

"You leave it open, it takes the quay next, and then the harbour road, and then my moorings."

"Yes."

"That's my harbour."

"I know." She had the handset still in her hand. It had gone dead at some point and she did not remember dropping the key. "But everyone on this quay is looking at a man who is walking into that street, and I am telling you nobody here will be able to say afterwards that they saw him and let the mud have him. Not to save a mooring. Not to save the wall."

The harbour master's hand was on the tractor's lever, and he did not move it, and he did not take it off.

Water came over the slip. It came over the mounting block, unhurried, and the crowd went back up the quay with their lamps, and the yellow light down the drowned street kept burning, and Rowan's jacket came off his shoulders into the water and lay flat on it like something laid down carefully.

She walked into the shallows to her knees. The cold went through her like a nail and she kept her eyes on his back, and he did not turn round, and his hand found the doorframe of a house that had been under the sea since before her grandmother, and he went in.

The door stayed open. The tide ran into it, and the lamp on the tripod leaned, and went out, and then did not.

Under the water, the light came on again, small and yellow, and turned down the drowned lane toward the church.

## 11. The Borrowed Ledger

Ten years before the archive had a borrower’s card with her handwriting on it, the parish strongroom at Carrow was a converted boiler room under the diocesan office, and Maren came down into it at twenty past low water with a ledger bound in oilcloth held against her ribs. Wet prints followed her across the concrete. Out in the yard the culvert had turned and was draining the street back into the sea, which meant the tide was low enough to leave the quay standing dry for anyone who wanted to walk out on it.

A boy in a cardigan sat by the door with a paperback open on his knee and a calor gas heater ticking at his feet.

“Strongroom shuts at five,” he said, not looking up.

She stood in front of him until he did.

“Christ.” He put a finger in the book. “You’re wet through.”

Out came the pad from her coat. RETURNING A PARISH REGISTER.

He read it, mouthing the words. “There’s a box by the door. Anything goes in the box, I log it in the morning. That’s the system.”

IT SHOULD BE ON THE SHELF TONIGHT.

“Tonight.” He laughed, then looked at her properly and stopped laughing. “You’re the midwife. The one from the lane.”

Maren nodded once.

“Water’s right out,” he said, giving up on the paperback altogether. “They’re walking the old road. My mother won’t have it. She says her uncle went down there the last time it showed and came back with his boots on the wrong feet.” He rubbed at the heater’s grille with his thumb. “Your parish clerk was in Tuesday, anyhow. Father Cussen. Wanted a birth book. He had a list in his coat and a face on him like a wet week.”

The pad came up again. DID YOU GIVE HIM ONE?

“There’s no birth books here. Deeds and tithes and the bishop’s coal bills.” He jerked his chin at the shelves. “You want to put that somewhere, put it somewhere. I’m not signing for it.”

She unwrapped the oilcloth on the counter. Deed boxes went back to 1911 along the far wall, spines numbered in white ink, and the air smelled of cold iron and dust. An open drawer held the ephemera of the place: brass paperclips, a stick of sealing wax, a press with the parish crest cut into its face, and a bundle of slips spiked on a nail, requests from people who thought an archive owed them answers.

She squared the slips up and read them, because reading was what she had instead of asking.

The third one was six months old, in violet ink. BELLAMY SHOAL — TIDE TABLES, 1984–1991, and beneath it a name and a return address in a hand younger than her own. The date column had never been filled in. Nobody had ever answered it.

She took a borrower’s card from the pocket pasted inside the front board and looked at the names on it. Three, all in the same faded copperplate, all older than the shelving.

Then she wrote *Iris* in the space for a name, and the date beneath it, and set the card back in its pocket while the ink was still wet enough to take the dust.

At the rear of the ledger, past the ruled columns, was the page she had been writing on for eleven months. Forty-one names, forty-one returns to the parish, and every one of them crossed through until the page looked like a hedge in winter. She turned to the last line she had made, in the ward, in the small hours, with the child still tacky on her hands.

She struck the name out. Underneath, in letters she pressed hard enough to emboss the reverse of the leaf, she wrote her own.

Speaking it took a moment to arrange, like a key that no longer fitted. “Maren,” she said.

The boy looked up. “What?”

Nothing came after it. The word had gone out of her like water out of a cracked pail and there was no second one behind it, and she stood there with her mouth still open and her hand flat on the page.

“That’s a queer sound,” he said, and went back to his book, and the fluorescent tube stuttered over the shelves and steadied.

She heated the wax over the heater’s grille until it ran, and pressed the crest into it, and banded the ledger shut so that the seam of paper and red wax would have to be broken before anyone read what was inside. Behind the deed boxes she found a gap the width of her hand and pushed it in there, spine inward, deep enough that a person would have to come looking with a lamp.

“That’s parish property, that,” the boy said, without heat. “You want to sign the book.”

The visitors’ book lay open at the counter, ruled for name and hour, and she wrote *Maren* and the hour and the day.

He came over to check it, the way he had not checked anything else all evening. His finger stopped under her name.

“That’s the one on Father Cussen’s list.”

She did not move.

“Whole quay’s on it,” he said, and turned the pages back to show her, though she had not asked. Names and names and names, going down, in the parish clerk’s small tight hand. “He had it out in the car and I saw it when he was doing his wipers. Everyone from the harbour road to the point.” His finger came back and tapped, once, the place where she had written. “Yours is the only one not crossed.”

The heater ticked. Somewhere above them a door opened onto the yard and let in the smell of mud and cold weed, and the boy looked at her as though she might explain a thing like that.

Maren took the pad out, and wrote, and turned it round so he could read it.

I DID.

## 12. The Third Tide

By half past six the water in Halloway’s street stood ankle-deep, and it had come up through the drains instead of over the shoal, warm as a hand pressed flat to the back of the neck. Iris carried Maren’s ledger at chest height in both arms, the way a person carries a thing they have already decided to ruin.

“The gauge is going backwards,” Rowan called. He was forty feet up the street with the staff in his fist, and its markings had gone under one by one. “Nine minutes. That’s what we get.”

“Then read it fast.”

“Read what?” He turned. Water slopped over the tops of his boots. “Iris. Read what?”

She didn’t answer, because the back pocket of the ledger had come open in her hands, the paste gone soft as a boiled sweet in a warm pocket, and the borrower’s card had slid out far enough to show its columns.

Three names, faded brown, all in a hand that predated the war. Then a fourth, in pencil, pressed hard enough to groove the pasteboard: *Iris.* No surname. Beside it a date ten years gone — a night she had spent in a student flat two hundred miles inland, eating toast over a textbook, entirely unaware that she had borrowed anything.

“Somebody signed for this in my name,” she said.

Rowan came back down the street, and the water came with him the wrong way, running uphill toward the church, and he stopped, and looked at his own boots, and understood that the ground was tilting under him.

Something rang. Not the buoy — that had been ringing all evening in a flat calm. This came from under his feet, under the street, one stroke, the way a sexton pulls once to see if the rope is sound.

He walked toward it.

“Rowan.”

Another stroke.

He walked, and the water took his knees, his belt, the flat of his hand.

Iris jammed the ledger under one arm and went after him, and for six strides the street was a street, and then it leaned and she was swimming in a lane between cottages with Rowan’s head a dark knot at the far end of it, going out unhurried, obedient, a man walking to a job.

The card was still in her fist. She got it above her head and said the only thing she had.

“Iris.”

The word went out of her flat and small. Water licked the pencil mark.

“Iris!” Louder. Her throat hurt with it. Under the street the bell stopped mid-stroke, as though a hand had been laid on the rope. “My name is Iris and I’m asking —”

The rest she had not planned. It came out anyway, in the tone she used on the archive floor, the one that made men in portacabins go quiet and check their own paperwork.

“— and I’m claiming the count.”

Stillness. Not silence; the sea was still working out beyond the shoal, gulls going about the mud, the buoy clanging its own business. But the street went slack, and the pull in it let go.

Rowan turned around. A man turns in his sleep and then wakes, and his arms came up, and he began, badly, to swim.

She got him by the collar where the lane met shingle. Both of them went down once; he came up spitting and swearing and alive, flat on his back in six inches of water, staring at the sky. “Why can I taste copper?”

“Because you were nearly drowned.”

“I was walking.” He rolled onto an elbow. “I was walking to — there was someone calling soundings. A leadline. Iris, I knew the numbers.”

The tide was draining properly now, the whole street hissing like a pan off the ring. Out past the shoal the last of Halloway went down — chimney pots, the church shoulder, the green door of the cottage where they had found the boot, whose it had been and when being questions she had not yet earned. She watched the roofline go under and said nothing, because there was nothing to say and a great deal to write.

Rowan sat up. “What did you do?”

“I put my name on it.”

“On what?”

“The count. Maren gave them hers and it took her voice. I gave them mine, and it took —” She stopped. Tested her mouth. The word *Iris* came back whole, now that it was no longer needed. “The leaving. I’m on this coast thirty years. That’s the term.”

“Thirty years.”

“Go inland. Sell the boat.”

He looked at his boots, full of the street. Then at the point, where the lamp had lit itself in a tower with no power to it, the beam turning slow and steady through the mist as though the clockwork had never gone into a van.

“I’ve a boat,” Rowan said, “and a licence, and a landlord in Falmouth who’d be glad of the room. What I haven’t got is anybody else who can read a gauge.” He stood, water sheeting off him, and held out his hand for the ledger. “Somebody’s got to keep the book while you sleep.”

The keeper’s cottage still had a radio, and it was talking before they were through the door — static, then a voice she knew from the harbour office, old and dry and unsurprised.

“Keeper,” it said. “Log the time.”

The clock on the wall had stopped at nine minutes past. Rowan found her a pencil. She wrote the hour, and the sea state, and her own name in the column for the keeper, and then she turned the book around and set the pencil in his hand.
