# The Ward

_Web Series · linear · genres: horror mystery · 5381 words_

## 1. Handover

COLD OPEN

19:42. Nkechi is doing handover with her coat already on, which is how Alma knows she's going to try it again.

"Bed one, Mrs Adeyemi. Night insulin. She'll argue about it."

"She always argues." Alma writes without looking up.

"Bed two, Mr Halloran. Confused after eight, keep him off the fire door."

"Mm."

"Four's bloods came back. Absolute neutrophils point nine. He's on tomorrow's list, so."

Alma's pen moves down the sheet. The nurses' station smells of floor polish and warm printer toner. Behind her the bed board holds its column of white cards — thirteen of them typed and slotted, and at the bottom of the column one card turned face to the wall.

Nkechi flips the page, reads out the last two surgicals, and then her thumb finds the bottom line and stops there. "And the back bed."

Alma signs the report. Dates it. Initials the top corner, the medication section, the two boxes nobody ever reads.

"Same as ever," Nkechi says.

"Same as ever."

The day nurse stands a moment longer with her bag on her shoulder. "Twelve years I've done this handover with you. You've never once—"

"Goodnight, Nkechi."

The ward doors breathe shut. Alma reaches back without turning and squares the face-down card with two fingers, so it sits straight in its slot like all the others, its number to the wall.

ACT ONE

Piotr Kade arrives at ten past eight with his lanyard still in the plastic and a rota he printed at home. Twenty-six, tall, hair wet from the rain, carrying a notebook he has already written in.

"You're early," Alma says.

"The email said half eight."

"The email's wrong. Bag in the office. Keys are on the hook. The fob with the green dot is the drug cupboard and you don't touch it tonight."

He clips the lanyard on. The photo sits crooked in its frame. "Where am I?"

"Front bay. Beds one to six." She walks him down, past the sluice, past the treatment room, and stops at the point where the corridor narrows. "Anything past this line, you come and get me."

"Why?"

"Because that's how I run it."

Piotr looks down the corridor. A door at the far end, half-glazed, the light behind it low and yellow. "Is that a side room?"

"That's the back room."

"There's no chart on the hook."

"There's a chart on the hook," Alma says, and moves him along with the flat of her hand between his shoulder blades. "Bed three needs a dressing. Gloves are in the trolley. The trolley pulls left, don't fight it."

He gets the dressing done. She watches him from the desk — good hands, talks to the patient, doesn't rush. At 20:50 he does the obs round and gets every number right.

At 21:30 the utility room printer wakes up and feeds one page into the tray.

Alma's head comes up.

ACT TWO

"Something printing?" Piotr calls.

"Stock list." She's already walking. "Do bed four's fluid chart."

The page is warm. She lifts it out with two fingers and holds it under the utility room light. The header is the ward's. The hospital number beneath it is new — the kind of fresh that gets stamped at 19:42, while she was still signing the report.

Patient name: KADE, PIOTR J.

Date of birth: 14 March.

Admitting consultant: a name she doesn't know. Admission type: elective. Diagnosis: for observation.

The bed field has a character typed into it. Alma presses her thumb over it and holds it there.

She opens the paper tray. Full. She queues a test page from the desk and the printer produces a blank sheet with a squashed footer and nothing else. She prints the ward list. Fourteen lines. She counts them twice.

Piotr finds her in the corridor with the chart against her chest. "Is that mine?"

"What?"

"The page. It had my name on it. I saw it from the bay."

"Reprint." Her voice comes out level, which costs her something. "Old admissions duplicate. The system's been doing it since the upgrade."

"It had my date of birth."

"Piotr. Bed five's cannula site is oozing. Go and look at it."

She gets the chart into the drug cupboard at 22:05. Steel, bolted to the wall, two trays of controlled drugs and a logbook. The keys go back onto the coiled cord on her hip. When she turns around he's in the doorway.

"You locked a chart in the CD cupboard."

"I locked a chart in the CD cupboard."

"That's not—"

"Nothing on this ward is."

He doesn't step out of the doorway. The strip light above him buzzes half a cycle and comes back on, and he's still holding his cannula hand at chest height like he's forgotten what it's attached to.

"Fourteenth of March," he says.

"Bed five."

"Fourteenth of March isn't my birthday." He says it to the corridor, not to her. "It's the third of November. It's been the third of November for forty-one years."

Behind her the printer wakes. One sheet. Then two. Then a run of them, fast, the tray filling.

Alma keeps her palm flat on the cupboard door, on the steel, warm where she's been leaning, and does not turn around.

A page slides out under the utility room door and stops against her shoe, face up. The ward list. She counts it. Fifteen.

The fifteenth line reads bed two. KADE, PIOTR J. Date of birth 14 March.

"Alma." From the doorway, very quiet. "Why is there a page with me on it at bed two?"

## 2. The Census

COLD OPEN

The tally counter clicks in Piotr’s fist, once for each body.

Bed one: a woman asleep sitting up, glasses still on. Two: the man with the chest drain, awake, watching the ceiling tile. Three: stripped, mattress rolled, the trolley parked across it. Four: Halloran, breathing under the sheet, fluid chart taped to the rail.

He works up the far bay. Curtain rings tick as he pushes through. At the end, the strip light is dimmer than the rest — someone swapped a tube and nobody has swapped it back. A shape lies under the blanket in the last bed, back to the room, grey hair fanned on the pillow.

Click.

Sixteen.

In the office, Piotr does the arithmetic on the back of his hand twice. Fifteen beds. The laminated card screwed to the door frame says BEDS: 15.

He prints the ward list at 01:58. Fourteen names.

ACT ONE

Twenty past two. Alma has her head down over the controlled-drugs book, counting entries in blocks of five.

“I’m over.” Piotr sets the counter on the desk between them. “Sixteen patients. Fifteen beds.”

“Which bed.”

“That’s the thing—”

“Which bed, Piotr.”

He opens his mouth and finds nothing behind it. “I counted bodies. Not beds.”

Alma sets her pen down at an angle, square to the ledger, and looks up at him for the first time since handover. “Then you haven’t done a census. You’ve done a walk.” She turns the ledger round and taps the column headed OCCUPANCY. “Go back. Count beds. Not people. The bed is the fact. The person in it is a rumour.”

“That’s not how counting—”

“Eight minutes. I want a number that matches the card on that door.”

He goes. She hears him start at bed one again, slower. She does not look at the office door while he does it.

Eight minutes later he is back, holding his phone up as a torch. “Fifteen beds. Fifteen numbers.” His voice has changed weight. “Except the last one in the far bay. The plate’s blank. Not missing — blank. Painted over. The paint’s still soft. You can smell it.”

Alma closes the ledger.

“Sit down.”

ACT TWO

The drawer at the bottom of the filing cabinet takes both hands to open. Inside: census forms, a decade of them, rubber-banded by year.

“You don’t invent a bed,” Alma says. “You don’t invent a patient. You never write a number you can’t see.” She lays a blank form flat. “You write what’s in the bed.”

“Then what do I write?”

“Three letters.”

Piotr looks at the form. BED. PATIENT. TIME COUNTED.

“Say the number out loud and it becomes a bed we’re responsible for. A bed with no admission, no consultant, no funding, no—” Alma stops herself. Starts again. “If the count doesn’t balance, the ward doesn’t balance. If the ward doesn’t balance, someone comes and counts it. Do you understand what it’s like when someone comes and counts?”

The radiator knocks twice in the pipe above them.

“Write QB.”

He takes the pen. She puts her hand flat on the paper before the nib lands. “In your hand. Not mine.”

“That matters?”

“Everything on this ward is somebody’s hand.”

He writes QB in the bed column at 02:26. She makes him initial it. Then she takes the form and feeds it into the scanner on the wall. The little light drags across the page. On the screen, the exception box turns green: LOGGED.

While she’s typing, he pulls the old folder towards him. Twelve years of the same three letters — 2013, 2016, 2019 — all in Alma’s sharp backhand, all identical, all initialled.

Then he finds one that isn’t.

The bed column on this one has a real number in it. Someone has gone over it with marker, hard, twice, until the ballpoint has bitten a ridge through the paper. He lifts the sheet to the desk lamp and tilts it.

Alma takes it out of his fingers without hurry and posts it into the shredder. The shredder runs for six seconds. She watches it the whole time.

“Whose was that?” he says.

“Go and do the four o’clock obs.”

ACT THREE

At 03:52 the screen in the office wakes itself.

OCCUPANCY EXCEPTION — UNRESOLVED
Physical capacity: 15
Recorded occupancy: 16
Provide numeric bed code for occupied position.

Alma types her log-in. The field returns a single line in grey: NOT AUTHORISED FOR THIS FIELD. She tries again. Same line. The cursor blinks in the box like something breathing.

“Twelve years,” she says, mostly to the monitor. “Twelve years and paper was enough.”

Piotr leans in. “It wants a bed number.”

“It wants a signature. It’s had paper signatures for twelve years, and now it’s had one that isn’t mine.” She scrolls. “Four hours. Then it escalates.”

He reaches past her for the keyboard. She catches his wrist, and her grip is not gentle.

“Don’t. Once you type a number in that box, there’s a bed here with a patient in it and no admission behind them, and the whole thing comes down on this ward by breakfast.”

“So what happens at six?”

The screen refreshes itself.

RESOLUTION DEADLINE: 06:00
REVIEWER ASSIGNED: S. BENEDICT

Alma sits back. Something goes out of her face.

“Who’s S. Benedict?”

She picks up the phone, holds it, puts it back down on the cradle. Picks it up again and dials from memory. The dial tone stops before the first ring finishes.

Sister Benedict’s voice, dry as a form being unfolded: “Whose hand wrote it?”

Alma looks across the desk at Piotr, at the pen still in his fingers, at the QB drying on the carbon.

“His.”

A pause on the line, long enough that the pips start.

“Then it was yours the moment you taught him,” Sister Benedict says. “Bring me the form at six. And Alma — don’t let him count again tonight.”

## 3. Day Matron

COLD OPEN

05:49. Rain coming off the fire door in ropes, the corridor lights still on their night setting, running at half strength and humming like something with a tooth loose.

Alma stands outside the matron’s office with her hands in her cardigan pockets, saying the sentence over in her head, and it keeps coming out wrong. Every version of it has the bed in it. She has been standing here since twenty to. She knows because she checked, and then checked again, and the second check told her nothing the first one hadn’t.

The day staff come up the stairs in their own weather — wet hair, coats over arms, the smell of toast and bus. A porter trolleys a stack of clean pillows past her and the plastic squeaks against itself. Down the corridor a cleaner buffs a square of floor, backs up, buffs it again, the machine whining at the same pitch it whined at a minute ago and will whine at in a minute.

Sister Benedict arrives with her coat still buttoned and does not look at Alma while she unbuttons it.

“Ask me the thing you came to ask,” she says. “Before the phones start.”

ACT ONE

She works the bed board as she listens. Twenty-two name plates, each on its own magnet, and she moves them with the edge of a wooden ruler, never her fingers — up a row when a patient is stepped down, down a row when they’re stepped up. The ruler clicks the board on every move. It is the only sound in the room that Benedict is making on purpose.

“I want Kade moved to days,” Alma says.

Click. “Days has three seniors.”

“Days has three seniors, a student and a ward clerk. Nights has me and a boy on his third week.”

“Third.” Benedict nudges a plate up a row. “Third week, and he counts well. I read the report.”

“That’s my point. He counts well because he needs to be right. Needing to be right is the wrong appetite for this ward.”

Benedict’s hand stops. She sets the ruler down flat, squared to the edge of the board.

“Say a reason that isn’t about the bed.”

The cleaner’s machine hits the end of the corridor, turns, whines back the way it came.

“The bed isn’t in my reasons.”

“Then say one.”

Alma takes the breath. “He’s twenty-six. He’s got a mother in Kraków who rings the switchboard of a Sunday because she doesn’t trust the time difference. He’s going to be a very good nurse, in a ward with windows, at a hospital with a coffee machine in the foyer, and I want him to get there before he stops being soft.”

Click. Another plate up a row.

“And if I move him,” Benedict says, “who takes the nights?”

“Put one of the day seniors on.”

“Which?”

“Haddad.”

“Haddad did three weeks of nights in her first year. She still sleeps with the light on the landing. Ask her if you like.”

“Then advertise.”

“I’ve advertised.” Benedict steps back and looks at what she’s made. “Four times. Nights here pays a band up and nobody from outside applies twice.”

Alma looks at the board herself. Twenty-two plates. She counts them without meaning to, gets twenty-two, and counts again to be sure, and gets twenty-two, which is correct, which has never once been the problem.

“Fill out a transfer request,” Benedict says. “Properly. Form’s in the top drawer. It’s been in the top drawer twelve years, so it’ll want dusting.”

“And you’ll sign it?”

“Bring it to me at six tomorrow and I’ll consider it.”

ACT TWO

The day room at 06:20, the good hour, tea going cold on the sill. Haddad sits with a bowl of cereal and the crossword, still in her coat, the pen not moving.

Alma sits down opposite her. “Swap with me.”

“Swap what?”

“Two of your days for my nights. Kade goes to days, you take two nights a week, I take two of his, the rest of yours are yours.”

Haddad keeps the pen on the same square. “Nobody goes onto nights, Alma.”

“People work nights in every hospital in the country.”

“Then ask the country.” The pen doesn’t move. “Nine years I’ve been on this ward. I have never once seen a night shift go up on that board empty. Two names, every week, twelve years, and one of them is always you.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer I have.” Haddad looks up, and her face does the thing faces do on this ward, which is nothing at all. “He applied for nights.”

“What?”

“Ask him. It’s in the folder on the clerk’s desk. I filed it.” She goes back to the crossword and this time she writes a word in. “The advert was for two day posts and one night.”

The folder is where she says it is. Alma reads it standing up, the corner of the desk pressing into her hip: the degree, the referees, the right to work, and the box marked *shift preference*, and in the box, in the small neat hand of a man who fills in forms as though they matter, **NIGHTS ONLY**.

He’s in the sluice when she finds him, sleeves up past the elbow, and the water’s too hot — she knows from the steam coming off it.

“You asked for nights,” she says.

“I did.”

“There were two day posts on that advert.”

Piotr turns the tap off before he answers, which takes a second, and she uses it.

“Were there?” he says.

ACT THREE

06:00. Benedict sits with the request in front of her and reads it the way she reads everything, running her thumbnail along each line as though she could feel a ridge where the ink went down.

“Reason for transfer,” she says. “Clinical.”

“He’s insufficiently supervised. Nights requires two seniors. There are not two seniors.”

“There’s one senior and a third-week.”

“Then approve the transfer.”

Benedict opens the top drawer. The wood grinds. She sets the page inside, and then takes out the other one — older, grey at the corner, the carbon still folded behind it — and lays it on the desk and turns it round to face Alma.

Same form. Twelve years old. Her own handwriting, which has changed only in how much less of it there is now: the block capitals, the too-hard press she used before the tendon went.

“You filled this within a month of starting,” Benedict says. “You were going to days. Orthopaedic ward, windows on two sides. You were twenty-nine and you had a tendon that worked.”

Alma’s hand is flat on the desk and she doesn’t remember putting it there.

“I don’t remember.”

“No. You had a night where you didn’t sleep, and in the morning you asked me to withdraw it, and I didn’t shred it, because I don’t shred things that have been signed.” Benedict’s thumbnail goes down the old page, along the line that asks which bed or room is affected. She doesn’t stop on it. She goes past it, and past it again. “You know what’s on that line.”

The entry’s there. It’s the size of a thumb. The nib went through the paper three times, twice one way and once across, and under it you can see the shape of a number the way you see a bone under skin.

“Why keep it?” Alma says.

“Because one day I’ll want to show somebody who signed what.” Benedict closes the drawer. “I’ll not move him to days. Not while that bed is on his nights, and not one hour before.”

Alma takes the old page off the desk, folds it, puts it in her cardigan pocket. Benedict watches her do it and doesn’t reach.

In the corridor the lights have come up to full and the day is making its noise, and Piotr is waiting by the drug cupboard with his lanyard already on, because he doesn’t know how to be off duty yet.

“Did she sign it?” he says.

“No.”

“Then I’ll stay on nights.”

The paper in Alma’s pocket has a corner sharper than paper should be. She feels it against her thumb, once, and takes her hand out.

“No, you won’t,” she says. “You’ll do something for me first.”

## 4. Observation

COLD OPEN

"Records shuts at four," Alma says. "You'll be back by half past."

The key is a mortice key on a loop of orange plastic. She rubs her thumb across the tag first, checking the stamp, and then drops it into his palm.

"Twelve boxes. Sign the ledger. Don't stop to read."

"Why would I read them?"

"Because you're the sort who counts." She takes a biro out of her breast pocket and clicks it twice, handing it over. "And Piotr. Bay Two's mine tonight. You don't go past the screen."

"What's behind it?"

"A bed."

"I know it's a bed."

"Then you know everything I'm going to tell you."

The stairwell door closes behind him with a suck of air. Through the wired glass he can see her at the far end of the ward, past the last curtain rail, standing with her arms at her sides. She doesn't check her watch. She doesn't turn. He takes the first flight, and the second landing puts a wall between them, and she was still standing there.

ACT ONE

Records smells of warm dust and toner. Strip lights come on in sections as he walks, one row at a time, like someone lighting him along.

The boxes are where she said. Twelve of them, on the bottom shelf, each with a ward label in the same hand. Someone has ruled a line through the old ward name and written the new one underneath.

The first book falls open at the middle. He sees the cut before he understands it — a rectangle taken out of the page, two inches by half an inch, clean on all four edges. A blade and a steel rule. He turns the page. Another. He pulls the next book, and the next, and it goes on: every night report, every month, a hole in the same place where a line should be.

Piotr sits down on an upturned crate and does the arithmetic in his head. Twelve years.

The last three slots in the bottom box are empty. He looks at the three books he carried down, stacked on the desk where he put them, still warm from the ward. Eleven months of them.

He takes the top one and opens it at tonight's date.

Nothing has been cut. The line lies flat and whole, and the ink is fresh enough to shine — not typed, written, in a hand he has watched all week. On the line where the room number goes there are four words: *the quiet bed.*

The column beside it is filled in. The name box. Not blank.

He closes the book on his thumb but keeps his finger in the page, breathing through his mouth, aware of the sensor lights and how long they have been on. Somebody cut eleven years out of these and left this one whole.

The book goes under his arm. The biro stays in his fist. He takes the stairs at a jog and does not sign the ledger.

ACT TWO

03:44, and the ward clock disagrees with his phone by a minute. Alma looks up from the desk.

"You're early."

"Records shuts at four."

"It's a quarter to."

"Then I'm early."

Her eyes go to the folder under his arm and stay there a beat too long. Before she can say anything, the buzzer for Bay One lights the desk — three presses, then three more, fast.

"Mrs. Oyelaran," Alma says, and she is already moving, pulling the toilet keys off the hook. "Stay at the desk. Don't do anything clever. She's eighty-one and she's locked herself in and she'll have her feet up on the cistern."

The Bay One door bangs. Her voice goes soft and carrying down the corridor, the way it did at handover: *Eileen, love, it's Alma.*

Steel trolley, third drawer. He knows it's against orders. The knowing doesn't slow his hands.

The quiet bed sits in its own bay, curtained on three sides, and the fourth side is a screen. Behind the screen the air is warmer. Somebody has propped the window a hand's width with a folded gauze pack, and the night comes in and moves the sheet.

The cuff hisses tight around the thin arm. Fifty-four. One-oh-two over sixty-four. Thirty-six point nine.

He knows these numbers the way he knows his own handwriting. He recited them into a mouthpiece in a rented room in Kraków, and the technician wrote the pulse down twice because she thought the machine had lied. The monitor here agrees with her. It agrees with everything.

"You're not supposed to be in here," he tells the bed, and lifts the wrist.

The band is old stock, yellowed at the laminate, a design they stopped issuing before he ever set foot in this country. He turns it with two fingers. The type has faded to grey.

He reads it. He reads it again. The name box in the ledger, filled in — he thinks of the book under his arm and cannot make his hand go for it.

Under his fingertips the pulse changes. Not a jump, not a stumble. It climbs, steady and unhurried, one beat at a time, until it sits exactly where his own heart is sitting.

The monitor and his wrist, in time.

## 5. Nine

COLD OPEN

04:07.

Alma turns the key in the office door from the inside, sits down, and opens the chart flat on the blotter.

She reads the number out loud.

"Nine."

Nothing eats the word. Radiator ticking. The cistern refilling next door. Her own swallow.

"Bed nine," she says, louder, to the ceiling, to the sensor light. "Patient in bed nine."

Over the quiet bay, the call bell lights up. One ring, thin as thread pulled through cloth.

She leans into the intercom. "Hello."

The speaker gives her breath. Slow. Wet. A sleeper's breath, close to the grille.

"Say your name," Alma says. "Go on. Any name at all."

The intercom clicks off. The light over the bay goes dark. Somebody knocks on the office door.

ACT ONE

Piotr stands in the doorway with his coat still on and his lanyard twisted the wrong way round, and he says:

"You said it out loud."

Alma folds the chart shut. "You're meant to be in Bay One."

"Mrs Oyelaran's asleep. She's had her feet off that cistern since four." He comes in. Doesn't sit. Sets something on the desk, face down. The wristband. "I heard you from the sluice."

"Then you heard a tired woman counting."

"Alma."

"Close the door."

He closes it and stays against it.

"The band," he says. "You know what's on it."

She looks at the blank side, the little plastic clasp. "I know what's usually on them."

"My name. My date of birth. The NHS number I gave occupational health in March." Steady voice, and it costs him. "The obs match my physical. Pulse, pressure, temp. Down to the—"

"I know."

"You knew before I told you."

"Expected," Alma says. "I've been expecting since Tuesday. It gets restless when there's a new one."

His hand finds the desk edge. "So what's in there?"

"Eleven years of charts in that cupboard with the name box empty, and one with a name in it. That's the whole of what I know." She stands. "It's a bed that has to be counted and can't be written. Write it and it's a record. Record makes it a person. Person—"

"Takes a name."

"Whoever's name is on it."

He starts to speak. She lifts a hand, crosses to the drug cupboard, works the keys.

"Here's what happens now. You're going off the ward. Not a punishment, not a promotion. I'll sign you back to the agency tonight, you'll take a placement at St Anne's, and in a month you'll decide you made the whole thing up."

"And the bed?"

"Somebody has to sign the admission," Alma says. "Get your coat."

ACT TWO

04:31. The terminal.

Patient number first, off the chart, and the system takes it — small mercy, that it will still read a number. Destination: HOME. The field blinks red. COMMUNITY. Red. It wants a named address. She types the hospital's own postcode, then deletes it.

Consultant: Benedict, S. REJECTED — NO DISCHARGE RIGHTS. Her own ID goes in. A yellow banner rolls along the top: CENSUS WILL FALL BELOW FUNDED ESTABLISHMENT. ADMIT TO CONTINUE.

Twelve years, and nobody at the trust has ever asked why bed nine has a patient who has never had a blood test.

The old form slides out of the back of the folder — three folds, a coffee ring, her own handwriting at twenty-nine. TRANSFER REQUEST. DAYS TO NIGHTS. Reason: establishment. Her signature in biro at the bottom. The matron's beside it. And a box the matron had ticked: REPLACEMENT AGREED.

Overriding the banner takes two clicks and a line that says she understands she is accountable.

The admission field sits open below it, cursor going.

She leaves it open.

The screen around the quiet bay is cool to the back of her hand. Out comes the folded gauze pack, and the sash drops the last hand's width with a soft, finished thud. Air moves. The curtain breathes. The sheet flattens, and the sats probe rolls off the pillow and knocks once against the rail.

Alma stands at the head of the bed, at close range, the way you'd wake somebody.

"Nine."

The monitor holds its old trace a beat, then goes to nothing, and the nothing does not alarm.

She lifts the wristband off the pillow, pockets it, strips the linen, and washes her hands for the length of two verses of nothing at all.

ACT THREE

05:12. The lift lobby, where the corridor lights are the colour of old milk.

Piotr has his bag on his shoulder. She holds out the badge. He takes it and doesn't put it on.

"The agency calls at nine," she says. "Take the placement."

"What did you do?"

"Signed you off."

"That's not the same as what you did."

The lift arrives. He steps in and puts his hand across the door so it can't close.

"It's still in there," he says. "Whatever it is. It's just got someone else's name on it now."

"Go home."

"Who's the new admission?"

The doors start their chime. Alma takes her hand off the frame.

"Alma—"

The doors close on him.

05:41. She types REYES, ALMA into the field and leaves occupation blank, because the drop-down has no box for what she is. The system thinks about it. Then it prints.

The band comes out warm. REYES, A. NINE.

She is fastening it when the doorway fills with coat and car keys and the smell of the car park.

Sister Benedict looks at the screen. Looks at the wrist. Sets the day report on the blotter without a word.

"Heard it in the car park," she says. "Nine. Carried like a bell."

"Sign the report."

The pen comes out. Her name goes down slowly, in that convent hand. "You were twenty-nine," she says. "You had a flat with a balcony."

"I had a flat with damp."

"Piotr's filed to come back." Benedict caps the pen. "Came through the night desk at four. Nights. This ward."

"Then decline it," Alma says.

"I'm not the one who can." Benedict sets her keys on the blotter and, for once, doesn't pick them back up. "You emptied a bed. Empty beds get filled. You made the vacancy, Sister. He's answering it."

The band has gone warm against her pulse. She presses the seal flat with her thumb, hard, until the plastic bites.

Bay nine's curtain is drawn. It has been drawn all night. She has walked past it eleven times and counted each one.

She takes the rail and pulls.

The bed is made. The chart is clipped to the foot, printed, filled in — admission 05:12, obs charted to the minute, no gaps, nobody's handwriting but the system's. Under occupation, where the drop-down has no box for what she is, someone has typed it anyway.

The sheet is not flat. It has the shape of a person in it.

Alma looks at the wrist on the pillow. The band is warm. It says REYES, A. NINE.

She looks at her own wrist. It's bare.
