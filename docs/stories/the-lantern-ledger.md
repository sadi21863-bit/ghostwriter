# The Lantern Ledger

_Novel · linear · genres: literary noir + quiet fantasy · 4221 words_

## 1. The Rain Debt

The eels were still moving in the tub when Ivo Marchetti came down the market row and found Nissa Vane gutting mackerel on a board worn to a shallow dish.

“Nissa.”

“Collector.” Her knife didn’t stop. “Season’s thin.”

“I know the season.” He took his hat off and set it brim-down on the post beside her, so the rain wouldn’t fill the crown. “I know your husband’s passage, too. Eleven months of it. Saint Yarrow wouldn’t have him. The harbor boats wouldn’t either, and I said his name in rooms that don’t care for his name.”

Silver flecks clung to her wrist. The knife went down flat.

“Ansel’s four months drowned,” she said.

“Five.”

“Five.” She wiped her hands front to back, both sides, and did not look at him. “You’d know it to the day.”

“It’s my trade to know it to the day.”

From under the board she brought out a brass token, small as a coat button, on a loop of tarred cord. A lantern had been stamped into it by a hand in a hurry. Rain struck the brass and stood in beads instead of running off.

Ivo turned it over. “That’s not coin.”

“No.”

“You understand what I’m owed.”

“I understand I’ll be owed it whether I pay or no. That’s the thing about your kind.” She pushed it across the board. “The lantern clears him.”

“Where did it come from?”

“Ansel carried night crossings forty years and never took the water without a light on the bow. When the harbor took him, the light came back in on the tide.” Her chin lifted. “That’s what I have. That’s what I’ve got to pay with.”

Behind them a man was shouting prices at a stack of hake, and a gull stood on the roof ridge tearing at something that had been a fish, and the rain found the seam of Ivo’s coat at the shoulder and began, patiently, to get in.

“Token’s not worth a ferryman’s passage,” he said.

“No,” Nissa said. “But you’ll take it.”

He took it. Warm, the brass was, on a morning cold enough to ache the fillings in his teeth, and he had said Ansel Vane’s name in three rooms where the saying of it cost him something, and the widow was watching him with her husband’s forty years in her face.

“I’ll have it entered,” he said. “If the book won’t hold it, I’ll be back before the tide.”

The customs house basement smelled of wet rope and lime and the particular sourness of paper that never dries. Sera Quill worked under a lamp with a cracked green shade, her sleeves pinned back, ink on the outside of her right hand where a pen rests and on the inside where it shouldn’t.

“You’re late in the day for a deposit,” she said, without turning.

“You’re late in the day for a clerk.” He put the token on the desk. “Nissa Vane. Her husband’s favor. She tendered this.”

Sera looked at the brass a long moment. Then she opened the drawer and lifted out the ledger, which was wider than it was tall and bound in something the color of a drowned man’s lips.

“Non-coin,” she said.

“She’s a widow with a fish knife and a bad season. Take it up with the weather.”

“The book takes it or it doesn’t. I don’t argue with it.” She turned to a page near the end, wrote the date, wrote Nissa Vane, and beneath it: one brass lantern, stamped, cord attached, received of a ferryman’s widow. Then she waited with her pen up.

Rain ran in the light well. A ship’s bell counted something out in the harbor.

“There,” Sera said, and let the pen down. “Passage cleared. She’ll not see you again.”

“That’s the arrangement.”

“That’s the arrangement.” She turned the book toward him.

The ink moved. Not on the page she had written — on the margin, where a narrow column of names had been kept in a hand generations older than hers, and a great many more of them than Ivo had ever seen. A line drew itself under the last. The line went out and came back and spelled, in a script that leaned like a man walking into weather:

IVO MARCHETTI.

He read it twice. Then he read the figures beside it, which were not money.

“What’s my name doing there?”

“You’re a debtor.” Sera laced her fingers on the desk. “As am I. As is every soul in this port who ever asked and was answered. The book keeps both sides. You collect for it — that’s your work, that’s what they pay you for — and every line you collect goes against your name.”

“Against my name for what?”

“For the day it balances.” She said it the way a woman reads out a tide table. “The book must close even. It always closes even.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“It always does.”

Ivo put one finger on the margin and drew it away, and the ink did not smear, and he noticed that his hand was perfectly steady, which struck him as the worst thing about the morning.

“Copy it,” he said.

“I beg your pardon?”

“The page. My page. Copy it out.”

Sera sat back. Somewhere above them, boots went across the customs floor, a door banged, and a man laughed at something he would not remember by supper. “A copy costs,” she said.

“Then it costs.”

She took a fresh sheet, trimmed the edge with a knife, and began to write. Her hand at first was the neat hand of a clerk and then it was not; it hurried, it leaned, it went out after lines that weren’t on the page she was reading from. The lamp guttered twice. When she was done she blotted it and held it out, and would not look at him while he took it.

Ivo folded the sheet into his coat, where it lay against his ribs like a second heart, and half the lines on it, he saw as the fold closed, were already gone soft at the edges, rubbed out, as though someone had been carrying it a long while and paying it down.

“One thing,” he said from the stair. “How many names are on that margin?”

“Above yours?” Sera picked up her pen again. “None. You’re the last entry in the book, collector. Every favor you ever collected is owed back to the day.”

Wet air came down the stairwell. He thought of Nissa Vane’s chin coming up, and the brass warm in his hand, and forty years of night crossings rowed home by a light that came back in on the tide.

“Then I’d better learn to read my own handwriting,” Ivo said, and went up into the rain to start counting.

Rain came off the market roofs in ropes, and the cobbles ran like a river finding its bed. Ivo stood in the lee of the fish stalls with the copy spread under his coat flap and read what showed.

The columns were not in Sera’s hand anymore. Forty names going up the page like a tide line, and against each name a small sum of days. Some struck through. Some struck through and then un-struck, the ink worried at until the paper went to fur.

Four lines above his own: Tobin Ash, the caulker, who owed him for a passage three winters back and had settled it in rope and in not meeting his eye afterward. The sum beside Tobin Ash was one night.

Not a night’s work. A night.

Ivo stood with the rain finding his collar and looked at the numbers until they stopped being numbers. Then he crossed the market to the caulker’s shed, where the lamp was lit and the smell of hot pitch came out into the weather like something alive, and knocked.

The door opened on Tobin’s wife. She had a chisel in her fist and grief already forming in her, the way ice forms on a still pond — from the edges in.

“He’s not to the harbor,” she said.

“No.”

“Then where’s he to?”

Ivo stepped past her into the pitch-smell and the lamplight, and did not answer, because he had not yet learned the words for it, and the copy against his ribs had gone soft as a leaf in his coat.

## 2. The Clerk’s Hand

Customs kept its dry floors above and its wet ones below, and the twelve steps down to the record room were slick enough that Ivo took them one at a time, one hand on the wall.

Sera Quill had a lamp burning and a second one dead. She was cleaning her nib with a scrap of rag, unhurried, and did not look up until he set both palms flat on the table.

“Stand where I can see your hands,” she said. “That’s better.”

“Read me the margin.”

“You can read.”

“Read it anyway.”

She set the rag down and drew the ledger around by its corner, the way a person moves something they have been told is heavier than it looks. The binding had gone soft as old boot leather. Pages fanned and settled. The room smelled faintly of vinegar.

“Names,” she said. “One to a line. Collected favors, and the day you took each one.”

“And the space after the names?”

“What’s owed.”

Ivo leaned in. The writing there was small and closed and patient, and he did not recognize the hand and did not need to.

“So I’m to pay it back,” he said.

“Every line.” Sera folded her arms. “The book doesn’t come due in installments, collector. It balances once. On the day you don’t get up.”

He straightened. Above them, a chair scraped the customs floor and stopped. “Who keeps this? Not you. You were clerking for a shipping agent eight months ago.”

“My mother kept it. She taught me the columns before I could read a newspaper.” A beat. “I inherited on a Tuesday. I had a coat on. That was the whole ceremony.”

“Where’s your mother now?”

“Farther inland than you’d think.” Sera picked up her pen and did not write. “Ask me a question I can answer.”

Rain found the window well and started in. Ivo took out the copied page and laid it beside the ledger and looked from one to the other and back, and there was his hand, and there was the flinching version of it that had gone soft at the fold.

“I want one struck,” he said.

“They don’t strike.”

“There has to be a way to put a debt down.”

That got her looking at him. “Forgiveness,” she said. “A creditor can forgive. He has to speak it out loud, with the book open. Then the line comes off.” She said the last part slower, as though she’d been made to memorize the words and had never liked them.

“Give me a name.”

“You’re in earnest.”

“Tully. Bram Tully, the flatfish man. He’s owed me since the spring, and he’s got a stall he can’t leave to pay it and a back that won’t lift a crate.”

“Then forgive him in your own kitchen and spare the book.”

“I’m not doing it to spare him,” Ivo said. “I’m doing it to see.”

She studied him the way she had on the stair the first time, though with less fear in it and more arithmetic. Then she turned the ledger, found the page, and held the lamp up so the script lay flat under the light.

“Say his name plain,” she said. “Say what you gave him and say it’s done.”

“Bram Tully.” The words came out rougher than he meant. “The loan for the icehouse. It’s done. I release it.”

The ink did not fade. It gathered—lifted off the page in a slow bead and ran sideways along the margin, and where it went it left a shadow of what had been there, like a nail pulled from a board. Ivo watched it crawl. It stopped under a name he didn’t know.

“Neve Tully,” Sera said. Her voice had gone flat. “Daughter.”

He turned the book toward himself with both hands. Neve Tully, and after the name the amount, written exact to the barrel and the week, and under it the word *due* in a hand he was beginning to know.

“That’s a child.”

“She’s nine. She carries her father’s flatfish in a basket up the fish row and she can count change faster than the customs clerk.” Sera took the lamp back and set it down with a knock that made the flame jump. “You forgave him, collector. The book didn’t lose the line. It moved it, and he loves her, so he’ll pay it, and every barrel of it will come out of her.”

Ivo stood with his palms still flat on the table, the wood cold and slightly tacky under them. Forty years he had walked out of houses with his hat in his hand and something owed in his coat, and it had never once been carried by a child.

“Take it back,” he said.

“There’s no back. There’s only down the page.”

“Then whose line is under hers?”

Sera wet her finger and turned to the front of the book where the names stood oldest and palest, and ran down the column until she found the place, and put her nail on it.

“Hers is the second-to-last entry,” she said. “The last is yours. When you die the book balances, and whatever’s left in the margin falls to the name closest above you.”

He looked at the two names, hers and his, with nothing between them but a ruled line.

“How long has she got?” he said.

## 3. The Harbormaster’s Toll

Paraffin, tar, and the wet wool of a man who had been damp so long he'd stopped noticing — that was the smell of the harbormaster's office at the end of the north pier, where the harbor board had never run a line for a lamp.

"You've got paper in your coat," Old Pell said, before the door was shut.

Rain ran off Ivo's sleeves onto the mat. "Who told you that?"

"Water told me. It came up the pilings an hour ago with a story about a man who took a brass fish off a widow and walked out into the weather with it." Pell tapped the barometer twice with a knuckle, an old habit, and didn't look at what it read. "Sit. I'll give you the bad gin, and you can tell me how much of the book you've read."

Ivo sat. The gin was bad. Pell lowered himself into the chair behind the desk with the care of a man setting down something he'd have to pick up again.

"Twenty-six years," Pell said. "That's how long I've been at the end of this pier. Before that I was on the water. You know what I did when my own book came due?"

"Tell me."

"I gave the sea a name." He turned his glass a quarter turn, then back again. "You can't owe a thing that has no name. So I wrote one into the column. Weather, I called it, and I entered every man I ever sent out into a blow and every hull I lost because I liked the freight better than the forecast — all of it — onto that name. The sea took the balance. It's been taking it out of me ever since."

Ivo set his glass down. "Taking what?"

"Leaving. I can't. Not past the last buoy. Not up the coast road in a cart. The book took the sea's word for it that I'm still settling." Pell showed his palms, blunt and flat as two boards. "I'm a tollbooth now. Everything that comes into this port and everything that goes out of it goes through my hand. That's the arrangement."

A gull hit the window and slid off, and neither of them turned to look.

"Then why am I here?" Ivo said. "You didn't ask me down for the gin."

"No." Pell drew a folded slip from under the blotter, soft from carrying, and pushed it across the desk. "This came up in my hand yesterday, the way things do. I've been sitting on it since. Read the hour first."

The hour was written at the top in Sera's hand. *Midnight.* Under it: *Quill, Corinne. One favor taken by I. Marchetti. Unpaid.*

Ivo read it twice. His own name looked smaller than he remembered being.

"The night the *Calla* went down with three aboard," Pell said, "you needed a body to swear you weren't on her. Corinne Quill stood in a room with a customs seal on the table and swore it, and she walked out, and she never asked you for a thing afterward. Not a coin, not a word. That's how the good ones do it. They don't bill you. The book bills you."

"I remember the room."

"You remember the table. That's not the same."

Ivo put the slip down flat and set one finger on the hour. "And at midnight?"

"Midnight the book looks for somewhere to put what it's owed. It prefers blood. It always has. Her daughter wrote herself into that ledger when she took the post — a clerk's name is in the book. So at midnight a line goes looking for Sera Quill, and the book calls that a fair transfer, because it is one."

The stove ticked. Rain came through the frame in a fine spray and made the paraffin smoke.

"Move it," Ivo said. "You're the tollbooth. Move it."

"I can't move it. But the book takes one payment to an hour. That's a clerk's rule, and clerks' rules hold." Pell leaned back; the chair complained. "If something else is collected at midnight, the hour's spoke for. The Quill line gets pushed to the next page, and the next page is a year off, and in a year a man might think of something."

"What's due at midnight that could be collected?"

"Me." Pell said it without ceremony, the way a man says the tide. "My own line's been due since the spring. No hour on it — the book's been patient with me. But patience is a thing with a bottom to it, and you're the only collector in this port."

Ivo looked at him a while. The old man's face had the soft ruin of a thing left out in weather, and under the ruin something that was not afraid and not quite calm.

"You're asking me to kill you."

"I'm asking you to be in the room when I settle. There's a difference, and the book knows it, and I'd rather not argue the point with you at my age."

"All right," Ivo said.

"You'll be in the room."

"I'll be in the room."

The door opened on a boy with rain in his hair. Behind him, Sera Quill came in with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and her jaw set like she'd been running and had decided not to arrive out of breath.

"You sent for me," she said to Pell. "Not him."

"Both of you. Sit down, girl, and get the book open. Midnight's not a patient hour."

She unwrapped it on the desk, and the pages found the place themselves the way they always did. Ivo watched her write. Pell's name, the amount, the hour; and beneath, in the ruled space kept for witnesses, she wrote *I. Marchetti*, and her pen caught on the last stroke.

The ink went from wet to dry faster than ink should. Something in Ivo's hand went cold at the knuckle, then warm, then ordinary.

"What did that do?" he said.

Pell picked up his glass and finished it. "It bound my death to your hand. When I go, the book has to be satisfied you were there to say the debt was paid. If I die and you're not in the room, it won't take me at all."

"And then?"

"Then it goes looking for the nearest name that will answer." The old man set the glass down and looked past him, at Sera. "Ask her whose it is."

## 4. The Day the Ledger Balances

Twelve came off the harbor bell, and the rain stopped with it — all in a breath, as though something overhead had closed a hand. Under the quiet, water still ran: off the fish-shed eaves, off the crane arm, down the iron ladder into the black.

Ivo stood with his shoulders to the wind and the ledger open on the capstan. Pell had not moved from his bollard. Sera had laid the oilcloth aside and set her jaw in the way he had learned to read.

"Read it," she said.

The pages had already found their place. One line, fresh-ruled, in a hand he knew better than his own: *Item — S. Quill, clerk of the book. Favor rendered: the ledger kept hid. Nine years. From the board, from the sessions, from the man himself. Due at balance.*

"Nine years," Ivo said.

"The spring my mother died." She watched the water, not him. "You gave me a key and told me to keep what was in the strongbox dry and not to say what I did with it. You asked nothing for it." A breath. "That's the trouble with a favor that costs you nothing. It grows."

Pell shifted, wool scraping iron. "Whose name answers, if it isn't collected?"

"Mine can't," Ivo said. "I'm the collector."

"Then hers." The old man's mouth worked around the words before he let them go. "A name's the one coin that won't weigh after it's spent. She'll be a clerk with nothing to call her, and she'll keep the book anyway, because the book will be all she has left to hold."

"Then I forgive it."

"You can't forgive one line." Sera turned the page flat with two fingers. "The column stands or falls together. Forgive mine and every line above it goes with it. Tavish. The eel woman. Every soul on that row. You'd be a man with nothing owing and nobody owing — fifty-two years and not a mark on the wall."

"Then they go."

"You'd be nobody."

"I'd be in the room."

Wind pushed at the lamp's glass. Pell took off his hat — Ivo had never seen him do that — and held it against his chest. "The book won't take an empty page," he said. "It wants weight in the margin first. Something of yours."

So Ivo took the pen out of Sera's fingers. She let him, which was answer enough. In the ruled space kept for debtors he wrote: *I. Marchetti. Due in full, this hour.* The ink went cold at his knuckle and stayed cold.

"Now the column."

He crossed the first line, and something went out of him, small and clean as a drawn tooth. A face went with it. He knew there had been a woman gutting mackerel over a board worn to a dish; he could not have said her name, or the color of her hands.

The second line took a shop with a leak in the roof and a man who wept when the sum was read out.

The third took a girl's voice, and after that only the shape of where the voice had been.

Down the column he went. Pell stood with his hat in his hands and said nothing. Sera held the page steady so the paper wouldn't tear. The rain came back out of the dark the way it always did, without asking, and the ink went on drying faster than ink should.

By the last line his hand shook badly enough that he used both.

Hers he forgave last, and it cost him nothing at all, because she was standing in front of him where the book could not reach.

"That's the last of it," he said.

"It's written." She was crying, which he'd have sworn she didn't do.

He sat down on the pier boards with his back against the capstan. The cold went up from his knuckle to his wrist, unhurried, a man walking home. Above the harbor the bell hung silent in its frame.

"Pell. Go home. The port's not holding you now."

"No," the old man said. He put his hat back on and did not move.

"Sera." He found he still knew it. "Shut the book."

Leather met leather. The ledger let out a sound like a long breath let go — the column landing, level at last, every line in its place.

She stayed with him until the tide slapped the pilings twice. Then she walked the north pier with the book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, rain finding her collar, and climbed the customs steps in the dark. At the top she stopped.

An empty ledger is a heavy thing. Heavier than a full one, she found, because a full one tells you what to do. By morning it would want a debtor.

She had already, coming up out of the rain, begun to think of a name.
