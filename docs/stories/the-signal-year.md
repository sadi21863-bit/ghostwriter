# The Signal Year

_Novel · linear · story type: linear · genres: sci-fi mystery · 26073 words_

## 1. BEAT: Inciting Incident

At 11:40 the K-band receiver began to chirp, and Rhea Kest muted it the way you mute a smoke alarm you have decided is toast.

By noon it had company. Forty-one alarms, then the whole console, a chorus you could hear out in the gravel. She stood at the window of the receiver shed with her coffee going cold and watched PEL-1 swing. Twelve meters of dish, twenty-two tons on a bearing she had greased herself in April, and the wind was doing things to it the brakes had never permitted. The feed horn dipped. Rose. Somewhere down in the pedestal a race was complaining in a frequency she felt through the floor more than heard.

"All right," she said, to the room, to the array, and put her palm flat on the console the way you'd steady a horse.

The magnetometer needle had walked off its scale at 11:52, and she'd missed it, because she'd been watching the sun. Nothing to see up there — that was the joke. A sky the color of dishwater, the light half a stop flat, and a hundred and fifty million kilometers away a coronal hole had opened its mouth and spat a billion tons of charged nothing at a rock wearing ninety thousand satellites like cheap jewelry.

Sixteen years of solar weather in the logbooks, and her hand still shook a little as she reached for the handheld. She keyed it.

"Pelham Point to the ferry. Tam. You there?"

Static, then his voice, thin and oversized at once. "I'm here. My radar's got snow on it. Both screens. GPS says we're in the North Atlantic, which is true, so I don't know what to tell you."

"Not you. Everything. The whole constellation's gone dark. Sit still and don't run the crossing."

"I've got fourteen cars on the dock waiting for the nine-forty," he said. "Six of them are Brannigans."

"Then tell the Brannigans to go home."

A pause with a lot of water in it. "You're serious."

"I'm sitting in a shed full of alarms, Tam. I have never been more serious."

She let him go and called Halifax on the satphone, which worked for ninety seconds. The consortium's duty officer got out the word *suspended* before the link folded into a click, and then there was nothing at all, not even the courtesy of a dial tone. The fiber still worked. That was the cruelest part — the cable to the mainland ran cold and perfect under the bay, and there was no one at the other end of it with money.

Nineteen years on this headland. She'd come at twenty-two with a master's in radio astronomy and no idea how much of the job was weatherstripping. She'd built the logbooks herself, green ledger books with cloth spines, and now they were ornaments, the whole observatory was ornaments — the dishes too heavy to lift, the receivers listening to a sky that had gone mute.

Tam came up the hill at two, diesel on his hands, the ferry's handheld clipped to his jacket like a warrant.

"Show me," he said.

She showed him. The spectrum display, the flatline where the geostationary beacons should have stacked up in tidy peaks. The correlator returning noise, noise, noise. Out the window, PEL-1 turning slow circles for no one.

"So it's not broken," he said.

"It's broken in the way a man with no one to talk to is broken. All the parts work."

He wiped his palms on his trousers, looked at the array for a while, looked at the bay. Ash Harbor had four hundred and eleven people, a school with one class per year, and a harbor board that had been arguing for a decade about whether the town belonged to the mainland or to the water. The observatory was the answer they'd liked best. The observatory was the answer they'd paid for.

"Board meets Monday," Tam said. "They'll want to cancel the winter run. No radar, no GPS, no insurance." He said it flatly, an arithmetic he'd already finished. "That's the last line we've got to the rest of the country. After that we're a rock with a generator."

"Then don't cancel it."

"Rhea."

"I'll stay. I'm not going to Halifax in January to teach undergraduates how to boot a telescope." She heard her own voice do something she didn't like, and corrected it. "Someone has to keep the receivers warm. If the band comes back in a week or a month, we'll be the only ears on this coast that know where to point."

Tam took the coffee she hadn't drunk, drank it, made a face, and drank the rest. "Warm costs diesel," he said, and went back down the hill to his fourteen cars.

By seven the wind had dropped and the light had gone the yellow of old varnish, and Rhea sat alone in the shed doing what she had done with every bad night of her adult life, which was to sweep the spectrum and write down what she found. One-eighteen. Three. Seven. Nine megahertz — the bands rolling over, the ionosphere still lit up like a shop window, whistlers singing their falling tones.

Then one line on the display would not flatten.

A carrier, narrow and steady, at 3.7. A band nobody had licensed in forty years. She checked the local oscillator, checked the clock, checked for a ground loop, and it was still there when she finished — a thin bright scratch across the noise, holding.

Rhea wrote the frequency in the ledger, then the time, then her own initials in the margin.

## 2. BEAT: The Dead Frequency

Three nights later the receiver shed smelled of hot dust and burnt rosin, and Rhea was on her knees in it, sorting salvage into three piles: worth cleaning, worth stripping, worth melting. Tam had left the jerrycan by the door without knocking. It made a soft hydraulic sigh whenever the wind pushed the wall.

Nine of the fourteen array feeds had come back to life after the storm. The rest were slag. She had a laptop running off a car battery, a spectrum display she'd built out of the wreckage of two others, and a brass chronometer that had belonged to a man who had taught her what a second was.

The door opened. Tam came in sideways, carrying a crate of stump candles that the co-op had been keeping for the apocalypse, which this was.

"You've got the iron on the floor. That's how sheds burn."

"It's on a tile."

"It's on a paper towel."

Rhea moved the iron. He set the crate down, looked at the three piles, and did the thing he did with any set of objects, which was count it.

"Twenty-two households are asking whether the ferry runs in February," he said. "I told them the truth, which is that the ferry runs if the diesel runs."

"And?"

"And thirteen of them have started walking down to the landing at eight to watch me not run it." He kicked a defunct preamp with the side of his boot. "You hear anything on this junk yet?"

"Radio noise."

"That's all?"

"Radio noise is what the whole country sounds like now." She wiped her hands. "There's a man in Yarmouth with a transmitter and a ham licence, and a coast guard repeater still repeating nothing on the VHF, and after that, static with opinions."

Tam grunted, decided this was survivable, and left her to it. The door banned shut behind him.

---

By ten the wind had gone round to the northeast, and the bands had gone long. Rhea worked her way up from 1.8, writing in the ledger as she went, and the noise rose and fell like something breathing in its sleep.

At 3.7 the pencil stopped.

A carrier, narrow as a hair, dead on frequency and not drifting. She marked it, checked her own clock against the chronometer, found them eleven seconds apart, corrected, and found the carrier still there, still dead on. No sidebands to speak of. No fade. The kind of signal that is either a very good transmitter or a very large lie.

Rhea let it sit and ran a hand-tally for two minutes, and then a third, and the frequency did not move, and then it did something she had not asked it to do, which was to start speaking.

"—and at Ash Harbor, high water at four eleven, one point nine metres. Low water at ten forty-three, zero point five."

She was out of her chair before she'd decided to be. The lead from the headphones pulled taut and clattered the iron back off its tile.

Her own voice. Not a voice like hers. Hers. The flat northern vowel in *Harbor* she had spent nineteen years at conferences sanding off, back again, thick as ever. The pause before *metres*, which she did when she was reading something she'd checked twice. The breath at the wrong place.

She sat back down. Wrote the time down, because the time was a thing she could do. Then played the next minute.

"—four eleven on the fourth. The fourth of February."

Rhea took the headphones off, held them at arm's length, and listened to the room: generator note, wind, a dog barking somewhere down the hill, the small tick of the chronometer. All present. All exactly what they should be. She put them back on.

The voice read the tide through the fourth, then the fifth, then a shipping forecast that described the sea state in terms she'd learned to write in a different decade, and then it stopped, and the carrier went down into the noise like a stone into water.

The fourth of February was twenty-two days away. Nobody on this coast had a tide table for February, because the mainland service that computed them had died with everything else, and the local copies lived in one place, which was a green accounting book in her desk drawer, and that book stopped on the thirty-first of January, and the second half of it was blank.

Rhea pulled the drawer out, put the book on the bench, and opened it to the empty pages. She sharpened a pencil. A tide prediction, done properly, was a sum: nine constituents, their amplitudes and phases taken from years of her own observations, each multiplied by the cosine of its own argument at its own speed, added up to a height. It took her forty minutes by hand and she had done it four hundred times.

It took thirty-eight that night, because her hands wanted to hurry.

When she had the high water for the fourth of February she drew a box around it, and she looked at the box for a while, and then she looked at the number she had written on the flyleaf in pencil when the voice had said it, so that the two would be side by side.

Two forty-seven in the morning. One point eight six metres.

The voice had said four eleven.

Rhea checked her constituents. Checked the chronometer against the shortwave time signal, which was gone, and against the chronometer's own rate, which she had logged since the storm. Then she did the whole sum again from the beginning, on a clean page, with her elbows planted on the desk so her writing would be small and exact, and this time it was forty-one minutes, and this time she came up with seventeen minutes past one.

Not four eleven. Not close to four eleven. A different tide, for a different sea.

She sat with the pencil in her hand until the generator note changed over, and then she wrote at the bottom of the clean page: *3.7, 02:14, noted, R.K.*, and underneath that, in smaller letters, *assume second harmonic constants wrong*, though she did not believe the constants were wrong, and both of them knew it.

## 3. BEAT: The Test

Yellow at the spine, swollen from three winters in an unheated shed, the notebook had only ever held calibration numbers. She wrote in block capitals because block capitals felt like evidence.

**4 FEB. 1612. THE *MAUD* LOSES HER ENGINE. T. OKONKWO SAYS — “Christ’s tits, not the bloody impeller again” —**

Impeller or impulse: she played the segment back six times, headphones pinched against her skull, the tape hiss layered under it like weather. It was impeller. The voice reading it was hers, down to the flat vowel she had tried to lose at university and never had.

Below the block capitals she wrote, smaller: ACTION — NONE. OBSERVE ONLY. Then she sat back and looked at the word *observe* until the lamp battery sagged.

Before dawn she carried the page down to the pier, sealed it in a manila envelope, wrote the date and the hour across the gummed flap, and locked it in the tide-gauge cabinet, where anybody with a key could break the seal and nobody had a reason to.

---

Sunday the *Maud* sat on the hardstand with her stern in the mud, and the hardstand smelled of diesel and cold weed. Rhea came down the ramp with a thermos she did not want to drink. Tam Okonkwo came out of the engine well wiping his hands on his thighs, which was as clean as he ever got.

“Mail boat’s not until spring,” he said.

“I know.”

“Then you’re here to look at my diesel.”

“Mostly,” she said.

The tank sat under the wheelhouse in a cradle of rust, and the gauge on it was a broom handle dipped and read against a scratch on the doorframe. Tam saw her see it and laughed without any pleasure in it.

“Four hundred and ten litres,” he said. “Which sounds like a number until you know the pump’s doing a litre every fifty minutes for the town, and then it stops sounding like a number.”

“How’s she running?”

“Sweet as anything.” He patted the hull. “Impeller’s the weak spot. Rubber, and it’s older than my nephew. But it’ll go when it goes.”

She had the sentence in her mouth. *Change the impeller this week.* It sat there, correct and warm and five words long, and behind it she could see the envelope in the cabinet with its clean seal, and the four hundred counts she’d have to unpick if the ferry ran fine to April. Under that, worse: two hundred and eight people who had lost their satellites, their weather, their mainland, and would now be told the future had a leak in it.

“It’ll go when it goes,” she agreed.

Tam looked at her a beat too long. “You all right, Kest?”

Tuesday, the third, she slept four hours and did not dream.

---

On the fourth she was on the breakwater at half past three with the good binoculars and the wind cutting round the head, and she told herself she was doing an experiment, which was a word she had been using since she was nine to mean *I am frightened and I would like a procedure.*

The *Maud* left the slip at ten past four with four passengers and a crate of feed. Rhea tracked her out past the green can and watched the white of the wake flatten and go wrong — the boat losing way, the bow swinging off the swell, the note of the engine climbing and then stopping dead.

She looked at her watch. 4:12.

Sound crossed the water clean as a wire. Two hundred metres of cold air and it carried everything.

“—Christ’s tits,” Tam’s voice came across, huge and plain, “not the bloody impeller again—”

The exact words. The exact order. She had them in a notebook under seal and in a cabinet with a lock on it, and they had arrived the same either way, and the arriving did not feel like proof. It felt like being handed a key to a house she was already standing inside.

Her hands were shaking on the barrels of the binoculars. She lowered them. On the water the *Maud* slewed broadside, and one of the passengers was already out of the wheelhouse with a boathook, and Tam was leaning over the stern with his arm in the wet up to the elbow.

Then he straightened and looked along the breakwater, straight up at her, and even at that distance she could see the shape of it — the arm still dripping, the long flat gaze of a man doing arithmetic.

The radio in her coat pocket came alive. *“Kest. Get off that rock and come down.”*

She climbed down with the wind at her back, and by the time she reached the slip the feed crate was on the stones and Tam was coiling a line, unhurried, the way he did everything, and the passengers were laughing the laugh that comes after.

“Six days,” he said, not looking up.

“Sorry?”

“You’ve been on that point six afternoons running with those glasses. Watching my boat.” He laid the coil down and stood with his hands loose at his sides. “So I’ll ask it plain. What are you expecting to see?”

## 4. BEAT: The First Turn

The ammeter read 4.1 amps. Rhea had her palm flat on the feed cable, feeling for the warmth that meant the splice was holding, when the shed door banged open and let the weather in.

Tam Okonkwo stood in the frame with a torch he wasn't using. Behind him, past the yard, the mast light on the point swung its slow red eye.

"Two forty," he said.

"I can read a clock."

"Since the storm your lines in my book have run long. Four nights running." He stepped in and pulled the door shut, which was worse than shouting. "Kelleher's cold store goes off at two. The houses are dark by eleven. Something on this point is drinking my diesel between two and four in the morning, and the only other things on this point are you and a dish the size of a tennis court."

Graphite on her palm. She wiped it down her coat and left a grey streak. "Receiver tests."

"Tests." He walked the bench, past the stripped chassis, the coffee tin of screws, the log she hadn't known he kept in duplicate. "You stood up in the hall and told forty people the array was dead."

"It is dead."

"Then what's the transmitter for?"

She could have held the lie. He had diesel figures and a grievance and no key to the cabinet she kept padlocked, and a week of saying nothing would have bought her a week of quiet. Instead she hooked the stool out with her foot and set it down in front of him.

"Sit," she said. "I'm going to play you ninety seconds. Then I'm going to want that pencil."

He sat, which surprised her. She threaded the cassette — a salvaged ferry-office deck whose capstan ran a half-tone slow — and pushed the lever down.

Her own voice came out of the little speaker, flat and unhurried, the voice she used for the recorder, because reading aloud made her feel ridiculous and flattening it helped.

*—high water at Ash Harbor, oh four nineteen, on the fourteenth. Causeway clear between oh six and oh nine. Ferry runs weather permitting, eleven forty, four aboard and a dog—*

Tam's face did not move. Rhea watched his hands instead, the way they lay on his knees, the way the left one had begun to turn the pencil end over end.

*—and the tank at the ferry shed is down to four hundred and ten litres, so he'll want to be careful with the cold store—*

The reel clicked round twice and she lifted the arm.

Wind worried at the shed roof. Tam looked at the deck, then at the log book, then at her, and his eyes had the flat, patient quality of a man checking a sum from the bottom.

"Four ten," he said.

"Six days ago it said four thirty. I wrote it in the book. I wrote the date next to it." She heard her own voice go thin. "I've never been inside your yard. I don't know how much diesel you have. I know now, because I heard myself say it before it was true."

"How's it your voice?"

"I don't know yet."

He turned the pencil once more, took the log from the bench, flipped past the ruled pages to the blank back, and began to write. Figures, in his small upright hand. 410, underscored. A column. A second column beside it.

"Six litres an hour when it's pushing," Rhea said. "I've measured it. That's the price of about twenty minutes of listening."

"Five point eight." He didn't look up. "You're running it lean. Cold store's twelve an hour, three nights a week. Eleven houses on the grid, average four." A line drawn, a circle. "I can give you two hours a night and hold the houses, if Kelleher takes his fish out Thursday instead of Monday."

"Kelleher won't."

"Kelleher will when I tell him what's in the tank." He wrote a number and underlined it twice. "That's eight weeks. After that the tank's dry and no lorry's coming, because there's no satellite telling the mainland we're short."

Rhea put both hands flat on the bench. The set ticked as it cooled.

"You're not going to ask me if it's true?" she said.

"I heard my tank." He squared the book, then turned it round so she could see what he'd done — the columns, the circles, the two numbers at the foot of the page. "You want me to be frightened of it. I'll get to frightened. Right now I've got four hundred and ten litres and a woman on my generator asking me for the future." He tapped the pencil twice on the paper. "So what I need to know is what we turn off, and I need it by morning, because whatever's in that cold store goes rotten on Sunday either way."

## 5. BEAT: Deepening

The catalogue had six columns the first night and nine by the fourth, which told Rhea more about the direction of travel than anything she wrote in them.

TIME. FREQ. CONTENT. VERIFIED. SOURCE. NOTES, in pencil, sharpened down past the printing. Then, on the Tuesday, RESPONSE — an empty column ruled out of superstition and left standing out of something she wasn't ready to name.

Two hours. Tam had budgeted her two and two was what she took, 21:10 to 23:10, the shed cold enough that she kept her coat on indoors and worked the pencil down to a stub.

Night one read the tide tables for the fourteenth, fifteenth and sixteenth. She checked them against her own slip and they matched to the minute, the third one included, which she had not yet finished calculating and finished afterwards with a bad taste in her mouth.

Night two gave a shipping forecast for a sea area abolished in 1984, in her own voice, in a register her own voice had never once used inside her own head.

Night three read out fourteen boat names alphabetically and said which were hauled and which were not. Fourteen for fourteen.

Night four had her reaching for the mug before the sentence finished.

"—and she's got the cracked one tonight. Ferry office mug. Chip at the seven o'clock position. Tea gone cold because she keeps forgetting her hands are doing something."

Rhea turned the mug. The chip sat at seven o'clock.

"It's a guess," she told the set. "Half this harbor owns a chipped mug."

The set said nothing. The needle sat at rest. She wrote RESPONSE in the column and left the field blank, and went home, and lay awake doing arithmetic on coincidence.

Thursday Tam came down at four with the fish box and his fuel log and stood dripping in the door.

"Kelleher took his fish out Thursday," he said.

"He told me he wouldn't."

"He told me a lot of things standing next to a man with a fuel log." He wiped his boots a second time, which he only did when he had more to say. "You're sleeping out here."

"I'm listening out here. There's a difference."

"There's a camp bed in the corner and it's got your shape in it." He set the log on the bench. "Two hours is two hours, Rhea. I'm not the one comes round checking."

She poured him tea in the good mug and kept the cracked one for herself, and they stood either side of the bench the way they had for years, and there was a thing she had been carrying since the eleventh that had worn a hole in her pocket, and she opened her mouth to set it down on the bench between them.

"Tam—"

"Don't." Fast, and then he looked sorry for it. "Don't do it in a shed with the wind in the door. Do it somewhere better."

Then he was gone up the track with the light going.

Night five said: *there's a moth working the lamp. She'll leave it till morning. She always does.*

The lamp glass was empty. Rhea looked at it for a long time. At 22:41 a moth came out of the dark behind the shelving and began knocking itself against the bulb, and she sat with her hands in her lap and did not move.

Night six said: *he stood in the door for four seconds and she let him go. Nine days now. She's still got it in her pocket.*

She read the entry back twice, then turned the page and wrote nothing for a while.

The whole of the following morning she spent pretending it was a recording. Recordings were the only thing that didn't frighten her. A recording could be an echo, a bounce, some duct in the ionosphere folding a transmission back on itself; the future was a computation she could run on the back of an envelope and get wrong by half a day. But a recording could not have known about the moth before the moth, and it could not have counted nine days unless it had been counting along with her.

So she tested it the only way she could think of. She finished her tea, turned the cracked mug upside down on the receiver lid with the chip at three o'clock, and went home.

Night seven, 21:10, carrier steady: *—mug's upside down on the lid. She's left it there like a flag on a hill.*

Rhea sat back and listened to the shed's own silence.

"That's not a recording," she said.

*No*, said the frequency. *It isn't.*

She looked at the column she had ruled for RESPONSE and had still not filled. Then she was standing before she had decided to stand. The transmitter key sat under a tin lid, warm from the valve behind it, and the diesel budget for it had been set by a man who had asked her for the truth and been given ninety seconds of it.

Her hand went out anyway.

"I'm not spending Kelleher's Thursday on this," she said, and lifted the lid, and the meter came up off the pin like something startled. "Twenty words. Twenty words and I shut it down."

She keyed the microphone, and her voice came out steadier than she was.

"Say something a tape couldn't say."

The reply arrived before her thumb came off the key.

## 6. BEAT: Midpoint Shift

--- CHAPTER 6 ---

Nine columns. A tenth ruled and left blank, which was cowardice dressed as methodology, and she had stopped pretending otherwise.

She put the question to the frequency that she had been circling for four nights.

“Where are you standing?”

Static. Then: *Where you’re sitting.*

“Describe it.”

*A gull’s got into the habit of the roof beam. The array’s on its face in the grass — half of it. The rest went into the ditch the winter the wind came through the north window and took the frame with it.*

Rhea looked at the north window. Whole. Streaming with rain.

*Your mug’s upside down on the receiver lid. Chip at three o’clock. I’ve never moved it.*

She had turned it over that morning. Turned it over again now, and the tea ring sat in the bottom of it like a struck crater, and she did the arithmetic on the milk and did not like the answer.

“What year is it?”

*I can’t say it in a way that helps you.*

“Try.”

*It’s the same year. It’s the same night. I’m just at the far end of it.*

Both hands went flat on the bench. The receiver hummed under her palms at fifty hertz — town mains, Tam’s generator two miles off behind the seawall — and some animal part of her wanted to switch it down, walk home, unlearn the last month.

“Who are you?”

*You know who I am. You’ve got my handwriting.*

She looked at the ninth column. Her capital S’s. Her father’s habit of crossing a seven. The pencil had been in her hand for every line of it, and she remembered writing them, and the remembering felt like a room she had only ever been shown a photograph of.

“You’re me.”

*I’m the one who’s still here.*

“Sorrel,” Rhea said, and it came out flat, a reading off an instrument.

*Yes.*

She let it sit. The valve behind the transmitter ticked as it cooled.

*You’re going to write all of it down*, Sorrel said. *Every column. The tide table, Tam’s engine, the Brannigans’ — all of it. And then the storm will take the roof off and the diesel will be gone and everyone will have gone up the coast, and you’ll sit in that chair with the notebooks and a valve you’ve nursed three winters, and you’ll start sending.*

“Sending where?”

*Back. Sideways. I don’t have the mathematics of it and neither will you — you’ll have a guess and a great many hours and a dead town. But nothing I’ve said to you came out of a machine. I said it.* A wash of static, rolled through and out like surf. *You’ve been calling me a recording because a recording doesn’t cost anything.*

“What does it cost?”

*Heat. Light. The tank.*

“Whose tank?”

*You know whose tank.*

She did. She had known since the night three houses went dark and Tam walked the seawall with a torch, counting windows and saying nothing about it. The exchange rate was written in the diesel ledger in his hard block capitals, and in her own soft hand alongside.

“I haven’t decided anything,” Rhea said.

*You have. I’m the decision. I’m just further along it.*

The carrier thinned, came back. She wiped her palms on her trousers, picked up the pencil, turned to the tenth column, and sat with it.

Six columns the first night. Nine by the fourth. The tenth had no name because she had been calling it RESPONSE in her head and there had been nothing to respond to, and now the naming was the entire problem. She wrote, in her father’s seven:

TO SEND.

The first entry under it she wrote slowly, saying it aloud as she went, because that was how she had always worked a proof — out once, then check it.

“Tide table, the twenty-third, corrected. Pelham Point. High water, 04:41.”

*Good*, said Sorrel. *That’s the first one I heard.*

Rhea’s hand stopped. “Heard. Past tense.”

*Everything you send, I’ll have heard. It’s the only reason I can say any of this to you at all. You’re not intercepting the future. You’re on a call, and I’m the far end, and you’ve been picking up the receiver and hanging it up again for a month.*

Rain hit the north window in a gust. The mug sat upside down on the lid, chip at three, exactly where it would be many winters from now, when the frame was out and the array lay in the ditch and a gull had learned the roof beam.

“So I become you,” she said.

*You become the woman in the cold shed. There’s no other way to be me.*

Rhea lifted the tin lid off the key. Diesel was Kelleher’s, and the hour was Tam’s, and the words, it turned out, had been hers the whole time — which meant she could spend them.

“All right,” she said, and keyed the microphone. “Tide table, the twenty-third, corrected. Pelham Point. High water, 04:41.”

The reply came back clean, no wash, no static, in her own voice the way her own voice sounded inside her skull and never on tape.

*Now the storm*, Sorrel said. *Do the storm next.*

Rhea turned to the entry she had written four nights ago and had never once been able to make come out right.

## 7. BEAT: The Cost

The clamp meter went around the feeder at 22:10 and read 11.3 amps. By half past it read 9.8. Rhea wrote both figures in the tenth column and left the heading blank, because she did not yet have a word that wouldn't flatter her.

Three nights of numbers made the shape plain. A minute of clean reception on 3.7 MHz cost between 1.4 and 1.7 kilowatt-hours above the shed's baseline — drafty, irregular, the way anything measured in weather is irregular. Fourteen minutes of Sorrel, and the feeder had shed load somewhere down the line. Ash Harbor's lights did not flicker. They simply went out in the houses whose wiring was oldest and whose thermostats drew hardest, relay by relay, the way the grid had always chosen, indifferent and correct.

Tam came at six with the fuel log under his arm and did not sit down.

"Eleven hours Sunday," he said. "Nine Monday. Eight Tuesday."

"Storm coming. You ran the ferry extra."

"I ran the ferry extra for two of those." He put the sheet on the bench and squared it with two fingers. "January average is six. Rhea, I can do arithmetic in my head and I don't like what it says."

"Say it out loud anyway."

"Every hour above six is a litre and a half I won't have in March." His eyes went to the ammeter, then to the transmitter, then back. "And it's not just the diesel. The load's got to come off the feeder somewhere. You know the branch layout better than I do."

She did. The shed shared a spur with the Corrigan house, the Prewitt place, and the Doyles' cottage — the three coldest buildings on the point, the three with the oldest cable in the walls. She had known it since the first night she keyed the microphone.

"I know," she said.

"That's the part that keeps me up." He pulled his cap off, put it back on. "What are you buying?"

"Tomorrow's weather. Thursday's, if I ask."

"Tomorrow's weather." A short breath through the nose. "You can look out the window like the rest of us."

"Not twelve days out. Not the veer."

## 9. BEAT: Dark Night

The cast ran at 22:40, six minutes late, and the carrier took nine seconds to find lock.

## 12. BEAT: Dead Air

The hall was quiet the way a hall gets quiet when the quiet is doing work. Thirty-nine chairs filled. The radiator under the window put out nothing at all.

He read down through October, the numbers getting smaller until the smallness was the argument. Then November.

"November fourth. Twenty-two ten to twenty-three oh five. Fifty-five minutes drawn at the outside feeder, eleven point three amps, and no appliance in this town on that circuit." He looked up. "That's the transmitter. That's the shed on the point. There's the Brannigans' lights the same night, and Wold's freezer, and I'm not going to pretend I don't know whose name goes on that line."

Rhea stood before she had decided to. Her knees were stiff from the cold of the chair.

"There's a frequency," she said. "Three point seven megahertz. Nothing's licensed there. Nothing has been for forty years." Someone in the third row shifted. She kept going. "Three weeks ago I heard the tide tables on it, for a date that hadn't happened, and the tide in them was one I hadn't calculated yet. I wrote down the next thing it said. Four twelve in the afternoon, the ferry engine dying, and Tam saying a word I won't repeat in a hall. It happened at four twelve. He said the word."

"Then it's a warning," said Nell Cusk from the back, and there was hope in it, which was worse than doubt.

"It's me," Rhea said.

The stove ticked. Nobody moved.

"It's my voice. Older. It comes from this point, from my shed, and I would rather tell you that than be caught keeping it. It costs to hear it — that's the eleven point three amps, that's your lights, that's the Brannigans' boy eating cold supper. I told them about the causeway and the tide, and the broadcast afterward was different. The drowning gone. I thought I'd broken it."

She put her hands flat on the back of the chair in front of her.

"It didn't break. It took something else out instead. There's an hour in March now where the order to clear this town is never given, and the voice on that tape comes apart in the middle of a sentence, and I don't know if I did that or if I only heard it."

"A minute of it," said Aggie Brannigan. "Play us a minute."

"I can't. The receiver's cold. It takes diesel to make it warm and I've taken enough of yours."

Tam closed the ledger. "Then that's the shape of it. She's not a witch and she's not a saint, she's a woman with a radio and a bill. I'll take a motion."

What the hall did then was not exile and it was not forgiveness. It was arithmetic, done by people who had walked eleven miles in the rain and come back to a town with no heat.

Forecasts in exchange for firewood. One hour at the receiver, no more, drawn against her own ration and not the town's. Nothing said on the frequency without a witness in the shed. The catalogue kept in the hall, in the tin box with the deeds, open to any hand that wanted it.

"Aggie," Tam said. "You've got the boy and no man and a stove that draws like a chimney. You'll stand the first watches."

Aggie Brannigan folded her arms and looked at Rhea the way you look at weather coming. "I'll want it understood that I'm not sitting in that shed because I've forgiven you. I'm sitting in it because you saved my Kevin and I'd like to know what he cost."

"That's fair," Rhea said.

"It's not fair. It's accounting."

The motion passed the way everything passed in Ash Harbor: by nobody objecting loudly enough to be remembered. Chairs went back against the wall. Somebody opened the stove door to show how little was in it, and the small orange of it made the whole room look like a photograph taken at the wrong exposure.

Rhea got her coat on. Her hands were not steady, and she put them in her pockets to hide it.

"One thing," said a man near the door — Wold, the freezer, the one who had a generator and pretended he didn't. "If it's tomorrow's news you're buying with our wood, ask it who's next."

The hall did not hush. It waited, which was different, and worse.

"That's not a forecast," Rhea said.

"Ask it anyway."

She looked at Tam. Tam looked at the ledger in his hand, the spine worn pale where his thumb went every night, and he did not help her, and she understood that he had handed her the room and meant her to keep it, and that the keeping was the price of the key in his coat pocket.

## 15. BEAT: The Second Voice

--- CHAPTER 15 ---

Aggie Brannigan brought her own kitchen timer to the shed and set it down where the flux tin used to live. Eight minutes, she said; the hall had counted it out. Then she sat with her coat buttoned to the throat, a pencil, and the back of an envelope, and the ticking got into the room worse than the wind did.

Rhea keyed up at 20:59 and let the carrier settle. The dial lamp flickered now. It had been flickering for a week, and she had stopped pretending otherwise. She read the tide. She read the wind backing north, the glass falling, the ice report she had nothing to add to. Then she said, "Kest, Pelham Point. Listening," and let the switch go.

Nothing came back. Escapement, pencil, the stove ticking as it cooled.

At two minutes past, a carrier rose underneath hers, badly tuned, somebody holding the key down a beat too long, the way people do who have only ever seen it done in films.

"—reading," a girl's voice said. "Am I reading. Right. Cape Hobb light, calling. Cape Hobb light."

Rhea put her palm flat on the bench. "Cape Hobb, Pelham Point. You're loud and clear. Who's this?"

"Nell Candless. I'm the watchstander." Something thumped over — a door, maybe a boot on a locker. "I'm nineteen. There's nobody else in the house tonight, so don't ask."

"How long have you been on this frequency?"

"Since the storm. My fog-signal set won't do anything else now, so I turn the dial when the light's done rotating. It's the only thing up here that talks."

"How many nights have you been listening to me?"

A pause with a shape to it. "Eleven," Nell said. "Twelve, if you count the one where I fell asleep."

"Why didn't you answer before?"

"Because the first night you sounded like a person reading to nobody. I thought if I said something you'd stop."

Aggie's pencil moved. Rhea did not look at her.

"Where's your keeper?" Rhea said.

"Falmouth, since October. It's me and the standby battery and the light, and the light doesn't need much." The girl's voice shifted, fumbled for a register, came back businesslike. "Here's my question and then I'll let you go. I've got a boat and eleven people and they want to run south before the ice. Tell me the third week in March and I'll tell them something true."

"I don't sell what I don't have," Rhea said. "Give me till Thursday."

"Thursday." Scribbling, faint, on Nell's end too. "There's others, you know. On here. There's a man down at Brant Rock who never says a word, he just keys twice and waits, and if you key twice back he keys three times. And there's a woman on the bay who reads names out. She's read the same forty names every night since Christmas."

The carrier dipped. Another came up beside it, thin and reedy, a woman's voice already halfway down a list — Aldous, Margaret, Frensham, little Frensham, the one that fished — and gone again before Rhea could get her callsign in edgewise.

"Now it's a party line," Rhea said, mostly to herself.

"It's been a party line," Nell said. "You were just the only one with the manners to introduce yourself."

The timer rang.

Rhea's hand went to the key.

"Pelham Point, wait—" Nell's voice jumped, then steadied itself with visible effort. "How many are you? Nobody up here will believe a number unless I bring one back with me."

Aggie leaned across the bench. Her sleeve smelled of the hall stove, coal and wet wool. She put her mouth close to the microphone and held the timer up where Rhea could see it, still ringing.

"Forty-one," she said. "And you can tell them the price of a forecast went up this evening. We've discovered there's a queue."

The carrier stayed open. Somewhere behind Nell Candless a shutter worked in the wind. She wrote something down. Rhea listened to a nineteen-year-old copying out a number in pencil, a number she would have to carry up the tower stairs and defend in the morning to eleven people who had sent her there to be useful.

## 16. BEAT: The Exchange Rate

Aggie’s kitchen timer rang at 21:12, and Rhea let it ring twice before she keyed the carrier open. The nine o’clock had run long; the frequency was still warm with somebody else’s traffic, a man at Brant Rock keying twice and waiting, then three times when she keyed back. At 21:16 Cape Hobb came up, four minutes late, and the watchstander had a cold in her voice.

“Pelham Point. Cape Hobb. I’ve got a question and a price.”

“Go ahead.” Rhea pulled the log close.

“The light’s on a five-gallon drum and a prayer. I need the wind. When does it back? If I can get the drum boat out before the swell, I can pay you.”

“Pay me what?”

“Forecasts. It’s all we’ve got. Diesel’s for the light.”

Rhea wrote Cape Hobb — wind? in the margin. The ammeter sat at 4.1. “Wind backs nor’west at 03:20. Two-foot sea by first light. Holds till Thursday noon, then veers. Don’t cross after Thursday noon.”

Pencil scratch. “Three-twenty.”

“Three-twenty.”

“Say it again.”

She said it again. The timer clicked to eighteen minutes. The girl repeated the numbers back, and Rhea corrected her on the veer—Thursday noon, not Thursday night—and the carrier held. When it closed, the ammeter had climbed to 11.6 and the shed bulb had gone the color of weak tea.

Cape Hobb’s diesel price, it turned out, was forecasts. Rhea had expected that. What she had not expected was the way the girl said thank you, quick and formal, as if a forecast were something you could put in a drum.

The transmitter needed 11.6 amps, and the shed’s circuit was rated for eight. Rhea had known that before she keyed. She went out into the cold with the log under her arm, crossed the gravel to the generator shed, and opened the breaker panel. Eleven circuits, eleven paper tags in Tam’s block capitals. Number seven said BRANNIGAN ROW.

She pulled it. The relay chunked. Across the road, three windows went dark again, one after another, like somebody blowing out candles in a room. Rhea wrote on the tag with the grease pencil: R. KEST 21:16–21:44. Then she stood there long enough for the hall stove to tick.

The door opened behind her.

Aggie Brannigan had a flashlight and her coat on over her nightgown. She did not shine the light in Rhea’s face.

## 17. BEAT: Spring Tide

--- CHAPTER 17 ---

By nine the harbor had lost its birds. They had gone off north before light, the whole flock of them, and the boathouse ridge stood empty the way a table stands after a meal.

Rhea went down Brannigan Row with the transcript folded to the size of a card. She had read it eleven times — enough that the words had stopped meaning and become an object she took out of her pocket and looked at and put away.

Aggie answered on the second knock, aproned, unimpressed. “It isn’t eleven yet.”

“High water’s at eleven-oh-four.”

“High water’s been at eleven-oh-four every spring of my life, Rhea, and it has not once come in my door.”

“Not like this.”

Aggie studied her. Then she went for her coat, and for the timer off the kitchen sill, and stood in the doorway a moment with it in her hand as though weighing whether to bring it — and pocketed it, which Rhea understood was the nearest thing to a vote she was going to get.

By ten the Methodist hall had forty in it. Tam counted them twice, moving his lips. “Forty. And nobody’s dog.”

“The dogs went with the birds.”

“That’s not funny.”

“It wasn’t meant to be.”

At ten-forty the two Brannigan boys came down the track toward the boathouse with a tarpaulin over their shoulders. Tam went after them at a run. There was shouting on the shingle that carried all the way up the rise, and then the boys came back up with their hands empty and their faces the color of the sky.

Water reached the causeway coping at 11:02.

Rhea watched it through Tam’s binoculars from the wall. Two minutes early, she thought — and then the thought corrected itself: the sheet had never given the time the water would touch the coping. It had given 11:04 and it had given what would go. At 11:04 the causeway went.

It went the way a thing goes when it has been argued with too long. The middle thirty feet lifted, cracked along a seam nobody had ever noticed, and slid off into eighteen inches of brown water already running over the road to the doors. The boathouse came off its blocks shortly after — whole at first, riding — and then not whole. Somebody in the hall began to pray and somebody else told them to stop, and then stopped themselves.

The array died quietly. From the headland it was a set of lines on a marsh; the wind was doing things to the shed roof; the guy wires leaned and one of them sang. Then the footings came out of the soft ground, all six together, like teeth out of a gum, and the frame lay down sideways into the salt grass with a sound that reached them half a second late. A long mineral groan. Nothing dramatic.

Forty people. Rhea counted them herself, standing at the hall door with her back to the water, and got forty, and did it again.

“Don’t get holy about it,” Tam said, later, on the shingle. The tide had gone off and left the causeway in pieces and the boathouse in the meadow, upright, ridiculous, its door swung open on a room that had been a room that morning. “Forty people are alive because you stood up in a hall and read a piece of paper out loud. That’s all it is.”

“It listed the boathouse.”

“It listed the boathouse.” He jerked his chin at the meadow. “It’s standing right there.”

“I read it out and the town moved. That’s the whole of it.” She turned her collar up. “I saved the boathouse too. It’s just in the wrong place.”

Tam laughed once, without any humor in it, and then looked at the water a while. “Causeway’s gone,” he said, in the flat voice he used for arithmetic. “Fennick’s Cut will go next hard rain. Ferry’s on the hard till I can get a gang down the slip.”

At the shed, the floor had a tide-line on it four inches up the shelving. The receiver sat in it, dead. The ammeter, when she keyed the carrier, climbed to 11.6 and held, steady as a pulse.

She sat with that for a while. The transcript was still folded to a card in her coat pocket, still in the present tense — the boathouse stands, the causeway carries, the array receives. Nobody left to send a correction.

At nine she keyed up and called Cape Hobb, and called again at nine-oh-five, and the needle moved and the meter held and nothing came back. Not the girl, not the carrier, not the hiss the receiver made when it was hearing weather. She put the phones down.

“Well?” Tam said from the doorway.

“The transmitter’s fine.” Rhea wiped her palms on her trousers. “I can still talk.”

“And?”

“There’s nothing to talk into. The array was the ear. It’s in the marsh with the gulls.” The housings, the feedline, the nineteen years of it, all lying down in the salt grass. She looked at the grease-penciled tags over the breaker panel, then at the empty ring of clean paint on the shelf where Aggie’s timer had stood.

Tam came in and stood dripping on the tide-line. “So what’s tall enough?”

Rhea was already taking her coat off the hook.

## 18. BEAT: What Stands

“Twenty metres,” Rhea said. “Give or take a hand.”

Tam tilted his head back and considered the shed roof as though the number were written on it. Outside, the harbor was doing its new trick — sucking at the shingle with a longer reach than it used to have, dragging back over ground that had been dry road in October.

She had spent the morning pacing a baseline along the hard sand with a surveyor’s chain she’d found in the boathouse loft, and the rest of it sighting up a stick with her eye against the transit case, because the transit itself was under a foot of water. A quarter wave at 3.7 megahertz wanted twenty metres of conductor in the air. Give or take a hand. Two hundred and nineteen paces of beach, a stick, a pencil, and a woman who had once argued with a postdoc about the refractive index of wet air.

The foremast of the *Aggie B* measured twenty-one point four to the truck. She’d taken the waterline off it twice to be sure.

“You want my mast,” Tam said.

“I want a mast. Yours is the only one standing.”

“There’s the church.”

“Nine metres, and it’s on the wrong side of the headland. Nobody’s listening west.” She unrolled the chart on the bench, weighting the corners with a flux tin and a package of fuses. Someone had drawn a smiley face on a fuse box in permanent marker years ago; she turned that corner away from her. “A quarter wave with a counterpoise in salt water — the marsh itself is a ground plane. That was the whole point of Pelham Point. It’s still the whole point.”

“Except the point is underwater.”

“Except that.”

He walked the length of the shed and back. Dripping again — he was always dripping now. Since the causeway went he smelled of diesel and cold iron and the flat green rot of the slip, and he stood in doorways the way a man stands when he has decided not to sit down.

“Say I say yes,” he said. “Where’s it go?”

Rhea put her finger on the north footing.

The surge had taken the array flat — nineteen elements laid down in the salt grass like a fence somebody had stopped bothering with, feedline peeled out of the conduit in curls — but the north footing had come through. Cracked at the shoulder, the anchor bolts eaten back to half-moons, standing in standing water. It had been poured in 1978 by a contractor who was owed money and had made up for it in cement.

“Bolt the mast to that,” she said. “Guy it off to the anchor points. The footing’s already in the ground, and the ground’s already in the water.”

“That footing’s not a mast step, it’s a—” He stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. “Rhea. The boat’s the last thing.”

“I know what the boat is.”

“No. Say it out loud, because I don’t think you’ve said it out loud.”

The stove ticked. Out past the slip, the *Aggie B* swung on her mooring in the leftover swell, foremast drawing a small clean arc against the grey — twenty-one point four metres of douglas fir, stepped in 1954, that had held a riding light through nine winters of his family’s ownership.

“The boat’s the only way off the headland,” Rhea said. “Fennick’s Cut’s gone, the road north is a rumour, and there’s forty-one of us on a rock that gets smaller every tide.”

“And?”

“And the transmitter’s the only way to see forward.” She flattened her palm on the chart. “It’s the same object, Tam. That’s not a metaphor. It’s one piece of wood doing two jobs.”

Tam put both hands on the bench and leaned. The bench creaked. He looked at the chart the way he looked at the diesel ledger — figuring, not arguing, the numbers moving behind his eyes in a queue.

“If she goes aground,” he said, “she’s still a boat.” He said it slowly, testing each word against a weight. “Boat aground at slack, on sand — that’s a bad week and a lot of pumping. Boat under the loader with her mast out, dragged up the beach to Pelham Point, that’s a boat no more.”

“Yes.”

“And if I want the mast out, I’ve got to get her over the shoal, because I’m not stepping a twenty-metre spar off a deck that’s rolling in eight feet of swell inside the harbor mouth.” His finger came down on the chart, north of the slip. “Out there. Where the swell’s still cleaning itself up from the surge.”

“Yes.”

“In a boat with a thirty-one-year-old engine that’s had salt through the injectors.”

“Yes.”

He straightened. Something in his face had gone past worry into the flatter country on the other side of an arithmetic he had already finished.

“Then we do it Thursday,” he said. “Slack’s at 06:40. I’ll want eight bodies on the falls and someone on the pump who isn’t frightened of it.”

“Aggie Brannigan’s not frightened of anything.”

“Aggie Brannigan is eighty-one.”

“Aggie Brannigan is not frightened of anything,” Rhea said again, and got half a breath of a laugh out of him — the first in a week, and it went out of him as fast as it came.

He picked his coat off the hook and stood with it over his arm, not putting it on.

“I’ll go up the mast,” he said. “You’ll be in the shed with your meter, telling me when the carrier’s clean.”

“Tam—”

“I’ve been up her a hundred times. You haven’t been up her once.”

There was a thing she had not said to him. It had been sitting behind her sternum since the night of the frost and the coal bill, and it had grown a little each week, the way the marsh had grown over the array. She looked at his hands — split across the knuckle, tar in the creases, one thumbnail going black from a hammer in November — and she folded the chart instead.

“Six-forty,” she said. “And I’ll want the stays measured off before we go. If she comes down on the shoal with the mast stepped, she’ll roll the footing over, and we’ll have a boat and no spine at all.”

“Then measure them tonight.” Tam pulled the door and the cold came in flat and salt. “And Rhea — while you’re measuring. Put it in the log where the mast has to go, so when it’s done, somebody who isn’t us can look at it and know it was on purpose.”

He went out. His boots rang on the slip, then didn’t, and the harbor took the sound back.

Rhea unrolled the chart again, put a pencil on the north footing, and drew the only line she had left — twenty metres and a hand, straight up out of water that was already rising.

## 19. BEAT: The Foremast

The tide turned at 06:58. Tam had the ferry’s engine turning over by 06:40, which Rhea counted as the first thing in nine days to arrive early.

“Stays,” she called across the gap. “Both aft, and the running backstay off the quarter. I want her stiff before you put weight in that chair.”

Tam leaned out of the wheelhouse with a coil already over his shoulder. “You want her stiff, come aboard and feel it.”

“I’ll feel it from the shed.”

“From the shed.” He spat over the side. “Nineteen years looking at the sky through a wire, and you won’t put a foot on a deck.”

Rhea raised the clamp meter and shook it at him. “This is the foot.”

He laughed, properly this time, the sound carrying flat over the water, and went back to the wheel.

Aggie Brannigan had come down to hold the bow line. She took the turn around the bollard with her weight low, then stood looking at the swell the way a woman looks at a bill.

“There’s a sea under her,” Aggie said.

“Leftover,” said Rhea. “Three days of it and no wind left to drive it. It’s the tide-set that will bite, not the swell.”

“It’s the swell that will lift her.”

That was true enough to leave alone. Rhea walked back up the slip with the chart tube under her arm and the feed coil over her shoulder, and she did not look around at the harbor, because looking around at the harbor had become a way of not doing arithmetic.

The plan was a plan because it had numbers in it. Slack at 06:58. The ferry over the north footing while there was still water to float her. The foremast standing where the drowned footing stood — one object, mast and way off the headland both, exactly as they had said in the hall. Rhea had run the wire down the night before and left the stub at the footing with a metre of tail and a thimble on it, and the whole thing would work if the ferry sat where she was told for eleven minutes.

She set the meter on the bench and switched the receiver warm. Through the shed’s one good window she could see the ferry come out past the mole, bow shouldered into the swell, Tam at the wheel, unhurried, the way he did everything that frightened other people.

“Bring her two points,” she said into the handheld. “You’re proud of the footing.”

“I’m proud of my boat.”

“Two points.”

The ferry came round. Her foremast crossed the window, black against a sky with no colour in it, and Rhea watched the numbers instead, because the numbers were what she was for. The carrier was there before the mast was even steady — a soft rise on the meter, 0.4, 0.9 — the marsh taking the signal and giving it back.

“Hold there,” she said. “Hold her right there.”

“I’m holding.”

“You’re drifting.”

“I’m holding, Rhea.”

The keel touched. She heard it through the hull of the shed rather than the air — a long grind coming up through the concrete — and then the swell lifted the ferry and set her down again, and the foremast whipped against the sky like something being argued with.

“Tam. Get her off and come round again.”

“She’s on the hump. She’ll sit.”

“She won’t sit, she’ll pound. Tam—”

The meter climbed. 1.8, 2.6, the needle steady for a breath and then not steady. He was up, then. She could see the shape of him on the rungs he had welded himself — one arm hooked, the other working the shackle pin, mousing wire in his teeth, the feed tail swinging below him in a wind that was not supposed to be there.

The ferry took ground again, harder. The mast rang — a tone you could hear, a struck bell of aluminium — and the sound of it went into the receiver and came out of the speaker as a howl she had to reach over and kill with the heel of her hand.

The swell picked the hull up. The hull came down. Something parted at the quarter with a crack like a rifle, and the mast went over to leeward in one long fall, and the rungs went into the water, one after another, and the water closed over them.

Rhea had the door open before she had decided to. Cold came in, and the smell of the marsh, and the ferry lying over on the shoal with her deck canted and her foremast down and the sea working at her, and Aggie on the slip with both hands at her mouth and her kitchen timer still clipped to her coat.

“The punt,” Rhea said, going past her. “Get the punt.”

“Rhea—”

“Get the punt, Aggie.”

The rungs came up out of the swell and went back down. Rhea got her shoulder under the bow of the punt and walked it off the slip into the water, and the water came over her boots to the shin, and she had never rowed it in her life.

Behind her, up in the shed, the meter was still climbing, because the antenna was up — the antenna had gone up beautifully — and the only part of it that had worked was the part that had taken him.

## 20. BEAT: The Blank Hour

Two inches of sea stood on the receiver-shed floor, and it had come in warm. Cold a person could work in. This had crossed the marsh first and carried the marsh back with it, and every time Rhea shifted her boots the water let go of something rotting.

The receiver sat up on the bench on four bricks, coax out the window and up the hill to a concrete footing with nothing on it. Out on the north shoal the ferry lay where the ebb had left her, canted eleven degrees, foremast down, the rungs going away into the grey. Boats had been dragging the line since first light.

Aggie came down the slip at twenty to nine with her hem in the water.

“Anything?”

“No.”

“The Brannigan boys have the punt out past the green can. They’ll not stop for the dark.”

“They should stop for the dark.”

“They won’t.” Aggie’s hand went into her coat and came out closed around the kitchen timer. “Ask it.”

“I did. At four.”

“Ask it again.”

Rhea looked at the chronometer. 20:51. “At nine. That’s when it comes.”

“It came at nine when there was a mast,” Aggie said, and went back up the slip.

Water moved when the swell moved. Rhea sat with the catalogue open to the current page and the pencil gone soft at the point from the damp, and read back over her own columns the way you read a ledger you suspect. Nine of them now. The one she had started in March held what the frequency gave her; the next held what the harbor did. Some nights the two matched down to a comma. Some nights they did not, and somewhere around the fourth of May she had begun to write in the gaps.

The timer rang at 21:00. She keyed the carrier open. One point two reflected. Forward power where it had been every night for six weeks. She gave the date, gave the hour, and let go of the key.

Nothing came back.

She waited nine seconds, which was the lock time. Then thirty. Then a minute, which was longer than the frequency had ever taken, and at two minutes she was on her knees in the water with a torch in her teeth, checking the feeder at the wall for the third time that day, and the patch cord was seated and the ammeter was honest and the tide clock was—

No. Not the tide clock. The generator hours were hers, four of them, signed for by a man who was not in the shed.

At 21:06 she stopped. The carrier hummed, open, empty, the way a room hums.

Fingers went cold before the rest of her did. She put the torch down and sat back on the stool with the water at the second rung and looked at the catalogue, and understood, in the plain way you understand a sum, that nothing had ever been sent to her. There was no fixed sheet. The causeway had drowned the Brannigans in one version of the record, and in another version they had driven up the hill at ten past four with their windows down. The fourth of May had come at 04:12 with three feet of surge and it had come at 07:20 with three inches. A log with two versions in it was not a log. It was a list of decisions, and somebody had been making them, and the somebody had her handwriting.

There was no column headed Sorrel. She had turned the pages looking for one.

She turned to a clean page. Water had got at the corner of it already.

Then she set her hands flat on the bench and read the tide tables aloud, because that was the appointment, and because they were the only thing left in the shed that was certainly true.

“Pelham Point, tidal predictions, Friday the tenth. High water 03:41, five point one metres. Low water 09:58, one point four. North shoal, low water 04:12, zero point six.” The pencil moved. “Sunrise 07:44. Sunset 16:29. Winds, southwesterly, three to four.”

The carrier stayed open and said nothing, and she read it again, slower, and put in the depth over the causeway sill and the height of the bar at the harbor mouth and the figure she had worked out at noon for the morning tide and had not yet checked.

At 21:20 she let the key go, and the shed went quiet enough to hear the marsh.

Then she keyed up a second time and said a thing that was in no column of the catalogue.

“Tam Okonkwo is in the water off the north shoal,” she said. “That is where he is. That is what I have.”

She held it a moment, the way you hold a door for someone you cannot see, and let it go, and keyed down, and wrote 04:12 in the margin — an hour nobody had given her — and set Aggie’s timer for it.

## 21. BEAT: The Answer

**CHAPTER 21**

The timer went off at 04:12. Rhea had been awake since three, so as to be there when it did rather than be woken by it. She keyed the carrier open on the second, gave the date and her name, and then held her tongue, because a woman talking into an empty frequency was one thing; a woman answering herself was another.

Static. The sump ticking behind the wall. Out past the marsh a bittern working its single note, as though it had mislaid the rest.

At 04:19 the frequency opened.

“— Pelham Point, tidal predictions, Monday the thirteenth.” Her own voice, gravel in it from a sleep she hadn’t had, slower than she had ever heard herself speak. “High water 04:57, five point three metres. Low water 11:02, one point six. North shoal, low water 05:31, zero point four.”

The pencil moved before the doubt did. Five point three. She had cast that figure on Thursday out of the tables and the almanac and had not yet spoken it into the carrier, nor to anything alive.

“Sunrise 07:51. Winds veering north-west, four to five, backing off by dusk.”

Rhea set the pencil down and listened to her own breath arrive a half-second behind the speaker’s. “That isn’t an echo,” she said. “An echo doesn’t know the tide.”

“No. It doesn’t.”

“Give me something I can walk over and put my hand on.”

A pause, and the wet click of a carrier keyed by a finger that had been keying it a long time. “North window. There’s a crack comes down from the putty and stops halfway. Tonight, when the wind veers, it finishes.”

Rhea crossed the floor. Cold putty, wet glass, a hairline that ran eleven centimetres and quit. She measured it against her thumbnail because there was nothing else to hand.

“What else have you got?” she said.

“The water’s down to the second brick course. You’ve set Aggie’s timer where the flux tin used to live, and you keep touching it. There’s a pair of boots inside the door you’ve walked around all night.”

The boots. She had walked around them a dozen times at least. “Say the rest of it.”

“I have a roof,” the voice said. “I have a lamp with oil in it and the wick trimmed. The floor here is dry. The aerial’s up, and it isn’t the ferry’s.”

“Say that again.”

“The aerial isn’t the ferry’s.”

“The roof. Say the roof again.”

“It leaks in one place,” the voice said. “There are forty-one chairs in the Methodist hall and nobody sitting in them.”

Rhea put her back against the bench and slid down until she was sitting on the wet boards, which she would not have done an hour earlier.

“How much diesel have you?”

“Four hundred litres. Before you ask — it isn’t enough, and I’m not going to tell you which thing I spend it on. That’s the part you have to do badly by yourself.”

“Eleven minutes,” Rhea said. “That’s what the tank gives me now.”

“Nine. There’s water in the feeder.” A sound she knew: a kettle set down on iron. “Nine o’clock,” the voice said. “Every night. That’s the whole of it. Don’t make it a religion and don’t stop, and put a note in the catalogue for the nights you don’t, because you’ll want it later, when you can’t remember which version of you wrote down what.”

“Which version—”

The carrier let go between one word and the next, and the static came back like weather.

Rhea sat on the floor until her feet went cold. Then she got up, wrote the exchange into the catalogue in her terrible shorthand, and went out. The wind had veered. Before she locked the shed she held her lamp to the north window, and the crack had run from the putty to the frame — eleven centimetres longer than it had been at four in the morning.

The hall had lit its stove by first light. Aggie Brannigan read the transcript twice, moving her finger under the lines, and when she finished she squared it against the table edge as though it might come apart in her hands.

“This is your handwriting,” she said.

“It’s my voice. You can’t put a carrier wave on a table.”

Fennick leaned in from the far end. “How much diesel is an hour of that thing?”

“Eleven litres,” Rhea said. “An hour of the mast is a day of heat for this room.”

The hall did its arithmetic in silence, the way a hall does, each person taking the number away and bringing back a different answer.

“She says forty-one,” Aggie said. “She counted the chairs.”

“She counted them empty,” Rhea said.

“Then it’s a real place,” Aggie said, “or it’s you, thirty years on, talking to a room you can’t fill.” She pushed the transcript back across the table. “Which is it?”

“I don’t know yet. I know she knows the tide, and she knows the boots.” Rhea folded the page along its crease and put it in her coat with the ledger. “Nine o’clock. I’ll open the carrier and read whatever you put in front of me, and we’ll have heard it together, and it won’t be my word against anybody’s.”

“And then what?” Fennick said.

“Then we decide whether the diesel goes in the boat or up the mast.”

Aggie’s mouth opened and shut on something she decided not to spend.

“Bring your answer at nine,” Rhea said, buttoning her coat. “And if you haven’t got one, bring somebody who has.”

## 22. The Ebb

Morning tide came in flat and brown and gave him back against the shoal at the foot of the foremast, face down, one arm out, as though he had swum the last length of it deliberately.

Fennick went in with a boathook and two others. Rhea stayed on the shingle with the rest and did not follow, and her boots were dry when it was over. That was the part she kept: four men doing the work, the water no colder than her hands, and herself standing above the weed line with her arms folded like a woman waiting for a bus.

They laid him on the hard. Aggie Brannigan brought a blanket, looked at the state of him, took it away again, and came back with one that could be burned afterward. Nobody said the obvious thing about the ladder, which was that he had gone up it fine.

“His coat,” Rhea said.

“You’ll wait.”

“I’ll wait.”

Ground on the headland runs four inches deep and then flint. It took two hours and a crowbar and every man who could stand upright in the wind. Aggie read from the back of a hymn book because the blank pages in it were the only paper left in the hall that hadn’t been written on. Fennick said the ferryman’s name five times in one sentence and each time it came out smaller.

“Who takes the boat out?” a boy asked from the edge of the crowd, meaning it practically.

“Nobody,” Fennick said. “There’s nowhere to take it.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“Say something,” Aggie said to Rhea, when the shovels had gone quiet. “You were the last one he talked to about anything.”

“I was the last one he shouted at.”

“Then say that.”

Wind worked the grass flat. Rhea put her hands in her pockets and looked at the mound and the crowbar standing in it.

“He was the only one who ever asked me what an hour weighed,” she said. “He counted everything. He counted the diesel and the days and the people, and he never once counted wrong to make anybody feel better. That’s the whole of it.”

They went down off the headland in a straggle, and the tide was already filling the track where the ferry had grounded.

The coat hung in the shed two days before she touched it. Salt had stiffened the shoulders into a shape that was not quite his. The breaker key sat in the inside pocket on the ring with the ignition and the padlock for the diesel store, and the ledger was in the other pocket, swollen at the spine, its seam gone white.

She opened it at the bench with the lamp turned up.

Tam had kept it in pencil so it could be argued with. His last full page was dated the morning he took the boat out, and the entries above it she knew, and the entries below it she did not. Mast, three hours ten. Draw at load, eleven point three. Weather, four point two. And then, squared off with a ruler, a line that was not an entry:

If I go, the hours are hers. She’ll hate that it’s in writing. It’s in writing.

Rhea read it twice. Then she turned the page and read the balance he had ruled underneath — six hours fifty minutes — and sat down on the upturned flux tin and stayed there until the lamp began to brown.

By seven the hall had its stove drawing and thirty-odd people in it, which was what a town did when it had no church and one warm room. Aggie was at the table with the tea and Fennick was at the far end with his boots up and his hat still on, and both of them looked at the ledger in her hand and neither said anything generous.

“He left me the key,” Rhea said. “It’s in his handwriting and I’m not going to pretend it isn’t.”

“A dead man can’t will the town’s diesel,” Fennick said.

“No. But he can write down what he spent, and I’m going to read it, because you should hear the number from me and not from arithmetic six weeks from now.” She flattened the page. “Mast, three hours ten. Draw, eleven point three. Weather, four point two. Balance, six hours fifty.”

The stove ticked. Somebody at the back put a mug down too hard.

“Six hours,” Aggie said, “is not six weeks.”

“No.”

“And three of those went up the mast before anyone voted on it.”

“They did.”

“Then why are you holding the key and not the constable?”

Rhea set the key on the table where everyone could see it and did not put her hand back over it.

“Because I know what it buys and none of you do,” she said. “Twelve minutes a forecast. Fifty an hour of talk. That’s the exchange. He worked it out before I did and he wrote it down because he knew I’d try to spend it fast and call it courage.” She looked around the room, and the room looked back. “So here’s my first act with the key. Nine o’clock tonight is cancelled unless the wind drops and the hall says yes. And the hours get written in this book before they get spent, in front of two witnesses, every time, mine included. If I want to talk to the future I’ll have to ask you first.”

Aggie’s mouth worked.

“That’s a strange kind of quartermaster,” she said.

“It’s the only kind I’ll sign for.” Rhea turned the ledger around and pushed it up the table, and put the pencil on top of it, and slid the key back into her coat where it made a small cold weight against her ribs. “Now read the pages. All of them. Then tell me which of those hours were his and which were mine, because I have been listening to myself talk for six weeks and I have stopped being able to tell.”

## 23. Nineteen North

## CHAPTER 23

The chalk was down to a stub by the time the last name went up.

Aggie counted twice. Nobody argued with the second count. Nineteen under the word NORTH, twenty-two under the word STAY, and the board was small enough that the two columns nearly touched.

“Read it,” someone said, so Aggie read it, and the hall listened to its own names as though hearing a weather report for a country it had only visited.

Rhea stood at the back with her shoulders against the door frame and her hands in her coat, not helping. The vote had not been hers to run. Somewhere in the four days since the headland the hall had decided she was the woman with the key, which was not the same as being trusted, and she had learned to sit inside that difference without reaching for anything.

“Question,” Fennick said, from the stove side. “Fuel.”

The room went still in the particular way rooms do when the real arithmetic arrives.

“Nothing in the tank goes north,” Rhea said. “It heats us and it runs the set. That was true before the vote and it’s true after it.”

“We’re meant to walk two hundred kilometers on that?”

“You’re meant to walk to the Cape Hobb road-end, where there’s a truck and a man called Drover who owes this harbor a tow. That’s from Tam’s own book, in his hand, and I’ll copy it out for whoever wants it.”

Fennick looked at his boots. “Tam’s book.”

“Yes.”

Nobody said anything else about the fuel.

Aggie came down the aisle with the chalk between two fingers. Rhea took it and wrote the nineteen names again on the clean half of the board, under NORTH, in the order they had volunteered rather than the order they’d stood. It took a while. The hall waited through it, and the stove ticked, and a child in the second row counted the names under her breath and got to nineteen before Rhea did.

“At first light,” Rhea said. “Which is 06:41. If you’re not on the gravel by 06:41 the road will start without you, because the road is a tide thing and I’m not sending anyone up Fennick’s Cut in the dark.”

“You won’t come,” Aggie said. Not a question.

“I won’t come.”

“And if we get to Cape Hobb and there’s nothing there?”

Rhea put the chalk down and looked at her properly. “Then you come back, and I’ll still be here, and we’ll be forty-one again and no further forward.”

That got a laugh — thin, one syllable, but a laugh. It was the first one in the hall since the burial.

They filed out into the cold in ones and twos. Rhea stayed to bank the stove, and by the time she had the damper shut the hall was down to six people and the nineteen were already outside, standing in the lamplight with their bags and their arguments about who was carrying the water.

Aggie came back for her timer. She stood in the doorway with it in her hand and did not put it down anywhere.

“You’ll want a witness at nine,” she said.

“I always want a witness at nine.”

“I’ll be here.”

Then she was gone, boots crunching, and the hall was empty.

“Because I’ve never heard a name come back that wasn’t already standing in the room,” Rhea said. “The frequency doesn’t tell me about people. It tells me about water and wind and what hour a thing breaks. If I say your nineteen names into it, I’m talking to myself in the dark.”

Fennick considered this with the patience of a man who had spent thirty years hauling pots.

## 24. Pelham Light

Two days to walk the foremast out of the ferry, and they did it with grease, planks, and twenty-two backs. Rhea cut the wedges at the partner herself, the chainsaw bucking, and when the spar came free of the deck it bled rust water onto her boots for a full minute. Then the sheer legs went up, Aggie Brannigan called the count, and the mast came down the shingle in a cradle of greased two-by-six, forty feet of it, rolling slow as a tide.

The footing had survived the surge with four inches of green pitch still in the cup. Nobody had to say what it was for. Aggie's nephew cut oak out of the boathouse rib and built the cradle; the pole-line glass came down off the road and went under the base foursquare, because a mast that touches ground is a mast that talks to ground and Rhea wanted the whole forty feet for talking forward.

"Half inch on the north guy," she said.

"Half inch it is."

"More. Two."

They got the rake right by four in the afternoon. Rhea stripped copper out of the dead array cable and wound the loading coil on a length of boat pipe, forty-one turns, and when she keyed the carrier the reflected needle sat flat at 1.2 and the lock came in nine seconds, which was the best it had ever done and nobody in the yard had the vocabulary to be impressed.

"Meaning what," Aggie said.

"Meaning the mast hears better than the array did. Meaning we can do this on a quarter of the amps." Rhea wiped her hands. "Which is lucky, because a quarter is what we've got."

The hall that night put it to a vote, because that was the habit now and the habit had teeth. Fourteen hands for a ration of one hour per tide, the nine o'clock, the rest of the day dark. Five against, one abstention, and the abstention was a man asleep in the back.

Then Aggie stood up again with her coat still on.

"One more," she said. "Who decides what gets said."

"Me," Rhea said. "I'm the one who can read it."

"You're the one who can key it. That's not the same question." Aggie laid her hands flat on the table, and the hall went quiet the way it did when a person had come to say a whole thing and not part of it. "Two of us heard the cape light answer in March and we didn't like it. There's twenty-two here and every one of us has got somebody up the coast road. If we're spending the town's amps to shout into the dark, I want to know what we're shouting, and I want to vote on it."

"Vote on a tide table," Rhea said. "Vote on a surge warning. That's what it's for."

"Vote on the names," said the woman from the north row.

Wind came off the harbor and worked at the hall windows. Rhea counted the faces and did not like the count before anyone raised a hand.

"Names don't come back," she said. "I told Fennick that. The frequency gives me water and wind and what hour a thing breaks. It doesn't give me people. Say his name into it and you're talking to your own kitchen."

"Fine," Aggie said. "Then we're talking to our own kitchen, all of us, together, once a night, out loud. I'd pay diesel for that."

Somebody laughed, and it wasn't a mean laugh, and that was worse.

The vote went sixteen, five, one. Rhea's hand was among the five, and she wrote it down in the ledger under the diesel, because a book that only records what you won is a book nobody should keep.

"Nine o'clock, then," she said. "An hour. Weather first, then the causeway, then whatever's left." A breath. "But I read the temperatures. I read the water. That part isn't a vote."

"Fair," Aggie said.

"Fair," said the north row.

Rhea went down to the shed with the lantern and set the microphone on the middle of the bench, twelve inches left of where she always put it, within reach of anybody's arm. Then she called them in and let Aggie have the key for the practice, low power, shunt in.

Aggie unfolded the paper Fennick had taken north and read the first name into it. "Abel Fennick." Then the second. Then the third.

Rhea was watching the meter, not the paper. At the fourth name the reflected needle swung off its mark and settled high, and stayed there, and nobody's hand was near the tuner.

## 25. The List

Nineteen names came through in four minutes and eleven seconds, with no weather in front of them. That was the first wrong thing.

Rhea had the ledger open at the middle page and the pencil worn flat at the point. Nine o'clock meant tides. She had the columns ruled, the mug set on the corner, the headphones clamped over her ears against the cold; she was ready for barometric pressure, and what she got was Abel Fennick.

The voice spelled the difficult ones. It gave ages, which it had never done. Then a date, then a second date, then it stopped and waited long enough that Rhea lifted her head to check the carrier meter for a drop.

Nothing had dropped. It was waiting for her to catch up.

She turned the page and wrote on the verso, small. At the eleventh name the carrier slipped — two seconds of hiss — and came back mid-word, and she took the letters in the order they arrived and made the name out of them anyway. Goss. Trewethey. Two Brannigans, one of them nine years old.

Then the voice said: *in every version but one.*

Then it read the one, and Rhea wrote until the pencil went blunt, and then she walked up the hill to the hall with the ledger shut under her arm.

Eleven at night and the stove still had a shoulder of coal in it, because the hall was where the heat was and nobody slept warm at home anymore. Aggie Brannigan was at the trestle with the ration sheet. The north row had claimed the back bench. Somebody was mending a boot sole with a fork handle.

Rhea set the ledger on the trestle, opened it, and found the stub of chalk.

"Nineteen names," she said. "I'm going to read them."

She read them. When she came to the child, Aggie's jaw went still, and Aggie did not look up from the ration sheet, and Rhea kept reading because stopping would have been worse.

"Ada Goss," she said. "Third of May. Every version but one."

"That's not a date. That's a Tuesday." Ellen Trewethey, against the wall, coat still on. "That's next week's Tuesday."

"It's the third of May," Rhea said.

"Read it again."

She read it again. Nobody moved. Somebody in the back row did arithmetic on their fingers and put their hand down when they finished.

"Nineteen," Aggie said slowly. "Same as went north."

"It's a number."

"Everything's a number to you."

Rhea turned the page. "The one version. You want it or not?"

They wanted it. She read it out the way it came, and it was not a warning. It was a works order. Close the causeway gap before the neap on the twenty-sixth — not the tide-set, the gap, the whole gap, stone and rail and pinned timber. Keep the north row's chimneys swept. Put the pumps where the boathouse footing sits. Be off the flat at Rye Mouth on the fourteenth between two and four in the afternoon. Have Trewethey's roof felted by the ninth.

Nobody asked whether it could be done. They asked what it would take.

"Whose say-so is this?" Ellen said.

"Mine," Rhea said. The room took it and gave nothing back.

Aggie went to the map on the wall, the Admiralty sheet gone soft at the folds, and put her finger on the neck of the causeway where the winter sea had eaten the road down to the culvert. "Closing that takes a pump, and a pump takes power, and power takes fuel we don't have."

"There's the ferry engine," Rhea said, and wished she had counted to three first.

The hall made a sound like a held breath let go. Twenty-two people, all of them understanding at once, and not one of them looking at her.

"That's the way off the headland," said the man with the boot.

"It's a diesel," Aggie said. "It'll run a pump."

"It's the way off."

"And after the twenty-sixth there won't be a way on, either." Aggie's finger had not moved off the map. "Rhea. Three names short of the twenty-two in this room. Say the three."

Rhea looked at the ledger. "Ilse Fennick. Davy Trewethey." She turned the pencil over. "Me."

The stove ticked. Nobody said the obvious thing, which was that this was the closest thing to mercy the list had offered anybody, and that it had cost her nothing to read it out.

"Then you're not in the versions," Ellen said.

"No."

"Good," Ellen said, and turned back to her coat, and that was that.

They did not raise hands. Rhea stood braced for a vote, for the long ugly business of a vote, and it never came; the hall had already turned itself toward the trestle and the map and the chalk, and men were asking whether the ferry's engine bed was bolted through or lagged in, and Aggie was writing *PUMP* on the ration sheet in letters big enough to be read from the back bench.

"Grease," Aggie said. "All of it. And somebody get the good bar out from under the ferry bench before it goes to rust."

Rhea closed the ledger on her thumb.

"Cape Hobb goes at nine," she said. "That's — the watchstander expects —"

"Nine o'clock," Aggie said, not looking up. "Bring the pencil."

Rhea walked home along the harbor wall with nineteen dates in a book under her arm, and behind her the hall door opened and shut twice, and boots went away up the north row, and nobody, in all of it, had asked her whether the voice could be wrong.

## 26. The Engine

--- CHAPTER 26 ---

The chainfall came down from the boatyard on a sledge, two men walking beside it and Aggie out ahead like a woman bringing a doctor.

"Bolted through," Aggie said. "I asked Tam in November. Bolted through and lagged in both, he said, and he was right, which he would want mentioned."

Rhea held the lamp in close. Under forty years of paint the bedplate nuts sat in their own rust, corners rounded off; the lagging had gone to powder where the oil fed on it.

"Through is better," Ellen said. "Through means we take it out the top."

"Through means we cut the deck," the watchstander said. She had come down from Cape Hobb with a bag of tools and no explanation, and nobody had asked her for one.

"That's what we're going to do," Ellen said.

Rhea put her hand on the rocker cover. Cold, and under the cold a faint heat left from the last time the ferry ran — weeks ago, she knew, and knew perfectly well it was her own hand coming back to her.

"We lift the whole bed," she said. "Frame and all. Cut the deck between frames four and six, keep the beam for cribbing. Nothing gets dropped. Whatever comes out goes back into something."

"You're talking about his boat," one of the boatyard men said.

"I'm talking about a heavy thing that doesn't care whose boat it is."

Nobody moved. Then Ellen took up the saw and went to the deckhead and made the first cut, and the noise of it went round the harbor and brought people down to watch, and after that the work did itself the way work does when the decision is already spent.

It took a day and a half. Under the deck they found a glove wedged behind the fuel line, dried stiff. Aggie took it out and put it in her coat without a word and went on chalking.

They ran the engine block down the north row on rollers in the rain, twenty-two backs on it, and the causeway gap took it at low water, and the cribbing went in around it while the tide stood off and let them. Aggie had the auxiliary generator stripped off the ferry already and dumped on the shingle. The propeller shaft followed the engine down; Rhea cut and sleeved it herself, and by the second evening the shaft ran from the gap's new wheel to the flywheel, and the pulley at the far end ran to the generator's belt, and the whole of it stood there in the sea like a thing built by people who had never been told it couldn't be done.

"It's not power," Rhea said, when the watchstander asked. "The tide comes in a surge and goes slack and surges again. A light that flashes isn't a light. The engine's the evenness. The mass of it, the governor, all that iron turning — the tide pours through and the iron smooths it, and what comes off the belt is steady."

The flood came at 19:40. Water stacked against the gap and turned the wheel, the belt took up, and the flywheel began to move — slow, heavy, appalling, a thing waking. Ammeter on the frame, under a tin. The needle climbed, sat, wobbled, sat.

"Twelve," the watchstander read. "Twelve and a half."

"That'll run the shed, the hall, and the Brannigans' pump," Aggie said, doing it on her fingers, out loud, as she did all arithmetic, as though sums were a thing you ought to be ashamed of doing quietly.

"Twelve and a half isn't the list," Rhea said. "The list wants eighteen at the masthead to reach forward an hour with anything on it."

"Then we put the ferry's ballast in as a second wheel."

"There's no ballast," Rhea said. "There's the hull, and the hull's a hull."

Aggie looked at the ferry, engineless at her mooring with a hole cut in her deck, no shaft, no propeller, no way to be anything but a building from now on. Her mouth went thin.

"Well," she said. "It's done."

Behind them the tide ran through the gap and the flywheel turned, and the lights in the hall came on down the row, one window and then another, and along the whole north shore the harbor watched Ash Harbor light itself off the sea. Ellen walked up to look at it with her arms folded. Something in the shape of her, standing there, was the shape of a woman checking whether a bridge would hold.

The watchstander came back down the shingle with the tide-book under her arm, stood next to Rhea, and did not look at her.

"Nineteen names," she said. "I heard them read out at nine."

"You heard them through a squall, off a mast two miles out."

"I heard them."

Rhea looked at the water. "Then you heard them."

"Am I on it?"

The wheel went round. The flywheel made its slow accounting. Rhea had read the list out four times and copied it twice, and she knew every letter of it — nineteen names, a space under each, the dates in Sorrel's even hand.

"No," she said.

The watchstander nodded once, slowly, as though she had worked that sum already and only wanted it confirmed by the person whose fault it was.

"All right," she said, and knelt down and began to wipe the salt off the ammeter's tin.

Rhea took the pencil out of her pocket, and on the back of the list, at the foot, in her own hand and not Sorrel's, she wrote the name down where any one of them could rub it out.

They went. Coal in a handcart, three hens in a crate, Mrs. Treen carried up the path in a kitchen chair because she would not be carried any other way. By six the line of coats had gone up into the half-light and the tide-set was still turning in the causeway gap below them, doing the town’s arithmetic whether or not the town believed in it.

Offshore wind came up warm about eight. Nobody said anything about it until later, and later everyone said they had felt it.

At 23:40 the sea stood at the boathouse step.

At half past midnight it stood at the boathouse step. Somebody had a paraffin lamp going and somebody else had brought a bottle and the bottle went round twice and then stopped going round.

“Offshore holds a tide off,” Aggie said. “That’s all this is.”

Dawn came grey and flat with the water like a swept floor. The tide came in the way a tide comes in. By two in the afternoon it lay a foot under the boathouse step, the sun was out, and a man down on the shingle was laughing, which was worse than shouting.

Rhea walked down to meet them because standing above them was no longer a thing she could do.

“It didn’t come,” Aggie said. Boots in the wrack, pencil still behind her ear.

“It’s nine hours late.”

“It’s an ordinary tide and a dry afternoon. Say the rest of it.”

“I can’t.”

“Then say the part you can.”

What Rhea could say was that a forecast was a reading, and a reading was not a promise, and she had let them hear it as one. She said that, standing in the wrack with her hands where they could see them. It did not go far.

The hall sat that evening with the lamps lit and the doors shut. Thirteen to nine, and the paper went facedown on the trestle.

“Receiver stays,” Aggie said. “You don’t key carrier without two of us in the room.”

“Two of us.”

“Two of us and the door open.”

“That’s not a ration. That’s a guard.”

“It’s a guard,” Aggie said.

On the fifth she came down to the shed and took her own kitchen timer off the shelf without a word, and that was worse than the vote.

Three nights the carrier opened onto nothing. Rhea keyed at nine and held it and let it run the full three minutes into dead air, and on the second night she keyed a second time at ten past, which was not in anybody’s rules but her own, and got the same flat nothing. Into the catalogue she wrote, in the column with no heading: *no signal, 5th. no signal, 6th.* The pencil pressed hard enough to groove the page.

On the third night the watchstander came down the shingle with the tide-book under her arm and stood in the doorway and did not come in.

“I’ve been raising Cape Hobb on the light’s set,” she said. “Every night since. Nothing.”

“That’s four hundred kilometres of water and a hundred-watt set.”

“I know what it is.” She turned the book over in her hands, once, the way you turn something to keep your hands busy. “It went quiet at nine on the third.”

The paraffin lamp ticked. Out in the gap the tide-set turned over and over, spending the ferry’s engine on the town’s behalf.

“Before we climbed,” Rhea said.

“Before we climbed.”

Rhea took the pencil out of her pocket and turned the catalogue back to the column with no heading and wrote the third of May at the top of it, and drew a line under the date, and sat looking at the line.

## 28. Dead Air

Three nights of nothing, and Rhea took the transcript box down off the shelf.

By the second night the bench had gone under paper. Every broadcast she had ever copied lay edge to edge in date order, corners held down with valve bases and a spanner, because the shed door let in a draught that would take a page into the dark under the bench, where the mice had the run of things.

She read them the way she had been trained to read a spectrum. Not for the words. For the spacing.

The ninth column had never had a heading. Into it she had written lock frequencies out of pure habit — cycles off the mark — and now she set the whole column out along a strip of chart paper, ruled it, and stood back.

Minus eleven. Plus four. Minus eleven. Plus nineteen. Minus thirty. Plus four.

Not a scatter. A scatter would have been honest. Arrivals came in knots, and the knots had dates, and the dates were not in order.

Gravel moved outside. Aggie Brannigan stood in the doorway with her coat buttoned wrong, one toggle through the wrong loop, and did not come in.

“The timer’s on my kitchen dresser,” she said. “I’ve nothing to set it for.”

“Keep it.”

“My girls asked me why the sea came late.” Aggie looked at the paper on the bench, then at the lamp, then at Rhea. “I told them the woman at the point was wrong. That’s what I told them, and I’d take it kindly if you’d give me a reason to be wrong about that.”

“There’s nothing coming.”

“Then why’s the lamp lit.”

Rhea had no answer that would fit in a doorway. Aggie waited the length of three breaths, and then she went back down the shingle, and the toggle stayed wrong the whole way.

Rhea turned the chart paper over and ruled it again.

Minus eleven was the earliest — eleven broadcasts, the last week of March through the second of May — and the spokes of that cluster all sounded like the same woman, because they were. She had a way of closing: *Pelham Light, closing.* The plus fours closed differently. *Out.* The plus nineteens had a habit of saying *we* when they meant *I*.

Six clusters. Six women.

She laid her own transmission log alongside, and the alignment came up like a face out of developing fluid. Every time she did a thing she had heard about beforehand, the next reply arrived on a different offset. The causeway first. Then the engine. Then the List.

“Tell me I’m reading it wrong,” she said, to nobody. The paraffin ticked.

It was the rosters that finished it. Each cluster’s voice, at some point in its run, had read out the hall. Minus eleven said twenty-two. Plus four said twenty-two. Plus nineteen said seventeen. Minus thirty said nine.

She took the pencil and crossed out nothing, because there was nothing to cross out. The counts were what they were. Ash Harbor, in each successive reply, had fewer people in it.

By dawn she had found the last of the minus elevens, dated the second of May, six minutes long, ending mid-word. *— tell Tam the lamp —*

No carrier after. No closing. Just the page stopping where the woman stopped.

Rhea sat with her hands flat on the bench and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do since March, and the arithmetic was small and clean and gave her no way out of it. A transmission goes out. One Ash Harbor hears it. She acts on what she hears — and the Ash Harbor that spoke is no longer the one downstream of her. It is downstream of a woman who never heard it. Overwritten. Not defeated, not warned off. Unwritten, with everyone in it.

The spring voice had a roof and a lamp and twenty-two people, and it had asked after the mug in her hand and the moth in the lamp, and it had stopped on the night she stood in the rain at the causeway and shouted the Brannigans back off the road.

She got up, and her chair went over, and she let it lie.

In the ninth column, under the numbers, she wrote the heading at last. HEARD BY. And beneath it, in a hand that had stopped being careful: *rate — one answer, one town.*

Then she took down the tide-book, because rationing had just become a joke at her expense, and if every nine o’clock she opened her mouth she spent a world, then the only honest thing left was to spend it all at once and let them hear everything — every forecast, every name, every mistake, the ledger read out loud — so that whatever Ash Harbor was listening would know exactly what it was and what it had cost.

## 29. The Whole Record

The water left the footings at 03:44, and Rhea walked out along the dry crown of the causeway with the tide-book under one arm and the biscuit tin of transcripts under the other, stepping stone to stone as though crossing a room.

Aggie Brannigan caught her at the third footing in a coat over her nightdress, no hat. “The whole of it,” she said. “That’s what the hall is saying you told them.”

“Every forecast I have, out to the end of the book. Then the names. Then the ones I got wrong.”

“And the money?”

“Tam’s ledger.” Rhea set the tin on the engine housing, where the ferry’s flywheel turned slow in the ebb, belt running up to the armature, armature to the mast. “Every gallon, every house it never reached. The whole record, read out.”

Aggie studied the set. “It’ll kill it.”

“Likely.”

“Then why.”

“Because I’ve been spending one town a night to save another and never once said so out loud.” Rhea got the lid off. “If they’re paying, they can hear the price.”

By 03:50 there were twenty-two of them strung along the causeway in the dark, and nobody had told anyone to come.

She keyed the carrier at 03:56. The needle woke and settled at four and a half.

“Tide tables,” she said. “Eighteenth to the twenty-fifth. Heights above chart datum. On the north road subtract four inches for the bar.”

Wind took the corners of the pages; she weighted them with a stone off the footing. She read through the spring tides, the neaps, the two hours of bare footings on the eighteenth, and her voice went out into the dark where the sea had been.

“Now the errors.” She turned the page with a thumb. “On the fourth of May I gave a surge for four in the afternoon. It came at one in the morning, three feet low. This town stood on the hill in the rain for nothing, and after that half of you stopped believing a word I said. That one was mine. I read it off a transcript that was already old the hour it landed.”

“Six point one,” Aggie said, quiet, at her elbow.

“Names, then.” Rhea took the list from the tin. “Nineteen of them. I’m going to read the dates as they came, and I want you to hear how many versions there are where the date is different, because there are a great many, and in one of them nobody dies at all.”

She read them. Somewhere behind her a man began to cry and did not stop, and no one moved to comfort him, because he was not the only one.

“Hobb,” she said, “if you’re on, this is the last one with any power behind it. Take what you need.”

The needle passed nine. A smell came off the mast housing, faint and sweet — varnish cooking, shellac going — and the flywheel’s note dropped a tone.

“Ash Harbor,” she said, “there is a town downstream of every word I say. All winter I have been talking over the top of them. I don’t know how to stop. I know how to say everything once, and I know how to say nothing, and that’s the whole of the choice.”

“Twelve,” said the watchstander from Cape Hobb, out of the speaker, thin and far, hungry.

Rhea opened the ledger. “One hundred and six gallons, March, to the hall stove. Four to the Brannigans, who have been cold since Tuesday. Twenty-two gallons held back for the ferry, which is grounded, and which nobody will raise again.” Her voice caught and she let it. “Eleven gallons, Tam Okonkwo, for generator hours he never once asked me to account for.”

At 04:09 the belt began to slip. Aggie put both hands on the housing as if it were an animal she could hold still.

“Forecast,” Rhea said. “Eighteenth. Wind southwest four, veering. Rain by the evening tide. The causeway goes under at 05:52 and stays under four hours.”

The needle hit the pin. A thread of smoke came up off the mast footings, and the smell turned sharp, and the carrier frayed into a hiss, and the hiss into nothing.

Twenty-two people stood in the lowest water of the year with a dead transmitter and no lights on the hill behind them.

“That’s the lot,” Rhea said.

Then at 04:12 the receiver, which had never needed the mast to hear, opened by itself. Clean as a bell. And her own voice came out of it, older, unhurried, saying the town’s name.

“Ash Harbor.”

Nobody breathed.

“Ash Harbor,” it said again, and then it stopped, and the carrier stayed open underneath the silence the way a held note stays under a rest — waiting, plainly waiting, for somebody to answer it.

Rhea reached for a key that had no current in it and understood she would never send another word.

“Here,” said Aggie Brannigan, to the speaker, out loud, in her nightdress, in the dark. “We’re here, you daft woman. Say your piece.”

## 30. Nine O'Clock

**CHAPTER 30 — Nine O’Clock**

Nine o’clock came up the hill with her: lamp in the left hand, tide-book under the right, the shed door swollen enough to want a shoulder. She shouldered it and went in.

Aggie Brannigan waited on the path in a cardigan and no hat, a plate in her hands under a tea towel.

“You’ll catch your death.”

“I’ve had it.”

Aggie peeled the towel back an inch — four slices of fruit loaf, dark as peat. “There’s a vote Thursday on the school roof. I want you at it and I want you quiet.”

“I’m quiet.”

“You’re right, which is worse.” She pushed the plate into Rhea’s hands, went three steps down the hill, and stopped. “Ellis walked out to the cut this morning.”

“And?”

“Water over the culvert to the second rail. He shouted. Nothing came back but the sea.” Aggie straightened her cardigan. “Nineteen people don’t stop existing because the road’s under.”

Inside, the lamp made the bench one colour and the walls another. The mast lay out in the salt marsh where the surge had dropped it, windings burnt through, and what Rhea had now was forty feet of wire up the corner post and across to the chimney — three watts on a good night. She had stopped calling that a compromise and started calling it the station.

At 21:00 she put the switch over, and the receiver was already open.

No search, no drift, no hiss under it. A carrier held steady at 3.7 with the vernier where November had left it, the needle sitting at 2.6, the way a room sits when someone has just left it. Rhea set the lamp down and looked at the key for a while. The key looked back. She did not touch it.

“Ash Harbor,” she said. “Nine o’clock.”

Tuesday’s weather first, because that was the habit and the habit was the point: wind southwest three, veering, rain off the sea by morning, the causeway dry between the ebb and four. Then the school roof, which had three votes for a tar sheet and nine against the expense, and no one yet willing to say the two were the same argument.

“Aggie’s brought fruit loaf,” she said. “She wants it known she baked it in the Methodist oven and not on her own stove, and that the difference is four pence of coal, and that she is not asking anyone to pay it.”

Somewhere on the far side of the shed, a door closed.

“The tide-set’s dead. The foremast is down. We’ve coal for the hall and wood for the rest, and the ledger says eleven weeks if nobody’s stupid.” She turned a page. “There are nineteen names in the front of this book and twenty-two in the back, and I don’t intend to be the woman who keeps them in separate books.”

At 21:38, half a second before she said it, she heard herself say it — *the road* — thin, unhurried, out of the speaker, in her own voice and not her own mouth. Then she said it herself, and the two of them landed almost together, and the carrier went on holding.

She kept talking. That was the whole of it. She had spent a year reaching forward with both arms, spending other people’s warmth to buy minutes of a thing she was not owed, and the arithmetic had bought her a dead mast and a plate of loaf going cold on the bench. Tonight she read them the ordinary inventory of a town: who was mending what, which roof leaked, where the chalk had worn to a stub.

At 21:44 the carrier lifted under her voice and a stranger came in on top of it — a man, flat and tired and too close, as though he stood at the other end of the bench.

“—anyone on this frequency. Anyone. This is Cape Hobb light, we’ve had the lamp out four nights, say something.”

Rhea’s hand found the key and stayed on it.

“Cape Hobb, this is Ash Harbor. We hear you.”

A pause long enough that she thought she’d lost him.

“Ash Harbor.” He said it twice more, testing it. “You’re the ones with the woman on the nine o’clock.”

“I’m the woman on the nine o’clock.”

“Then tell me something.” Static washed through, then steadied. “We’ve had nineteen on the road. Counting them south past the cut every night since the thaw, and tonight there’s no count. Nothing on the shingle, nothing on the shore road. Have you got them?” A breath, wet, badly held. “Have you got nineteen people, Ash Harbor, or have you got a story about nineteen people?”

Rhea looked at the tide-book. At the ledger with its two columns of names. At the plate of fruit loaf, and the lamp, and the switch she had not once had to press, open before she climbed the hill, waiting the way a habit waits.

She pulled the log close and squared it and wrote the date at the top, and under it, in the space where the forecast went, she wrote a single word, and underlined it.

“Cape Hobb,” she said. “Go slow. How many did you count?”
