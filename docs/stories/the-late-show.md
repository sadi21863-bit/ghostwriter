# The Late Show

_Novel · linear · genres: horror + comedy · 4562 words_

## 1. Chapter 1

Two minutes forty on the light, and the room had already voted. Nell could see it in the phones — up like little headstones, one per table.

“Dying alone,” she said. “It’s not the dying I mind.”

A chair scraped somewhere behind the bar.

“That’s a lie. It’s mostly the dying.” She set her glass on the stool and nudged it a half inch, as if it might need adjusting. “I’ve got a will. One page. My lawyer read it and said, ‘Nell, this is a note.’”

The man at table two laughed — one syllable, then a check of the room to see if he’d been the only one. He had.

“My landlord asked if I wanted to renew. I said, God, no. He said, I meant the lease.”

Nothing. A woman in the back lit her phone like a small rescue flare, and Nell watched her scroll.

“Emergency contact is my dentist. Not because we’re close. Because he’s the only man who’s touched my face since 2019.”

Two people laughed at that, which was worse than none.

She had six jokes about the apartment, four about the will, and one closer she trusted more than her own family. She did not use the closer. Finishing strong on a night like this felt like tipping a slot machine that had already taken the rent.

“Goodnight. You’ve been adequate.”

She came off the riser, collected her coat from the stool where she’d draped it, and made for the bar while the host did the thing where he held the mic too close to his mouth and said *we’ll be right back, we’ll be right back* to a room that had never been anywhere.

The bar was a length of plywood with three stools. The bartender poured without being asked — house red, an inch and a half, in a glass with a chip out of the lip.

“That bad?”

“It was fine,” Nell said. “For a wake.”

“It’s Tuesday.”

“That’s what I mean.”

The man who came down the aisle did it slowly, one hand on the seat backs as though the floor might be uneven. Cream coat. Gray gloves. A leather book under his arm, big enough to be a Bible or a menu. He stopped at her elbow, set an envelope on the plywood, and didn’t sit.

“Miss Varga.”

“It’s Nell.”

“Miss Varga,” he said, and left it there. “Barrow. I have a booking.”

She looked at the envelope. Then at the bar clock, which read 10:52 and had read 10:52 since she walked in.

“You want to book me. Me.” She pointed at the riser. “You saw that.”

“I saw it.” His voice sat low, the register of a man who spends his days in libraries. “The Bellwether Hotel. Up past the reservoir. Thirty guests, Saturday night, two sets of forty, no material about the war.”

“Which war?”

“Any of them.” He slid the envelope two inches closer, and the corner of it lifted. Cash. Not a folded twenty palmed across the bar — a brick of it, rubber-banded. Nell counted a few bills and stopped, because stopping was a way of keeping her dignity.

“What’s the catch? It’s a cult. It’s a timeshare. It’s a cult that sells timeshares.”

“It’s a private party. They don’t get out much.” He laid the book flat and opened it. Columns, ruled in red. Names in ink that varied in age, some gone brown as tea, some still wet at the edges. “I keep the ledger myself. The old ways are the best ways.”

“Do I sign in blood, or—”

“Your name, and the night.” He turned the pen over to her — a fountain pen, heavy, cold enough that her fingers reported metal from another room. She wrote *Nell Varga* in her own terrible hand and set the pen down with the obscure sense of having been tattooed.

“There’s no deposit, is there,” she said. “Nobody’s ever come back to complain.”

Something moved through the sheet of Barrow’s face, too fast to name. “You could put it that way.”

“I’ve done worse rooms. I did a wedding in a garage.”

“You’ll do this one.” He closed the ledger on his finger and looked at her over it. “Tell me a joke, Miss Varga.”

“I just told you twenty.”

“Tell me the one you didn’t.”

Nell drank. The wine was warm and tasted like a coin. “You want the closer.” She set the glass down. “I’m not scared of dying alone. I’m scared of dying mid-sentence and somebody finishing it wrong.”

Above them, in the dark of the balcony that had been closed since the fire inspection in March, someone laughed.

Not a polite laugh. A howl — wet, helpless, hand over the mouth — the laugh of a person who’d been holding it since the last time they were happy. It went on too long. It went on until the bartender stopped pretending to work and stood with his towel in his fist, and Nell felt her scalp tighten like a fist of its own.

“Who’s up there?”

“Nobody,” the bartender said. “Balcony’s chained.”

Barrow turned the ledger around. There, on the facing page, above her name — still wet — sat another entry in a different hand, the ink gone the brown of dried blood.

*Tandy, M. — 40 min. — opening.*

Nell checked the date in the red column. Tonight’s.

“I’m the opener?” she said, and her voice had gone up into the register she used only for landlords.

Barrow pressed the pen cap closed with a click that sounded, in that room, like a latch. “The Bellwether doesn’t have openers, Miss Varga. We have a running order.” He lifted the ledger and held it against his coat like a sleeping child. “You’re second.”

“Second to what?”

On the sealed balcony, the laughing started again.

## 2. Chapter 2

The green room had been a pantry once — a mirror ringed with bulbs, two of them gone dark, a couch that gave up under Nell the way the open mic crowd had: all at once, and with a sound. She set her bag on the folding chair and looked at the fruit basket. Cellophane pulled tight as a drum. The apples were real. The grapes were wax, and now she knew that in her back teeth.

"Those are for the picture," said the woman in the doorway. "Nobody eats them."

"Noted."

She was tall, sleeves of a dinner jacket shoved up past her elbows like she'd been washing dishes in it. A cigarette sat behind one ear, unsmoked, the filter chewed flat. Her hair had been set in a style that stopped being fashionable before Nell's mother was born, and it had not moved since.

"Tandy," the woman said. "The M on the ledger was my husband. He's dead. Don't ask."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to ask if I was the M."

"I was going to ask if the basket came with the room."

Tandy's mouth did something small and filed it away. She crossed to the mirror, straightened a bulb that didn't need it, and said, without turning, "Seven minutes. Upstairs."

"It's a tight five. I've done it four hundred times."

"Then how was it seven?"

"Room was slow."

"The room was dead," Tandy said. "There's a difference, and you'll learn it here." She turned around. "You were funny for four of those minutes. Which four?"

"Which four were you in?"

"Don't be cute with me, sweetheart. I had forty minutes and I used all of them."

Somewhere past the wall, a door opened and a lot of chairs took weight at once — a sound like rain arriving on a roof. Nell felt it in her back fillings.

Barrow appeared in the doorway with his ledger against his coat. "Miss Varga. The house is open. Fifteen minutes, then I introduce you."

"Introduce me as what?"

"Second." He was gone before the sentence finished settling.

Tandy pushed off the mirror and went to the wing, and Nell followed, because the alternative was standing alone in a room with a couch that smelled like a wet dog's coat. The wing was narrow and smelled of dust and cold radiators. Beyond it the stage, and beyond the stage the house — chairs in rows, coats draped over chair backs, ice shifting in glasses, a low murmur that wasn't words. Squinting didn't help. The dark stayed dark and full.

"You always warm them?" Nell whispered.

"They like to be warmed. Go on. They don't bite." A pause. "That was a joke. I'm told I have to say when it's a joke."

Nell stepped to the edge of the light. "Evening. You're a handsome crowd, in a way I can't verify." Laughter, low and spread out, the way a room laughs when it's already on your side. She felt her shoulders come down an inch. "Who here's had a bad gig? Show of hands. I can't see hands. Shout."

"Blackpool," said a man's voice, gravel and phlegm.

"Blackpool. What happened in Blackpool?"

"Wind."

"Wind." She nodded slowly. "See, I've played a working men's club where the mic was a phone headset and the soundman was a dog, and I still feel like I got off light compared to wind."

Ice clinked. A woman's voice, very close: "Ask him about the pig."

"Do not ask about the pig," Tandy said from the wing, flat as a board.

Dennis had died in a gale. Not a guess — she'd asked what he remembered, and he said *salt*, then *the banner came down*, and then he laughed at himself, dry as a hinge, and the laugh did something to his face. Colour came up in it. Ten seconds of colour, then out again, water finding a drain.

She filed that.

“Right,” she said. “New rule for the evening. Nobody's allowed to be dead at me. Say your worst. Not your saddest — your worst. The one that's funny.”

Silence, but the good kind. The kind with weight on it.

“Mine's a heart attack in a Beefeater car park,” said the woman up front, and the room went up like a match. Her hair had been smoking when Nell started. It stopped.

So that was the trick of it. Laugh at the death and the death lets go of your collar for a minute. Nell had spent nine years learning to make strangers like her and four minutes learning the actual mechanism: everybody wants permission to find their own worst thing funny. The dead especially. The dead have had nobody to tell.

She went round the room. A man who'd fallen off a pier at Blackpool — “the wind,” Dennis said again, and Nell said, “Dennis, we've all been blown off something,” and got a real one out of him, a shout, and the salt dried on his collar. A woman who'd been electrocuted at a hen do. “In front of the bride?” Nell said. “She's never getting married now.” Another man whose last words were *watch this*.

“Everybody's last words are *watch this*,” Nell said. “You're not special. You're a demographic.”

By the fourth or fifth round she had them. They were easy. That was the awful part — they were easy, and they were grateful, and pity went out of her like a drink off a tray and they took it and asked for another. Every one of them who laughed looked briefly like a person who had been in a room with a lit fire.

Except Tandy, who stood in the wing with his arms folded and his mouth doing the thing it did, which was nothing.

She tried him. “Tandy. You died onstage. That's the best death in the building, you know that? That's the Rolls-Royce of deaths. Mine's going to be choking on a Twix in a Travelodge.”

Nothing. Worse than nothing — the bulbs over the mirror behind him stuttered, one of the two dead ones flaring and dying, and the cold came off him like an open fridge.

“That was pity,” he said.

“It was a joke.”

“It was pity with a joke holding its coat.” His hand came up halfway and stopped, the way it had been doing all night, and she understood suddenly that it was holding a microphone that had been taken off him in 1974 or 1980 or whenever it was he'd stopped. “Don't do that to me. Do it to them. They like it. They're tourists.”

“What are you?”

“I'm the one who was in the middle of something.”

Nell looked at him. The folded card in his jacket pocket, corner worn white. The way he stood with his weight on the back foot, always ready to be introduced.

“You died mid-sentence,” she said. “Didn't you. They pulled the curtain and you were still going. You never got to the end of the bit.”

Tandy's face did something complicated. Then he laughed — one bark, ugly and surprised out of him — and the two dead bulbs over the mirror came on all at once, all six burning hard enough to throw shadows, and for a second he was a man in his forties with a bad tie and a good haircut and a whole life in front of him.

“Oh, you're a nasty little thing,” he said. “I like you.”

The applause came down off the house like a dropped crate. Nell turned to take it, both arms up, and Mr. Barrow was standing at the edge of the stage with the ledger open against his forearm.

She hadn't heard him come.

“That's the first time in fifty years,” Barrow said pleasantly. “You should be very pleased with yourself.”

“He's a hard room.”

“He's a hard man.” Barrow turned the ledger. Columns, in a hand too even to be handwriting. Names, and beside each name a number, and the numbers went down the page in a slow arithmetic she didn't like. “May I show you something, Miss Varga? The room must laugh. Laughter is — how to put this — a reprieve. A small parole. Without it, my guests return to the moment of their deaths, and they stay there, and they stay there, and it is not an evening I'd wish on anyone.”

“So it's a laugh or the lungful of water.”

“It's a laugh or the lungful of water.”

Nell nodded slowly. “And what happens to me if I stop being funny?”

Barrow closed the ledger with a soft sound, two boards meeting.

“Midnight is the last set of the night,” he said. “The house must go out on a laugh, or the house does not go out at all. If the room is silent at the stroke, the evening simply — continues.” His eyebrows lifted. “You'd be the closing act. Permanently. I did explain the terms were generous.”

The house chairs creaked. Somewhere behind her Dennis was still laughing at something, and the laugh had nobody under it now.

“Generous,” Nell said. “You said generous.”

“You'll never be heckled again.”

She looked past him at Tandy, who had gone very still in the wing, and at the two bulbs that had burned for a second and were burning still, and she thought about what he'd said. *I'm the one who was in the middle of something.* Fifty years of the same lungful.

“Tandy,” she said. “What time did you go on?”

## 4. Chapter 4

Four minutes in and the room had given itself over. Not all of it — a back table kept flaring and guttering like a bad fuse — but the first three rows were laughing, and the laughter had bodies in it: elbows, heels, the wet honk of a man who couldn't catch his breath.

"Nine forty," Nell said. "Tandy went on at nine forty, and I say this as a professional: that is a punishing slot. Nine forty's where the audience has had two drinks and no dinner and one fight with a babysitter. Nine forty is where comedy goes to—" She stopped, hand up. "Sorry. Poor choice. Nine forty is where comedy *drowns*."

A howl went up from the left. At the rail, Tandy made a great show of checking a watch she wasn't wearing.

"Fifty years," Nell said, "in the same bit. That's not a set, that's a mortgage." Laughter, rolling. "My grandmother had a casserole like that. Every Sunday, same dish, and we all had to tell her it was good. She's dead now, but so are you, so." That one took the room apart. She could feel the set lift under her the way a table comes off the floor when four people grab it.

So she pushed.

"Here's what gets me about Tandy, though." She turned her shoulder to the room, and behind her the silence came in like a draft under a door. "You die mid-set, you get what — two minutes forty on the light? Two minutes forty. You never reach the good material. You die in the setup. Dying mid-set is dying a—"

One laugh. A single one, from over by the fire door — short, wrong, entirely alone.

Every glass on every table began to sing.

"You're doing my bit," said a voice from the dark. Tandy's, but come apart at the seams, tuned wrong, like a radio sliding off the station. "I haven't got to it yet. I haven't *got* to it."

"Tandy, I'm—"

"Cut the rope." That was Dennis, from the back, at a volume with weather in it. "*Cut the rope.*" The wind came up through the floorboards and took the tablecloths and the napkins and the last of the warm air.

Then water. Rising, enormous, in a room with no water in it — a sound like a bathtub the size of a cathedral — and a man's voice underneath, flat and patient and reasonable the way drowning men are reasonable: "It's not deep. It's not even deep. It's not even deep."

Nell got off the stage. She did it without looking at the microphone, without saying goodnight. First rule. Only rule. Broken. Behind her the boards stayed lit in a bright accusing rectangle all the way to the door.

The green room smelled of hot dust and old coffee. Somebody had put out a plate of sandwiches that nobody had touched in fifty years. She got her back against the couch, slid down, sat on the floor with her knees up, breathing through her mouth.

Tandy stood in the doorway with her arms folded and didn't come in.

"You said nine forty," Nell said.

"I heard what I said."

"There's no clock out there. There's no clock anywhere in this—"

"Nine forty." Tandy's jaw worked. "Nine forty and forty minutes of material and I had it *mapped*. I had it on a card. I never went out without the card. Check your pocket. Go on. Check."

Nell's pocket was empty. So was the other one. Then she looked at the couch, at the gap where the cushion met the arm, where the horsehair had burst out, and there it was: a rectangle of card, soft as cloth at the corners, gone the yellow of an old tooth.

Pencil, in a small hand. *9:40.* Then numbers. One through eleven, each with a joke scrawled beside it, six or eight words apiece, and beside each joke a tick. Twelve through twenty weren't there at all. Below the last tick, in a slant that pressed hard enough to emboss the card, someone had written:

*if they're still with me — tell them about the night I quit. it isn't funny. that's why it works.*

Nell turned the card over. Blank.

She read it again. Forty minutes of set, eleven jokes. Tandy had planned to close with the one thing that would have cost her something, and she had never gotten there, and she had been paying for it at nine forty every night since.

"You never told it," Nell said.

Tandy didn't answer. In the corridor the light over the stage door went down once, twice. The signal.

Nell got up off the floor. Her hands were doing something they'd never done before a set. Shaking. There was no cold in the room. She stood in front of the ringed mirror and looked at a woman who had a tight five about dying alone that killed at open mics because it wasn't about her at all.

"Ask me what I'm like when I'm not being funny," she said.

Tandy frowned. "What?"

"There's my closer." Nell picked up the card and walked out with it.

## 5. Chapter 5

"Nine-forty," Nell said. "Every club in this city puts me on at nine-forty. Never nine-thirty. Nine-forty is the slot they give a comic they're hoping will quit before the check clears."

The laughter came back at her dry and rattling, like a laugh they'd all practiced in a colder room. Dead crowds don't warm up. They do laugh, though. Tonight that was the arrangement.

"And I know what you're thinking. You're thinking, Nell, you've got five minutes on dying alone that absolutely kills." She took a step off the mic and let it hang. "It kills. It's the only thing I ever wrote that kills. And the reason it kills is that I've been doing it at home, alone, at night, to nobody, for two years. That's not a bit. That's a rehearsal for the material."

A woman in the third row — pillbox hat, gloves, dead before Nell's mother was born — cupped her hands and shouted, "Do the funeral one!"

"Ma'am, they're all the funeral one. It's a theme."

Dennis laughed so hard his coat blew back off his shoulders and kept blowing, a gale in a room with no windows. Nell pointed at him. "Dennis. Fifty-eight years dead and still dressed for weather. That's commitment."

At the back, by the door where the plaster had gone soft with damp, Tandy stood with her arms folded.

"Tandy's not laughing," Nell said. "Every room's got a critic. In this room the critic is dead, which is the only promotion comedy's ever offered anybody."

"We've got eleven minutes to midnight," Tandy said.

"I know what time it is. I've known what time it is since the light went out over that door." Nell reached into her jacket and brought out the card. Soft corners, old-tooth yellow. "You left me a set list. Eleven jokes and a note. You wanted to close with the night you quit."

Something went through the room like a door opening onto a stairwell. The ghosts stopped shifting in their seats.

"Don't," Tandy said.

"I'm doing it."

"It isn't funny. It doesn't get a laugh."

"It doesn't need one. It needs to be true, and then the laugh comes, because you'll all recognize it." Nell set the card on the stool. "You quit the night you killed in front of eleven people and one of them was the guy who'd told you to quit. And you looked at him and you thought — nobody here knows I'm dying. And you were right. That's the joke. You were right."

Nobody laughed. Dennis's coat settled. The clock over the bar clicked over to eleven fifty.

Mr. Barrow had come forward, ledger open in both hands like a man carrying a font. "Miss Varga. The ledger is mine."

"You've been keeping score on people who can't leave. That's not a host. That's a bad open mic."

"You read a name aloud," he said pleasantly, "the room remembers how it ended. That's not a threat, it's a policy."

"Great. Then let's do some crowd work." She took the ledger out of his hands. He let her, which she would think about later, at length, in a car. Names came up off the page in a hand that wasn't hers. "Dennis Rourke. Nineteen fifty-eight. Cause of death: weather."

The gale hit the chandelier. Dennis stood up in it, coat full of wind, mouth open on the sound he'd made at the end — and then the room laughed, and the wind dropped out of him like a plug pulled, and he sat down grinning with his hair standing straight up.

"See? You laugh first, it can't get in."

"Miss Varga—" Barrow said.

"Vernon Halloran, nineteen seventy-four. Cause of death: one more." She read it fast, and the laugh was already there waiting, and the man in the burnt jacket folded over at the waist and came back up, and the fold in his jacket smoothed itself flat. "Betty Simms, nineteen forty-one. Cause of death: the balcony. Don't ask. She was funny."

She read them all. Every name got a setup and every setup got a laugh, and the room got younger and lighter and meaner, the way a room does when it's winning.

Barrow stood very still at the edge of the stage.

Nell turned the page. One line left, in a neat, patient hand.

"Mr. Barrow. Cause of death —" She looked up. "That's a blank."

"I don't die."

"Everybody dies. You've just been holding the pen." She set the ledger on the mic stand and squared it. "Here's mine. Mr. Barrow, host. Cause of death: a bad review."

The laugh started in the back row and rolled forward and did not stop, and Barrow's mouth opened to say something about policy, and the ink in the ledger came up out of the page the way water comes up through paper. His name darkened. His shoes stayed. The rest of him went into the line, and the line closed over him, and the clock on the wall clucked once and it was midnight.

Then the ghosts clapped. Not laughing now — applauding, all of them at once, hands made of nothing making the sound a room makes when it means it. Tandy was still standing where she'd been, and she was laughing, actually laughing, head back, mouth wide, no sound coming out but the shape of it was unmistakable.

"Ask me what I'm like when I'm not being funny," Nell said to her.

Tandy got out, "I know," and then there was nobody by the door but a coat hanging on a hook that hadn't been there at nine-forty.

Nell walked out through the lobby with the ledger under one arm, Tandy's card in her pocket, Barrow's cash still folded in her jacket. At the desk she stopped. The last page had one clean blank line under his, waiting the way a stage waits.

She took the desk pen. She did not write. Then she took the pen with her, which was a decision she was aware of making.

Out in the lot the car turned over on the fourth try. Nine minutes down the road, doing sixty with the window open to keep herself awake, she said the new closer out loud to the windshield — just the shape of it, the first four words of a bit about being the last one to leave a party — and it needed one more word, and it was going to be a good one, and she had four hours of driving to find it.
