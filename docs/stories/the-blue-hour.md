# The Blue Hour

_Novel · linear · genres: hardboiled noir + slow-burn romance · 4520 words_

## 1. The Widow of Rua da Saudade

Rua dos Fanqueiros took the rain like a drunk takes advice — badly, and all at once. By six the gutter had quit and the water had come in under my door and made a lake of the linoleum, which is how I came to be sitting with my feet in the desk drawer, four inches above the flood and one inch above useless.

She was in the room before I heard the door.

Nine in ten people who climb my stairs bring a problem shaped like a husband or a debt. This one stood dripping on the mat and looked at the bucket, the bottle, the burnt place on the sill where I forget cigarettes, and did not apologise for the puddle her umbrella was making.

“Senhor Vale.”

“That’s the name on the glass. Backwards, but it’s the name.”

“Ines Mouraria.” Down went the umbrella into the stand, as though the stand had been dusted since the war. Black coat. Hair pinned once and decided forever. Fifty, or the kind of fifty that means money and a doctor.

I cleared the newspaper off the client’s chair. She sat, drew her gloves off finger by finger, laid them across her knee, and looked at me.

“I want to hire you to find a man.”

“That’s the trade.”

“His name is Aurelio.”

The pencil was on the sill, gone soft with the damp. “Aurelio what?”

“Aurelio is enough. He sang.”

“Sang where?”

“Alfama. There are tables. There’s a piano that’s been tuned twice in its life.”

“And he’s stopped singing.”

“Nine days ago.”

I wrote it down. Fires go out faster than that; men don’t.

“What is he to you, senhora? With respect, and without.”

“He was my husband’s voice.”

A tram ground round the corner, threw its light up the wall, took it away again. Rain filled the space where an answer should have been.

“Say that again in Portuguese,” I said.

“My husband can sign a thing. He can put a figure in a column and make the column add up. But at a table, with a glass in his hand, he opens his mouth and the room looks away.” One glove got smoothed flat against her knee. “Aurelio sat at the end of the table and said it for him. What the man had been thinking all evening, in better words. Then he sang, and my husband was the one who had thought of it.”

“That’s a service you can buy.”

“We bought it.”

“And where is your husband now?”

“Rua da Saudade. He takes lunch at one and coffee at three and doesn’t leave the house. Ask him where Aurelio is and he’ll set the cup down and tell you musicians move.”

“There’s a certificate,” I said. “There’s a grave in Prazeres with his name on the stone.”

“There is.” Her chin came up a fraction. “I paid for the flowers. The stone is the only part of it that fits.”

I made a note in the book I wouldn’t be showing her. Nine days was nothing to the police, she said, and everything to her, and the whole of her argument was thin as paper and stood up anyway, because she did not blink. Not through the mud of the story. Not through her husband at his coffee. Not once, at me, like a woman paying a fare and knowing the fare.

An envelope came out of her bag. Brown, soft at the corners, set down on the dry corner of the desk.

“Count it.”

“I’ll count it when you’ve gone. That’s the polite way.”

“Be rude and quick,” she said. “The polite ones take a month to tell me nothing.”

Five contos. Notes so new they cracked when I fanned them, and my hand did a small greedy thing I didn’t care for, and I set the money down and flattened it too carefully.

“For nine days,” she said. “The same again if he’s alive.”

“And if he’s dead?”

“Then you’ll tell me the way you’d tell a client. Not the way you’d tell a widow.”

I came round the desk. Water moved under my shoes, cold through the stitching.

“One thing. You’ve had nine days. What happened tonight?”

Her hand found the knob. Behind her the corridor bulb hummed its one note.

“There was a man before you. He asked better questions than mine, and he found where Aurelio sang.”

“Where is he now?”

“At home. He sits in his chair and he doesn’t go down to Alfama anymore.” The door opened; the dim light made a stranger of her. “His name’s on a door like this one. He’s stopped answering it.”

Then heels on the stairs, the umbrella ticking, the street door shutting.

Five contos went into the drawer where the rent money wasn’t. I put my coat on and wrote Rua da Saudade in the book, and under it the name of a man four streets over who had stopped answering his door — because whatever was down in that basement had already eaten one man’s curiosity, and I wanted a look at the bones before I went in.

## 2. A Cheap Kind of Rain

Rain came off the river thin and cheap, the kind that doesn't fall so much as get into your clothes by agreement with the air. Rua do Arsenal ran grey with it. So did I.

The Conservatória do Registo Civil smelled of wet wool and sealing wax. A clerk behind a counter of scarred oak wore spectacles mended with copper wire at the hinge, and he did not look up when I came in, and he did not look up when I wished him a good morning.

"Aurelio Mouraria," I said. "Death record."

"Relation?" The pen never stopped.

"Creditor."

That stopped it. "There is no such category."

"There is now. He owes me eleven months' rent, and I'd like to know whether to keep on being owed."

The clerk looked at me over the copper wire. "You are owed by a dead man."

"I'm told that's how it usually ends."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he took his spectacles off, breathed on them, put them back on — a man buying time in small coins — and went into the back. He stayed there long enough for me to read the notices on the wall twice: typhoid in the eastern parishes, a warning about dogs, the new price of a certified copy.

He came back with a ledger and set it on the counter as though it might bite him.

"Fifth of last month. Cardiac failure. Signed, Dr. Anselmo Reis." A fingernail travelled down the page. "Informant — J. Mouraria. Brother."

"Brother." I let it sit there. "He came in himself?"

"Came in, signed, paid the fee, asked how soon the ground could be opened. An efficient man." The clerk tapped the page. "A good coat. November is a month for a good coat."

"And the widow?"

The copper wire caught the light as he tilted his head. "No widow declared."

"Man dies, no widow declared."

"I don't make the declarations, senhor. I file them." He turned the ledger so I could see the neat black hand — the empty line where a wife ought to have been, and in the margin, in the same careful hand, the word *nil.*

I laid down four escudos. The copy went into my breast pocket with the chill of the room still in it.

Prazeres is a long hill and a short tram ride, and I took the tram, because the rain had got into my shoes and my shoes had begun to make a case against me. The cemetery wall is white and high, and the gatekeeper's clock runs four minutes fast, which makes it the only thing in Lisbon that hurries.

The sexton's name was Fausto, and he carried his spade over one shoulder like an opinion.

"Plot forty-one," I said. "Mouraria."

He set the spade down. "Row of the poor. Filled the sixth."

"Who paid?"

"Cash." He sucked his teeth. "The brother. Graça man. Wore a good coat to bury a poor one."

"Did he come back? After."

Fausto scratched his jaw. "Sundays. Some Sundays. Stands at the end of the row with his hat off. Doesn't kneel. Doesn't say anything I can hear."

"And the widow?"

He looked along the flat stones, where the rain beaded on the crosses and ran. "Twenty years I've buried men, senhor, and there's always a widow, and she always cries in the same key. This one —" he shook his head — "there wasn't one. Not at the grave. Not at the wake. The brother said the family wanted it quiet."

"Somebody kept it quiet."

"That's what I said."

I gave him a note for his trouble and stood a while at the row of the poor, where the stones lie flat because the ground is cheap and the dead get no opinions. Plot forty-one had flowers on it. Fresh ones, cut and laid and getting rained on — somebody's garden, watered twice.

Thursday. It was Thursday.

Back down the hill the rain thinned to nothing and the streets began to steam. I walked, and did the arithmetic I did not want to do. A signed death. A filled grave. A brother in Graça who had not been in her story. And nowhere, on any line of it, the name of Ines Mouraria.

Nine days, she'd told me. Nine days to say she was a widow — and I had a paper in my breast pocket that said nobody was.

I did not go up to Graça that night. I went home, lit the lamp, and laid the certificate flat where the light could get at it, and read it until the words stopped meaning anything. Then I put it in the tin with the others, face down.

She would ask me what I'd found. I would tell her about the signing, and the grave, and the good coat. All of it true.

And then I would say the word *brother*, and watch her hands.

## 3. The Blue Hour Club

The stairs went down off the Beco do Arco, tiled blue and white and worn to the biscuit at the edges, and the bulb over them was the only light in Alfama that wasn't honest daylight. Halfway down, the smell came up to meet me — spilled wine gone sweet, cold stone, cigarette smoke that had lived in the plaster long enough to be part of it.

Nine steps. Then a door with a peephole, and a man behind it who had the face of a drawer that doesn't open.

"Closed," he said.

"I noticed. I'm looking for a singer."

"Everybody's closed." He let the chain hang. "Come back in a year."

I showed him the retainer's letter, folded so only the letterhead showed. It is not a badge. He looked at it the way a man looks who has been shown worse things. Then he shut the door, and I heard the chain come off, and the door opened again the width of a shoulder.

The room was a cellar with ambitions. Blue walls that had given up being blue sometime in the war. A stage no higher than a coffin lid. Chairs stacked on the tables, all facing the same way, like a congregation promised something.

Piano at the back. A man at it, one hip on the stool, cigarette burning in the ashtray on the lid. Ginjinha in a glass you could see through.

"Sabino," he said, without getting up. "You're the one from the Rua dos Fanqueiros."

"Word travels."

"Word crawls. It's an old street." He picked the cigarette up, put it down again. "Nine days I've not played for a room. My fingers are getting stupid."

"Nine days."

"Ask me why."

"I came to ask other things."

"Ask this first." Three notes came down, slow, and didn't go anywhere after. "Because nobody else is going to. A man came with a key to my own door and told me the club was shut for the summer. It's not summer."

"Who."

"Ask the other things."

I turned a chair around and sat, and he watched me do it. His hands were on the keys but not pressing. There was a scab on the back of the right one, healing about a week.

"Give me his last set," I said.

He got up. The folded paper came out of his jacket, soft as cloth from being folded and unfolded, and he laid it on the piano lid under the ashtray so I could read the pencil: five songs, numbers against them. The fifth had been crossed out. Not crossed — dug at, the pencil back and forth until the paper had a hole you could see the lid through.

"Play it for me."

"Senhor—"

"Play it. I'll stand here like a paying customer."

He played. The first four he played in pieces, hands remembering more than he wanted them to, the pedal down so the notes ran into each other. Then he came to the fifth and played that too, the whole of it, and it was the best of the five and he knew it, and when he finished he sat with his hands still on the keys and the cigarette had gone out on its own.

I read the hole. Underneath the dig, the title was still there.

"When did he cross it out?"

"Night he went. Took the pencil off me, did it standing right where you are, and said, this one's mine tonight, nobody hears it but the room." Sabino picked the cigarette up, found it dead, and relit it. "And then a man asked for it."

"From the back table."

"Second to last." He looked at me. "You know that?"

"I know the coat came in and didn't take it off. Go on."

"He didn't drink. Sat there like a bill he was waiting to be paid." Smoke came out slowly. "Aurelio sang it. Sang it to the wall, not to the man, which was the bravest thing I ever heard in this basement, and when it was done nobody clapped, because nobody knew yet if it was allowed. And the man in the coat stood up. Before anybody. Before the room. He stood up first."

"Meaning what."

"Meaning that's how you tell a customer from a creditor." His hand went to the top button of his shirt and held it. "Customers wait to see how the room feels. That one had already decided for us."

"And then?"

"And then the lights came up and I had a job stacking chairs." He drew on it. "I heard the door. I didn't turn round. I've thought about that a good deal, senhor, and I'll tell you what I think: I didn't turn round because I've been in rooms where it was healthier not to."

The glass was empty. He hadn't touched it.

"A name," I said.

"One."

"What it cost you?"

"Nothing yet. That's the part that's costing me."

He said it: a doctor's title, and behind the title a name, Seixas. Said the doorman knew it because the doorman had put the man in a taxi twice that spring, and the man had said it aloud, both times, the way men do who like how it sounds.

Then Sabino walked me to the door and put the chain on before he opened it, standing in the gap with his shoe against the wood, and I noticed it was not yet five in the afternoon and he had already bolted himself in.

I went up the nine steps. The coat would smell of that basement for a week — the wine and the stone and the smoke — and I did not mind it, because it was cleaner than what I had in my breast pocket.

At the top of the Beco, out of the blue bulb's reach, I turned the name over once and put it away, and went up the hill to Graça, to the brother she had left out of her story, to ask him what he knew about a man called Seixas.

## 4. What the Barman Pours

Tomás Mouraria sold candles and plaster saints on the Calçada, and he heard the name Seixas the way a man hears weather — with a glance at the window. He was wrapping two candles for a customer who had already gone, and while the paper creased under his fingers he told me that a man who wanted to know about Seixas could drink at the Estrela do Mar, on the Rua dos Bacalhoeiros, and ask for Chico, who remembered every coin that had ever crossed his counter and most of the men who had spent them.

Which was how I came to be sitting, at four in the afternoon, in a room with four tables and no windows, watching a barman pour me a white that had never met a grape.

“You want the good one,” Chico said, “you pay for the good one.”

“I want the one that makes you talk.”

He set the glass down. “That’s the cheap one. Lucky for you.”

He was small and round, sleeves furled above the elbow like a man about to do something with his hands, and he showed no sign of doing it. He stood with his back to the shelf, arms crossed, and let me drink first.

I drank. It was cold and it was wet and after that it was a matter of opinion.

“Aurelio,” I said.

“Sang here Tuesdays.”

“And?”

“And then he didn’t.” Chico took a plate of sardines off the counter and put it in front of me without being asked — the gesture of a man who has decided what the conversation is going to cost. “You want to know what I know, or what I think?”

“The first one. I get the second one free everywhere.”

“He was paid to stop.” Chico uncrossed his arms. “Not paid like a man buys a horse. Paid like a man buys weather. Somebody puts an envelope in his hand and says, senhor, the city is very loud this year, perhaps you should rest your voice. And Aurelio — you understand, he was not a brave man, he was a man with a voice, those are different trades — Aurelio said thank you, and he rested it.”

“You saw the envelope.”

“I saw him put down the last set list and walk out with his coat over his arm, and I saw the coat had a shape to the pocket it hadn’t had at nine o’clock.” He scratched his chin. “Also I heard him say a name to the man at the door. Not the way you say a name when you’re frightened of it. The way you say it when you’re trying to convince yourself it’s nothing.”

“What name?”

“Seixas.”

He let me sit with it. Outside, a tram went by on the Rua dos Bacalhoeiros and the glasses rang faintly on the shelf.

“How much,” I said.

“In the envelope?” He shrugged. “Enough that he bought a new coat to put it in.”

“How much for you.”

Chico looked at my glass, then at me, and I understood the price would be the retainer’s small change and that he would name it without apology. He named it. I put it on the zinc. He counted it as though I might have made an error, and put it in his apron without ever looking down.

“The man who carried the envelope,” he said. “He drank here after. Not with Aurelio — before. Wrote a receipt on the corner of the counter, folded it, put it in his coat, and I thought: a man writing receipts in a tasca at eleven at night is a man who is not sure the office will believe him.”

“What office?”

Chico turned and took down a bottle he did not need and set it on the counter between us.

“Rua do Alecrim. Up the stairs, second door, the plate says Mouraria e Filhos, comércio de navegação. Shipping.” His eyes came round to mine and stayed there, and I felt the afternoon go cold in a way that had nothing to do with the wine.

“Say the name again.”

“Mouraria. It’s on the plate. It’s on the deed — I know because my brother-in-law works their manifests and he’s a man who tells me things when he’s drunk, and he was drunk at my sister’s funeral, God forgive him.” Chico picked up my glass and filled it without my asking. “You’ve gone a colour, senhor.”

“It’s the light in here.”

“There is no light in here.”

I paid for the second glass. He poured the third free, which is how a barman tells you he has decided he likes you, and it is not a thing to be glad of.

At the door I turned back with my hand on the frame. “Chico. When the man wrote his receipt. Did he sign it?”

“He signed it.”

“You see it?”

“I see everything from this side of the counter,” he said. “It was an I. That’s all. An I and a surname, and the surname I already told you.”

The rain had stopped and started again while I was underground, which is the kind of afternoon it was in Lisbon that month. I walked up to the Rua do Alecrim and stood in the street and read the plate from below like a man reading a headstone. Mouraria e Filhos. Second door. The same name that was folded twice in the envelope against my chest. I did not go up.

The retainer was still in my breast pocket, still in the envelope with her handwriting on it. A man could put that envelope back in her hand this evening. A man could say, senhora, there are things you didn’t tell me, and watch what her face does. It would be professional. It would be clean.

Instead I walked down to the Conservatória and stood in the queue until the clerk told me the registry closed at five and I said I would come back at nine, and then I went home and hung the coat where I could still smell the tasca in it, and left the envelope where it was, in the pocket, still folded, still hers.

## 5. The Warm Hour

Rain had got into the stairwell and made a job of it, a slow brown line down the tiles, and she stepped over it without looking down — a woman who had learned where the water goes in a building that isn’t hers.

“You’ll want to sit.”

“I’ll stand, thank you. My shoes are ruined and I’d rather think about it only the once.”

Ines Mouraria set a bottle on the desk, wrapped in paper the way a chemist wraps something for a cough, and beside it a packet of notes folded twice. Then she took off her coat and hung it on the hook by the door as though she paid rent on the hook.

“Count it.”

“I’ll count it later.”

“Count it now,” she said. “I don’t want to be a woman who has paid you and a woman who says she has paid you. There’s a difference and it’s mine to close.”

So I counted. Her notes were smoothed flat and squared at the corners, and there were two more than the sum we had agreed. I pushed the two back across the desk. She looked at them a while, then at me, and left them where they lay.

The name Seixas sat in my notebook with a line under it, and under the line a question I had not yet worked out how to ask her. I got the glasses down instead — one of them chipped at the rim, which I turned away from her — and pulled the cork.

“Bagaço,” she said. “My husband kept it for the men who came about the boats. He said it was a drink for finishing conversations.”

“And did it?”

“It finished them.” She took the glass. “It never once started one.”

Water ticked somewhere behind the wall. Down on the Rua dos Fanqueiros a tram went by with that sound they make, like a coin rolling to a stop.

“You want to ask me about the marriage,” she said.

“I want to ask you about a lot of things.”

“Ask about the marriage. The rest will keep.”

She drank, and set the glass down, and told it without a single note of the violin in it. The house on the Rua da Saudade with the piano in the front room that no one had tuned since the year they married. The meals. A man who came home at the hour a man comes home and sat with his hat still on his knee, as if he had been invited and was being polite about it. She said kindness was a thing her husband could do with his gloves on, and that she had spent eleven years being grateful for it in a way that used her up.

“I was a good wife,” she said. “I want that written down somewhere. Not good at it. Good. There is a difference and it is also mine to close.”

“I’ll write it down.”

“You’ll do nothing of the kind.” The glass went to her mouth and came away empty; I filled it. “You’ll find him.”

“You said your husband isn’t dead.”

“I said he is missing. If I were lying to you, senhor, I would have told you something easier to check.”

I took the envelope out of my coat and laid the new notes with the retainer and put the whole of it back in the pocket, and her handwriting went into the dark folded the way it came out. The bagaço had got between me and the last hour. Somewhere in the middle of her talking about the piano, I had stopped hearing a client. That is the whole of it, and it is not a thing I decided, and I want it understood that a man does not choose the hour. He only notices afterward that the room has two people in it.

“Did you go to the cemetery?” she said.

The glass stopped halfway to my mouth. “Yes.”

“And to the Conservatória.”

“Yes.”

“And my name was on neither.” She said it flatly, into her glass, not looking up. “Don’t look like that. I’ve had a week to know what the clerk would tell you. I wanted to see whether you would tell me first.”

The rain thickened on the window and the room went the colour of the inside of a shell. She sat down then, finally, in the chair with the sprung seat, and put her elbows on my desk like a man would.

“Well,” she said. “Which of us says the next true thing?”
