# Low Tide Message

_Web Series · linear · genres: sci-fi + coming-of-age mystery · 6459 words_

## 1. Dead Battery

COLD OPEN

Salt stinks up the arcade the morning after a storm. Jo has the door propped with a milk crate, hauling a mop across linoleum that never comes clean, when the gulls go quiet all at once.

Out on the pier, the water is doing something wrong. It has pulled back off the pilings the way a held breath pulls in, and the pilings stand naked to their green knees, and in the gap the storm has laid its table: kelp, a shoe, a car door, a thousand white crabs scuttling over the bones of the jetty.

Jo leans the mop on the counter.

Down the beach, the storm wrack has stacked itself into a ridge above the sand. Something in it catches light. She picks her way down the ramp past the CLOSED sign, past the nozzle where her mother's tide chart is taped up in the harbour window, still showing yesterday's numbers, and when she kneels into the weed the smell is a slap.

A phone. Cracked across the face like river ice, screen split from corner to corner, salt crusted in the seam. The back is a faded sticker of a cartoon fish wearing sunglasses.

She wipes the screen on her hoodie. The phone lights up. Water sloshes deep down inside of it, a soft slapping when she turns it.

"One man's garbage," she says to no one, and pockets it.

ACT ONE

The pier arcade smells like popcorn and hot electrical. Jo pulls her shift behind the counter, drying quarters in a rag because the machine keeps jamming on wet ones. The claw machine wheezes in the corner, dragging its empty claw through a drift of plush lobsters nobody wants.

On her break she takes the phone into the stockroom and dries it under the hand dryer. Its screen shows one bar. No service won't say whose it is. No lock screen. The photos folder is empty, the messages folder is empty, the contacts folder is a single blank line.

Then the hand dryer cuts out and the phone buzzes against her palm.

*Tide 4:12. Stairs gone by 4:15. Go up.*

No number. No name. The time at the top of the screen reads a date she has to look at twice: not next week. Not next month. A date she can't place on any calendar she knows.

Jo turns the phone over. Water ticks inside it. She looks out the stockroom door at the pier stairs, the ones that go down to the beach where the sign says DANGER and the sign has been there so long the paint is a rumour.

"Weird," she says, and goes to check.

She stands at the top of the stairs with her hands in her hoodie pocket. The sea is flat. Flat the way a plate is flat. A man fishing off the end reels in his line, looks at it, and reels again. Nothing moves.

At 4:12 by the arcade clock, the water at the foot of the stairs pulls back.

At 4:13 it pulls back further, past the low-tide line, past the place where the sand turns to slick rock, past where it has ever gone. A gull lands where the sea was, and looks confused.

At 4:15 a wave the colour of the inside of a shell stands up out of nowhere, tall as the pier deck, and folds itself onto the stairs. Foam slams up through the railings and spits over the top. The whole pier shudders, and the man at the end of it drops his rod and swears, and then the water's gone again, sucking back down, and the stairs are gone. Ripped out by the roots. Iron bolts in the air where the treads were.

Jo's hands come out of her pockets. She looks at the phone. The text is still there, the time still there, the date still there.

The arcade owner yells for her through the door.

"I quit," she says.

She doesn't. She waits out her shift with the phone under her thigh, buzzing every now and then like it's got something else to say, and she doesn't pawn it at the shop on Front Street, though she thinks about it the whole walk home, because a phone is forty dollars, and forty dollars is a bus ticket, and a bus ticket is out.

ACT TWO

Ana has the tide charts spread the length of the kitchen table when Jo gets home. She draws them by hand for the harbour office, and she has been drawing them by hand, Jo is pretty sure, for longer than the harbour office has existed in its current form. Rulers, a rotary pen, a mug of tea that goes cold at her elbow. The radio on low.

"Stairs went out at the pier today," Jo says, dropping her bag.

"Mm." Ana doesn't look up. Her line is very straight. "You weren't on them."

"I was at the top."

Ana's pen stops. It stops for less than a breath, and then it goes on again, and the line is still straight. "Then you were lucky."

"I was. I had a warning."

"Warnings are for people who go down the stairs." Ana holds up the chart. "Tide tonight is at 9:40. Whole town's going to smell like low water by midnight." She pins it over the stove, smoothing the corner with her thumb. "Leave the phone on the counter, Jo. You were always on it."

"I'm not on it." Jo's thumb is on the phone inside her pocket. She can feel it not buzzing. "It's dead."

Ana laughs, one short note, and turns back to the radio.

Jo lies in bed with the light off and the cracked screen under the sheet. The phone has one bar, no signal, and a date that doesn't match the clock on her nightstand. It gets no more messages, all night. She lies there and thinks about the stairs, the bolts in the air, the gull on the dry seabed, and she thinks about the forty dollars at the pawn shop, which would buy her a ticket, which would take her any place the sea isn't.

At some point she sleeps. When she wakes the phone's clock has jumped past morning.

ACT THREE

Two days later, the phone talks again. Jo is on the pier where the stairs used to be, sitting with her boots over the edge, when it buzzes.

*Tide 6:55. A blue boat comes in. Don't be on the slip.*

She checks the top of the screen. The date is still wrong. She takes out the little notebook she uses for the claw machine, where she keeps track of the crane's weak angle and the claw's true grip, because she is the only person in town who can pull a lobster out of that thing without spending ten dollars, and she writes down the two numbers. Six fifty-five. And the date, and then her own date at the top of the next page, and then her own hour, and she does the arithmetic twice because she doesn't trust it the first time, sitting there in the wind with her pencil going.

Thirty-one hours. Every message is thirty-one hours ahead of the tide it describes.

She gets up. Her hands are shaking and she presses them flat against her thighs until they stop, and then she runs, because she has one test she can actually run, and if she's wrong she looks like a maniac, and if she's right she looks like something worse.

The arcade is full of kids. The claw machine sits by the change desk with its lobster pile and its greedy little jaw, and behind the desk Sasha is pretending not to watch the Twilight machine. Jo feeds in her last three quarters and does not play fair. She never plays fair. She walks the claw to the back-left corner where the crane has an extra inch of reach the manager doesn't know about, the angle that only bites if you come at it from the corner, and she closes the jaw on the plush lobster with the sunglasses, the twin of the sticker on the back of the phone.

Outside, the harbour master's truck pulls up to the pier head. Roger gets out, looks at the missing stairs, at the child-sized dent in the railing where the water threw the foam, and he goes the long way around to the arcade door, and Jo watches him come through it.

Sasha leans over. "He's asking about your mother."

Jo's hand is closed around a plush lobster. Her hand is closed around it very hard. "What about her?"

"About a date on a chart they can't find." Sasha's eyes are bright and wary at once. "He said, was she here in '98, and she said, was she what. And he said, on the slip."

She goes out. Roger is at the window in the harbour office, and Ana is at the chart board, and Ana's voice carries out across the deck, flat and cold and very clear: "If you have a question for me, Roger, you'll ask it where my kid isn't standing."

But Jo is standing there.

And her mother turns and looks at her across the boards with a face that says the question has already been asked, and already been answered, and that Jo has walked in on the answer.

CLIFFHANGER

Jo takes out the phone. The screen has gone bright in a way it hasn't before, and the message at the top of it is not from a number she's seen and it is not thirty-one hours ahead, it's stamped with a time that's four minutes ago, and she reads it with her mother watching her from the window.

*This one's yours. Don't come down the slip alone.*

She looks at her mother. She looks at the missing stairs, and the flat sea beyond them, and at the man in the window who is waiting for an answer about a date she's never heard of.

"Mom," she says, holding up the phone, "who's M?"

And for the first time that Jo has ever seen it, her mother does not answer her.

## 2. Inked Over

COLD OPEN

Six in the morning and the harbour office already smells of marker.

Ana stands at the chart board with her back to the door, dragging a black chisel tip across the sheet. Not a line. A block. She fills the square solid, then squares the corners off.

Jo sets the kettle down hard enough to ring. "That's the fourth one."

"Fourth one what."

"Fourth time you've blacked out the same day this month."

The cap clicks. Ana turns with her pleasant face on, the one she wears like a coat over the other one. "Printing error on the sheet. Whole run's off."

"Four sheets in a row."

"Then ask the printer."

Steam clouds the window glass. Over her mother's shoulder Jo can see the square, and under the ink a crease in the paper, the dent of a pencil that went down first and hard. Somebody wrote something there before they buried it.

Her jacket buzzes. Not the left pocket, where her own phone lives. The right one. The cracked one. The beach phone, dead as a brick for eleven days.

She turns away to look at it. Bright. Brighter than it has any right to be.

*Ask her what she inked out. Not today. Ask her Thursday.*

Jo's thumb goes through the loop of her keyring, round and round. "Mom. What's under the black?"

Ana's hand stops on the tin lid. "Nothing's under the black."

"There's a pencil dent. I can see it from here."

"Then it's a pencil dent." Ana crosses the room, takes Jo's chin between two fingers, tilts it up, and smiles the smile that ends conversations. "Go to work."

ACT ONE

The arcade at nine smells of hot circuitry and old popcorn. Jo has the change cart, the mop, and Sasha, who is supposed to be restocking the plush bin and is instead sitting in it.

"Nineteen ninety-eight," Jo says.

"Before I was born, boss."

"Your brother was born."

"Cal was born." Sasha holds a pink dolphin up against the light. "Cal was born in the spring and Mom says it rained for a hundred days and then the ferry stopped running."

Jo's hand stops on the mop handle. "The ferry stopped running in ninety-eight?"

"Cal says the slip's been shut since before he could walk. Why?"

"Nothing." Jo wipes the counter, then wipes it again. "I'm on a double Thursday. Tell my mother if she calls."

Sasha's eyes narrow. "You're not on a double Thursday."

"Now I am."

That night Jo sits at the kitchen table across from her mother and does the asking wrong. She does it fast, before the plates go down, before the fear can find her.

"What happened June fourth, ninety-eight?"

The fork in Ana's hand doesn't move. Water runs in the sink where she left it running. "Who said that date to you?"

"Nobody. It's on the chart. It's on every chart."

"It's a misprint." Ana turns off the tap. "Eat your fish."

"You always say that. You've never once said the same thing about anything else."

Her mother sits down, and the chair scrapes, and she picks up her fork, and for one bright second Jo thinks she's going to answer. Then she says, "Two dollars for the bus tomorrow," and the room closes like a door in a draught.

ACT TWO

Thursday, three o'clock, and Jo tells her mother she's at the arcade.

She is in the harbour office.

Ana is out on the north wall with Roger, doing whatever it is they do with the gauges and the clipboard, and the office is empty except for the kettle ticking as it cools and the chart board, vast and pale under its own light.

Jo climbs the step stool. Forty years of tide curves run down the board in blue, tight as stitches. June 1998 is there, and June 4th is a black square the size of a postage stamp, and around it somebody has drawn, faintly, in pencil, in a hurry, a line that goes up and stops.

She uses her thumbnail first. Then the edge of a ruler. Then she wets her thumb on her tongue, because she has seen her mother lift a mistake that way a hundred times, and the ink gives.

Black comes away in flakes. Beneath it, the paper is soft as skin from being written on and erased and written on again. Letters come up out of the fibres.

*…RKE.*

Jo leans close enough to fog it. Wets her thumb again. Two more letters.

*ROURKE.*

Her mother's name, before she was her mother. Ana Rourke. And under it, one word, underlined twice, hard enough to score the sheet: *OUT.*

Behind her, the door opens.

ACT THREE

Ana stands in the doorway with her jacket on and the rain in her hair and a rolled chart under her arm, and she is not surprised. That's the thing Jo will think about for weeks. Her mother looks at the stool, the ruler, the raw scraped patch on the board, and she is not surprised. She is tired.

"Which shift did you say you were working?" Ana asks.

"Sasha's. Eight till close."

"Because I just passed Sasha on the pier." Ana sets the rolled chart down on the desk, deliberately, the way you set something you don't want to break. "She asked me why my daughter isn't at work."

The kettle ticks. Out past the window a gull drops onto the wall and gets nothing off it.

Jo says, "What happened to you at the slip, Mom?"

"Don't."

"Somebody wrote my mother's name on a chart and then a whole town buried it under black ink." Jo's voice comes out steadier than her hands. "Who did you sail out for? Who came back?"

Ana crosses the room. Her hand stops an inch from the board, and the fingers curl, and stay there, not touching.

Then the right pocket of Jo's jacket buzzes.

Ana's eyes come down to it, and her whole body changes. Not anger. Braced. As if the room had a wave coming into it and she'd seen the set of it from the wall.

"Where did you get that?"

"The beach," Jo says. "After the storm."

"How long has it been working."

"Eleven days. It only started."

"Put it on the table." Ana's voice is flat and careful and it does not shake, and that is worse than if it did. "Put it on the table, Jo, and step back from it."

"It's mine—"

"Put it down and step back from it."

## 3. Thirty

COLD OPEN

Eighteen fifty-two and the pier is empty except for a dog that belongs to nobody and Jo, in her apron, hands on the rail.

The phone in her jacket pocket goes off against her hip. Not a ring. A buzz, dry, like a wasp in a jar.

She has read it eleven times since three o'clock. It says: *18:52. TWO RINGS FROM THE EAST BELL. NOTHING TOUCHES IT.*

Out past the wall the wind has fallen to nothing. The bell hangs off its bracket above the waterline, galvanised, green at the seams, and there is not a bird near it.

Clang.

Jo breathes out through her nose.

Six seconds later — clang.

Nothing touches it.

"Jo!" Sasha has her head out the arcade door, hair stuck to her cheek. "Table four ate the change machine again. It's doing the thing."

Jo doesn't turn around. Her lips are moving. The header on the message says SATURDAY 11:52, and it is Sunday evening, and she counts it twice on her fingers, then a third time against the rail.

"Thirty-one hours," she says, to the dog.

ACT ONE

The arcade back room smells of hot dust and old carpet. Jo has a photocopied tide table taped to the wall, a pencil stub, and a page ruled into three columns.

Sasha comes in with the crisp bag and sits on the coin counter. "Your mum rang the arcade. Twice."

"What did you say."

"That you were on the pier." Sasha folds a crisp into her mouth. "I told her the truth, which is a terrible habit of mine, and now she's got a voice like a wet rope, so thanks for that."

Jo writes 11:52 in the first column, 18:52 in the second. She circles the difference.

The bell. The stairs and the wave that came up them. The grey dinghy at the north mooring that slipped its line at 05:12 while the man who owned it stood on the wall with his coffee.

"Three for three," Jo says.

"Three for three what?"

"It's not a prediction." She sits back. "It's a schedule. The phone knows things a day and a bit before they happen. Thirty-one hours and twenty minutes. That's not a guess, Sasha, a guess doesn't come with a time on it."

Sasha looks at the wall a while. "Or someone dropped a phone in a storm, it's got salt in the board, and it's making up times."

"Then it's making them up in the right order."

Downstairs the claw machine whines, resets, whines again.

Jo tears the page off the wall. "I need a test nobody can laugh at."

"You need a test?"

"Me." She folds the page into her back pocket, small. "I need to know I'm not the reason this is happening."

ACT TWO

At 15:38 the arcade is loud enough to feel in the teeth. Nico and Dell are at the claw machine with nineteen goes between them, and Jo has taped a page to the glass.

Dell reads it out. "'At 15.40 the north breaker blows and the claw drops what it's holding.'" He looks at her. "That's the stupidest thing I've ever read, and I've read my brother's texts."

"A week's tips," Jo says.

Nico stops chewing. "A week's?"

"Mine against yours. Everything in your pocket."

Dell counts. It takes a while. Then he feeds the machine a coin and says, "Sixteen seconds, Josephine."

The claw swings out. Grips. Lifts a pink octopus with one crooked eye. It climbs, and the whole north bank of machines hums, and the clock on the wall says 15:40, and then the lights go down the row like someone pulled a plug out of a wall — claw, skee-ball, air hockey, all of it — and the octopus drops into the chute with a sound like a coin down a well.

Silence, then Dell, quietly: "What the hell."

Jo puts her hand out. He gives her the money.

The harbour master is in the doorway before she's finished counting it. Rennie. Big coat, smell of diesel, hat he doesn't take off indoors.

"That the north bank?" he says.

"Breaker," Jo says.

"Who signed off the electrics?"

"Nobody's signed off anything since the storm."

Rennie crosses to the claw machine and reads the page taped to the glass, and Jo remembers her own handwriting and feels it land on the back of her neck. He peels a corner up, looks at the tide tables printed on the reverse.

"This is office paper," he says. "This is next month's tables. They're not out yet."

"I photocopy things."

"You photocopy things." He lets the corner fall. Then he takes his phone out and photographs the page, twice, and puts his hand on the door. "Stay off the north bank till it's tested."

His boots go up the pier, not down.

ACT THREE

The harbour office window is fogged at the bottom from the kettle. Jo stands outside it with her hands in her pockets and the cracked phone face-down in her fist.

Rennie hasn't knocked.

"She had the time written down," he says, indoors. "15:40. Before the breaker went. And she had next month's tables, which come off your desk."

The ruler moves along the chart, stops, moves again. "She's sixteen and she's bored," Ana says.

"Bored children don't get the times right."

"Bored children get lucky."

"On the fourteenth," Rennie says, and the ruler stops. "Ana. Did you take a boat out on the fourteenth?"

Jo's shoulder is against the frame. Her mother's back is straight and still, and the marker is uncapped in her hand, bleeding a dark ring onto the blotter.

"That's a harbour question, Ivo."

"It is."

"Then put it in writing."

Rennie breathes for a moment. "Your daughter is going to get an answer to this," he says. "It just isn't going to come from me." The door opens, and Jo steps back into the shadow of the fuel tank, and his boots go off down the pier.

The phone buzzes against her wrist.

*Monday 05:40. The slip still works. She'll tell you she wasn't there.*

Through the window, Ana caps the marker. She rolls the chart with both hands, slides the tin out of the drawer, lifts a torch down off the hook by the door, and kills the light.

The back of the harbour office gives onto a ladder down to the water. Jo hears it rattle, and then the clink of the tin, and then that two-stroke cough that has woken her up on a hundred mornings of her life.

Her mother is in the skiff.

Jo steps out of the shadow. "Mom."

The flooding engine settles into a note. Ana's hand is on the rail, and it does not move, and her head does not turn.

Jo counts to ten. Then she takes the boat hook down off the wall and goes after her.

## 4. The Ferry Slip

COLD OPEN

The ramp comes up out of the harbour green and dripping, weed hanging off the pilings in long wet hair.

Jo ships the oars and lets the dinghy coast the last thirty feet. Low water at the decommissioned slip. Nothing down here but concrete and the smell of it.

Her mother's skiff is tied at the bottom. Bow and stern. Engine ticking as it cools.

She sits with the oars across her knees and listens to it tick. Nobody in the boat. Nobody on the ramp. The water going out with a sound like paper sliding.

Then a cut padlock, bright in the weed. Shackle sheared. The chain hanging slack off the gate.

Above her, behind the cracked window of the old ticket office, a light slides across the glass and stops.

Her phone goes off in her jacket.

*Sunday 05:58. The gate's open, if you want it.*

ACT ONE

Concrete under her boots, slick as a fish. She goes up with one hand on the rail, past a painted depth scale worn down to ghosts, past a life ring so old the white has gone the colour of teeth.

The waiting room smells of diesel and cold ash. Benches bolted to the floor in two rows, facing nothing. A brass bell on a bracket by the door, green in the throat. Through the swing door at the back, a camping lantern sits on a desk, throwing light up the wall — a wall of pigeonholes, each one labelled with a year, all of them empty.

The lantern is on. There is nobody in the room.

Boots, then, on the ramp below. Jo drops behind the ticket counter, back against the wood, knees in.

A man comes through with a plastic crate against his hip. Rennie Ivo, in his harbour coat, hair flattened by the wind.

"Ana," he says.

Her mouth is open before she knows it. She shuts it.

Water ticks somewhere.

"The tide's dropping," he says, louder. "Anything on these shelves you want, it's now or it's the crusher."

Jo counts the pigeonholes. She gets to eleven before he moves again, and the crate goes down on the desk with a knock of files.

"Suit yourself." Then, quieter, the way you say a thing you have already said twice: "The fourteenth. Whatever it is you're carrying, say it before the fourteenth."

A door at the back of the office clicks. Rennie's boots go that way. A gust comes through, cold and salt, and then the boots come back, slower, and go out and down the ramp, and an outboard coughs and fades.

Jo stands up. Through the window, the bottom of the slip is bare concrete.

Her mother's skiff is gone.

ACT TWO

She calls her mother from the ticket counter, because it is what she can do with her hands.

"Come on. Come on."

The harbour office voicemail picks up. Ana's recorded voice, flat and quick: *Tide's on the wall. If it's about a berth, put it in writing.*

"Mom. It's me. Your boat was here. Where —"

The beep cuts her off. She stands holding a phone that has told her nothing.

The drawers under the desk are half out. One has a folder wedged in the runner. She pulls it.

FERRY SAINT BRENDAN. SAILINGS, 1998. The paper has gone soft as cloth and smells of the bottom of a cupboard. The first two pages are freight — pallets, a tractor part, four crates of live birds. The third page is people.

Halfway down, in typewriter ink: FERREIRA, ANA M. — 19 — DECKHAND — SIGNED ON.

A line drawn through it in ballpoint. Beside the line, in the same ink, a single word: *no*.

Two names down: IVO, R. — 24 — ORDINARY SEAMAN. No line through that one.

The column headed DESTINATION is blank all the way down the page. All three pages. Every one of them.

Under the folder, a second sheet, soft with old water. A sailing notice, stamped, for the fourteenth of March. At the bottom, a line of handwriting she knows better than her own — the loops, the flat-topped a, the way her mother crosses a t with a hook.

*Struck through. She doesn't sail.*

Jo puts both hands flat on the desk and looks at the wall of empty pigeonholes until the ticking sound resolves into her own pulse.

The phone buzzes against the wood. She reads it before she has decided to.

*Sunday 06:29. Ana. She's in the office. She's read the page.*

Her thumb is on the glass. She writes, for the first time since the storm: *who is this*

The reply is immediate, and it is not for her.

*Not you.*

ACT THREE

Outside, the wind has backed. The ramp is longer going down than it was coming up. The dinghy sits in six inches of water, and she hauls it out to the edge of the channel with the painter over her shoulder, boots filling, the cold coming up through her jeans to the hip.

She doesn't go home. She sits on the slip's bottom step with the folder open on her knees and reads her mother's name four more times, and then she does the thing she has been circling since she read the word *no*.

*she's not here. she left in her boat. you want me to tell her something, tell me.*

Water slaps. A gull argues with another gull.

Then, in order, three messages, as if someone has been holding them:

*You're the one who found it.*

*I've been writing to her for eleven years.*

*Sunday 06:31. Jo. Put her on.*

The folder slides off her knees into the weed. She gets one boot on it before the wind takes the loose pages, and then she is on her hands in the green water grabbing at them, and the last one goes under, and she comes up with a fistful of wet paper and her breath tearing.

At the top of the thread — above the message about the gate, above the one about the tide stairs, above every text she has read and not understood — there is a name.

Not hers.

A.

## 5. Low Tide Message

COLD OPEN

The tide is out so far the harbour looks like a bathtub someone pulled the plug on. Mud flats shine where water was an hour ago. Boats sit on their keels in channels too narrow for them, and the whole town smells of rot and salt and old rope.

Jo stands at the edge of the ferry slip with the cracked phone in her fist and her boots sinking.

Behind her, the harbour office window is dark. In front of her, across the flats, the decommissioned slip runs out into nothing, its pilings furred with barnacle, a single cleat worn silver by thirty years of hands that aren't there anymore.

The phone buzzes.

*Sunday 06:14. Ana. The office. Now.*

She doesn't move. Above the town, on the noticeboard outside the co-op, a sheet of paper flaps: HARBOUR CLOSURE — BRING FORWARD. The date has been changed by hand, in marker, from September to the twenty-eighth. That's four days.

Four days until the town stops being a town.

The phone buzzes again. Same sender. Same name.

*She's already there. Go.*

ACT ONE

The office door is unlocked, which it never is. Inside, the marker smell is so strong it's a taste. Ana is at the chart table with her back to the door, and she has been drawing for a long time — there are three ruined tide charts crumpled on the floor, and one in front of her, and on it the black ink has gone in a shape Jo has seen before.

A name. Not a date. A name, boxed in, boxed over, boxed over again until the paper has gone soft.

"Office is closed," Ana says, without turning.

"You changed the date." Jo stays in the doorway. "On the board. That's not your job."

"Nothing's my job in four days." Ana caps the marker. Sets it down parallel to the chart's edge. It's the same careful way she does everything, and it makes Jo want to break something. "What do you want, Jo."

"You drew over a name on a chart in 1998." Jo steps in. The floorboards give. "And a phone's been texting me about it."

Ana turns around.

Her face does a thing Jo has never seen it do. Not surprise. Recognition — the wrong kind, the kind you get when the bill finally arrives and you've been expecting it for eleven years.

"Where did you get that."

"Beach. After the storm." Jo holds it up. "It's cracked. It shouldn't work. It works."

"Give it to me."

"No."

"Jo."

"No." Jo's voice comes out wrong — too loud, then too quiet. "You've been lying to me since the day I could talk. I'm not giving you the one thing that's been telling me the truth."

Ana stands very still. Then she says, quietly, "You don't know what it's telling you."

"Then tell me."

The clock over the door ticks. Outside, a gull hits the gutter and scatters.

"Her name was Maren," Ana says. "She had a boat. She left in it." A breath. "She's not coming back. That's the whole story, and you don't get to make it a bigger one."

"That's not the whole story."

"It's the one I'm giving you." Ana picks up the marker again. "Now give me the phone, or get out of my office."

Jo gets out of her office.

ACT TWO

The pier at seven in the morning has a quality of light that makes everything look abandoned on purpose. Jo sits on the rail above the arcade with her legs through the bars and reads back through every message from the beginning.

*Who is this,* she sent. And the phone said: *Not you.*

And then: *You're the one who found it.*

And then: *I've been writing to her for eleven years.*

And then, this morning, first light, before any of it: *Sunday 06:31. Jo. Put her on.*

She types. Her thumbs are shaking and she hates that.

*she won't take it. she says it's nothing. she says the name is maren and she left in a boat and that's all.*

The reply is slower this time. She watches the little dots move, stop, move again. Somewhere below, a man hoses down the fish tables and the water runs off the end of the pier in a white sheet.

*Maren is my mother. I'm the baby she left with. I'm thirty-one. I live two towns over and I've never met you.*

Jo's hands go cold.

*The phone was hers. She threw it in the water the night she left. It comes back. It keeps coming back. Eleven years and it keeps washing up where someone will find it.*

*Someone who can take it to Ana.*

Jo looks up. The harbour is emptying of water. Boats leaning, ropes slack, the whole town tilting.

*Why not you?* she types. *Why not just walk in.*

*Because she won't open the door for me.*

*She'll open it for you.*

ACT THREE

The farewell is the same night as the closure, because of course it is, and it's held in the arcade because the arcade is the only building big enough. Jo works the counter. Her mother stands by the door with the harbour master, nodding at things, and every four minutes Jo catches her looking at the phone in Jo's apron pocket.

At nine, the power to the machines is cut and the town stands around under the dead lights with paper cups and says goodbye to itself.

Ana finds her at the claw machine.

"Four days was fast," Jo says. "Who asked for it fast?"

Ana doesn't answer that. Her eyes are on Jo's pocket. "Give it here."

"I texted her back."

Ana's hand stops halfway to the pocket.

"She's thirty-one," Jo says. "She lives two towns over. She's been writing to you for eleven years and you changed the date on the closure board the day she found out how to reach you."

Ana's mouth opens. Closes to a line.

"You moved our leaving up," Jo says. "You moved our whole life up by six weeks rather than open a door."

"You don't understand what happened."

"I understand what you're doing." Jo takes the phone out. Slaps it into Ana's palm. Holds on to it for one more second. "She's not Maren. She's the baby."

The look on Ana's face goes through her like a hook.

Ana's thumb finds the glass. She stands there a long time, and the town talks around them, and then she types something short, and something short, and then she takes a breath and starts to type the long one — the one Jo can see her reading back over, line by line, wiping at her eye with her wrist.

Jo turns to go. Ana's other hand catches her sleeve.

The message lands on Jo's phone a second before Ana's does.

*Sunday 06:29. Ana. She's in the office. She's read the page.*

Jo's thumb is on the glass. She writes, for the first time since the storm: *who is this*

The reply is immediate, and it is not for her.

*Not you.*

Outside, the wind has backed. Jo walks the ramp down to the slip with her boots filling and hauls the dinghy out, and she doesn't go home. She sits on the bottom step of the ferry slip with the folder open on her knees and does the thing she's been circling for days.

*she's not here. she left in her boat. you want me to tell her something, tell me.*

Then, in order, three messages, as if someone has been holding them:

*You're the one who found it.*

*I've been writing to her for eleven years.*

*Sunday 06:31. Jo. Put her on.*

The folder slides off her knees into the weed. She gets one boot on it before the wind takes the loose pages and she's on her hands in the green water grabbing, the last one going under, and she comes up with a fistful of wet paper and her breath tearing.

At the top of the thread — above the gate, above the tide stairs, above every text she's read and not understood — there is a name.

Not hers.

And her mother's handwriting on the wet page in her fist, bleeding.

*SHE KNOWS WHERE I AM. DON'T LET HER COME.*

Jo reads it twice. Then she looks up the ramp, at the harbour office with its one lit window, and starts walking back up.

Halfway, her phone buzzes, and it isn't M. It's Ana, and it's a single line:

*Where are you. Bring it back. There's something on this one you need to see.*

Jo looks at the time on the cracked screen.

Sunday 06:32.
