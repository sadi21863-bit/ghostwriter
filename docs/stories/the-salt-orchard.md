# The Salt Orchard

_Novel · linear · story type: linear · genres: literary fantasy · 8052 words_

## 1. The Inheritance

The grave was too shallow.

They had cut through the red soil to a hand’s depth of grey clay, and under the clay the bar rang and would not bite. Ilya stood at the head of the hole with the road’s dust still white on her shoes and caught herself reading the cut face the way she read a core sample — sand, sand, clay, refusal — before the priest cleared his throat, and she made herself look at the coffin instead.

It went down badly. The ropes were old; one of the four men lost his footing on the spoil heap; the box tipped and struck the clay and settled at an angle, shoulder first, as though her father had somewhere to be.

Nobody spoke. The village stood back from the grave in a loose crescent. She knew six of the faces. The six looked at her, then at the sky, which was the colour of the ground.

She had expected the heat. She had not expected the quiet — the cisterns in the lane cracked open and dry, the standpipe in the square with its handle wired shut, the salt bloom on the stones marking a tide line at ankle height where water had last stood and then stopped standing.

Afterwards the priest shook her hand and said her father had been a patient man, which she did not believe, and the crescent came apart and went back to its houses without offering her anything to drink.

Sena was waiting at the top of the lane, in black, her hands folded. Eighty-one, and straight as a gatepost.

“You remember the orchard,” she said. It was not a question.

“I remember the stories.”

“Come and see it before you go.”

The wall was prickly pear, grown eight feet and shaggy with age, and there was a gate in it. Sena lifted the latch. The air came out at them cold, and wet, and green — a smell so particular that Ilya’s eyes stung before she had taken a step.

Inside, the trees ran down the slope in rows: quince, bitter orange heavy in fruit, fig, olive. Grass to the knee. A bird somewhere in the leaves, and under everything the sound of moving water.

She crouched at the first row and pushed her fingers into the soil. Wet to the second knuckle. She wiped them on her trousers and looked downhill, past the last trees to the sea, and did the arithmetic she could not switch off: a lens of fresh water over salt, thin as a coin, this slope draining it, the village above with its cisterns dry.

“The spring comes from the hill,” she said.

“The spring comes from under,” said Sena.

A man stood at the wall with a coil of rope over his shoulder and a bar in one hand, salt dried white on his forearms from the elbow down. He had been one of the four at the grave. He looked at the hole her fingers had made in the soil, and then at her, and said nothing at all, and went away down the row between the trees.

“Wick,” Sena said. “He works the tides.”

The spring was at the bottom of the orchard where the trees stopped: a low stone basin the size of a table, its lip worn round, the water in it black and glassy and moving, though no wind reached the place. A channel ran out of it, green-stained. She followed the channel with her eye. It went seaward. Downhill. The wrong way.

“The ledger,” said Sena, and put a bundle in her hands. Oilcloth, and inside it canvas, and inside that a book with a broken spine and pages gone soft as cloth at the edges, ruled in columns and written in a dozen hands, some careful and some not.

The first column was a date. The second was a name. The third was a tree.

“Every one the water took,” Sena said. “We write them in. It’s owed.”

Ilya turned the pages. The names were old, then less old, then there were years with two to a page and years with none. Her thumb found the last written line without being told to.

Marchetti, Tommaso. Eleven days ago. In the third column, in a hand that did not shake: the fourth quince from the basin wall.

She looked up. The tree was in front of her, ten paces off, its fruit hanging at shoulder height, pale and faintly wrong in the way fruit is wrong when it has gone past ripe.

“He drowned in his own bed,” Ilya said. “His heart.”

“The water took him,” Sena said. “I write what’s owed.”

“You’ve kept a book of drownings for—” She did not finish. She closed it. “I have to be back at the office by September. There’s a survey in Cádiz. I have a flat with a door that locks.”

“One season,” said Sena. “One turn of the water. If you still hear only a story, you go, and I’ll not say another word about it.”

Something moved in the grass at the base of the tree. The water went on moving in the basin, moving the wrong way.

“Don’t eat what falls,” Sena said from the gate. “Not unless you want to remember.”

Then she was gone up the row, and the orchard was very quiet, and Ilya stood there with the book in her arms.

She meant to put it down on the wall. She meant to walk up the lane and find the bus and be back in the city by Thursday, and she was composing the sentence she would say to the old woman — you can’t chart a story, you can’t take the depth of a thing by hearsay — when she looked down and found the pale quince in her hand.

She did not remember picking it.

The skin was warm. It carried a bloom like the bloom on the stones below the cisterns, and the stem was still wet.

## 2. Salt in the Roots

She was at the basin before the light had properly come up, her kit spread on the wall in its canvas roll: the refractometer in its case, two sample bottles, the tape, the brass sounding line she had carried since her first year on the coast and never once used inland.

The water lay black and still and moved anyway.

She filled a bottle to the line and held it up against the paling sky. Clear. She would have called it clean. She put a drop on the prism and held it to her eye and read three parts per thousand at the surface, and when she drew from the bottom with the bottle inverted and her arm in to the elbow, eleven. Brackish. Not enough to taste as sea, not on the first mouthful. She tasted it anyway. Salt at the back of the tongue, and under the salt something older, iron or green.

The lip of the basin had a tide mark of its own — a dark line, a hand's width down from the rim, furred with moss that had died. She chalked the present level and wrote the hour in her book.

At eight the water had not moved. At nine it had. By half past ten it stood an inch and a half above her chalk, and the channel below it had gone from a trickle to a run.

She unfolded the tide table from the port office in Cádiz, softened along its creases from a season in her coat pocket. Flood at the harbour, seven fifty-two. Her basin had come up forty minutes behind it, slow and steady, the way water comes up through sand.

She checked the arithmetic twice. Then she walked the channel down to where the trees stopped and the slope fell away toward the sea, and measured, and checked it again. The basin sat above the tideline. The channel ran downhill. Water did not climb hills, and yet the level in the basin was doing what the sea was doing, an hour and a half inland and four metres up a slope.

A man was standing at the wall with a bar over his shoulder, watching her hold a tape measure against nothing in particular.

“You’re Wick,” she said.

He nodded once.

“Where does the spring come from?”

He looked at the basin. Then he came down the row, unhurried, and crouched at the channel where a flat stone lay across it, and lifted the stone out and set it aside. The water in the channel dropped away from its banks and went on down the slope, and behind her she heard the basin settle — a small swallowing sound, the level easing down.

He put the stone back. The water climbed.

“Salt in it,” he said. Not a question, either. He had a way of saying things as though he were confirming what the ground had already told him.

“Some.”

“More at the bottom.”

“Yes.”

He straightened, wiped his hands on his trousers, and looked at her kit on the wall without curiosity, the way a man looks at a tool he has seen before in other hands. Then he took his bar and went off along the wall, and she heard him long after she lost sight of him, working somewhere in the prickly pear, cutting.

She spent the afternoon with the ledger.

The hands changed every few pages and the dates were sometimes the harvest and sometimes the burial, and by the fourth year of her reading she had the tide table beside it and had begun to be afraid of how well they matched. Not the day. The hour. The entries with an hour written beside them kept to a narrow band of the month — the springs, when the water was highest and most pulled.

She read past names she knew, past the year she was born, past a line in a hand she thought she recognised and was not ready to name. She did not read to the end. She told herself the light was going.

At Sena’s table there was bread and oil and small fried fish and a jug of water beaded with cold, and Ilya was thirsty, and the water tasted of the sea. Faintly. A quarter of what she had drawn from the bottom of the basin, no more, but it was there, and she set the cup down and looked at the old woman, who was eating and had not noticed, or had.

“Your cistern’s gone brackish,” Ilya said.

“Everything down here is a little brackish.” Sena tore bread. “It’s a seaside.”

“No. Your cistern. Which means it’s drawing from the same body as the orchard.” She heard herself building the sentence and did not stop building it. The day had been long and strange and she had wanted, suddenly and with a violence that surprised her, to be the one who said the true thing first. “The level in the basin follows the tide. Forty minutes behind the harbour. It’s not a spring in the way you mean it. The sea’s in the roots, Sena. It comes up under the hill and the trees are sitting in it.”

Sena set down her spoon.

She did not argue. She did not do anything for a while. The fire moved, and the shadows of the fish bones on the plate moved, and the old woman sat with her hands flat on the table and her eyes on the middle distance, and the silence went on so long that Ilya heard the orchard through the open door — water going the wrong way, downhill, seaward, all night.

“You’ll have read the ledger,” Sena said at last.

“Some.”

“Not to the end.”

“No.”

Sena rose and took the book down from the shelf and laid it on the table between them, and opened it at the back — not the last page, but near it, where the ink was brown and the paper had been mended with a strip of cloth. She turned it around with two fingers.

The third column said: the first fig, by the wall.

The second column said: Marchetti.

The first column said a year, and beside it an hour, and Ilya looked at the hour and then, against her will, at the tide table folded in her pocket.

“That was my grandmother’s grandmother’s brother,” Sena said. “He was six. I was not born.” She closed the book on her finger. “You’ll want to sleep. The lamp’s lit up the lane.”

Outside, above the wall, the dark had a shape to it that had not been there before — a man sitting with his arms around his knees, still as a stone in a channel, looking down at the water.

## 3. The Ledger

She went up the lane with the lamp and stopped below the wall.

Wick had not moved. In the light his face was the colour of the stones behind him, and he was watching the channel where he had lifted the flat stone and laid it back. She climbed the wall and sat down a body's length from him and set the lamp between them. Below, the water went on going seaward, small and continuous, a sound she could not now unhear.

“You’ll take the damp.”

“I’ve taken it.”

She waited. He came to a thing by his own road, and hurrying him was like hurrying the tide.

“Not from here,” he said at last. “People think I am. I let them.”

“Where, then?”

He put his chin on his knees and did not answer, and in time she left him the lamp and went down.

---

Sena had the book open before Ilya had finished her bread. The morning light lay along the table in a bar, and the old woman’s finger came down on the page like a fly settling.

“You read it like a list,” Sena said. “It isn’t a list.”

Three columns. The first was a year, and sometimes an hour beside it, small, pushed in afterwards as if the writer had meant to go back and had not. The second was a name. The third was a tree.

“The year is when the water took them. The hour, when there’s an hour, is when it was highest — the springs, mostly. You’ve worked that out for yourself or you wouldn’t look so sick.”

“I’m not sick.”

“You’re the colour of the channel.” Sena turned a page with two fingers, mended along the gutter with a strip of cloth. “Every drowning has a page. Every page has a name. Every name has a tree. That’s the whole of it. The rest is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is yours.”

“And the tree is the one they planted for them.”

“The tree is where they are.”

Ilya looked at the third column and made herself read it properly for the first time. *The first fig, by the wall.* Not a description. A place. You walked the wall west to east and counted, and the book did not lie about what a thing was, so the first two trees you came to did not count, because they were not figs.

“How many are there?”

“Two hundred and eleven names. Ninety-something trees. Some are gone. Some are stumps with the ring of them left in the ground, and those still count.”

“And the hands?”

“Four keepers in that book. My mother, and hers, and her mother’s brother before he was lost, and me. The hands change.” Sena closed her finger on the page. “The water doesn’t.”

She went out to the orchard with her bar and her apron and left Ilya at the table, and Ilya told herself she would read to the end of the year she was born and then stop.

The page was not where it should have been. She found it by the hand — a hand she knew from the front of the book, from the papers in her father’s desk, upright and thin, the loop of the *M* always collapsing.

The year was hers. The hour was a spring tide, given in the old way with the month beside it.

The name was Renata Marchetti.

The tree was the almond that leans, by the gate.

She sat with it. Outside, a bar went into the ground somewhere and came out, and a bird went up complaining, and the bar went in again. The ink was brown. Somebody had come back afterwards and traced the letters over so that a woman three generations later could read them without squinting, and Ilya found that she was angry about this, and could not have said at what.

---

Wick was at the channel in the afternoon, setting the flat stone back in its notch, and she stood over him with her book shut and her hands shaking and asked him.

“What did she tell you about this place?”

He straightened. He looked at the book, then at her face, and then at the orchard, and whatever he saw there he did not say.

“I was pulled out,” he said.

“Out of what?”

“The water. I was a boy.” He wiped his palms down his thighs. “Off the point, in a blow. I don’t remember it. I remember being cold and I remember a woman’s arms and I remember the bar going into the ground all that year, and after that I remember everything.” He nodded at the book. “They think I fell off a boat. There wasn’t a boat.”

The channel ran between them. She heard it going down the slope, out through the trees, toward the sea it had come from.

“Was there a page?” she said.

Wick crouched and laid his hand flat on the water, and the water went round his fingers and went on.

“Ask her what she wrote the night they pulled me out,” he said. “Ask her why it isn’t in the book.”

## 4. What the Water Knows

The wind came round before dawn and took the loose tile over the bread oven, and by the time the light should have been grey in the lane it was not light at all.

Ilya stood in the doorway with her boots unlaced and read the sky the way she had been paid to read water. A spring tide, and a blow behind it, and the two of them meeting in the same hour off a coast with no shelf to speak of. She had written that combination into reports. She had never once had to stand in it.

The channel was already over.

It came up the lane in a skin of brown, thin at first, finding the joints between the stones and then covering them. Below the wall, the lower orchard was gone to the ankles, then the knees. The trunks stood in it like pilings. The water was not moving like rain. It was moving like the sea, with a long push and a longer pull, and it brought weed with it, and a smell she knew from the deck of every boat she'd ever surveyed.

Sena was out in it. Apron to the thighs, bar in her fist, going from trunk to trunk and setting the bar in and levering the soil open so the water had somewhere to go.

“The salt,” Ilya said, coming down the bank. “The salt will kill them.”

“Not in one tide.”

“Two tides. You want to stand here and count them?”

Sena did not look up. “I have counted them.” She put the bar in, leaned, opened a channel in the earth, and the flood ran into it, sullen, and did not thank her. “Take the far row. West. Do what I do.”

So she did. For an hour, two, she put a bar into ground she had no right to and opened it, and the water went where she sent it, and the rain came sideways, and her hands stopped being cold and became something she was using. Wick was on the wall with the flat stones, lifting them and laying them back to let the flow through, and he did it fast and without hurry, the way he did everything.

When the tide turned the water began to go, and it went out the way it had come, with the long pull, leaving the rows combed flat and shining and the smell of open sea lying over everything.

They sat in the lee of the wall. Sena's hands were shaking and she held them out in front of her and looked at them as if they belonged to someone she was disappointed in.

“Ask it, then,” she said. “It's been in your mouth since the fig.”

Ilya wiped the rain off her face. “Where does the spring come from.”

“You know where.”

“There's no spring under an orchard a hundred paces from the cliff. There's no water table that fresh this close to the sea. The salinity would—” She stopped. She heard her own voice, the flat professional certainty of it, and heard how it sounded out here. “The cliff is porous.”

“The cliff is broken,” Sena said. “There's a fault in it you could put a boat through. The sea goes in at the point and comes up under the orchard a day later, and it comes up sweet. Something in the rock takes the salt out of it. I don't know what. I know that it does.”

“And the drought.”

“The sea doesn't have droughts.” Sena turned her hands over. “That's the bargain. The village drinks from the sea and doesn't know it, and the year the sea forgets us we'll know it fast enough.”

Ilya sat with that. It was not a story. It was a hydrology, badly stated, and she could feel it fitting the shape of the coast like a hand in a glove.

“And the trees,” she said. “What do they take up with it.”

Sena looked at her for a long moment, and then she got up, and went along the wall, and came back with a fig. Late, small, split at the eye, rain still standing in the split.

“Everything the water meets, it takes a print of,” she said. “The sea has been taking prints a long while. The trees drink it and the fruit keeps it, and when the living eat it they get a piece of the dead for a night. That's why the book has a tree for a name. The tree is where they are. You want to meet her, you eat what her tree makes.”

“You're telling me the fruit is haunted.”

“I'm telling you what it is. You can call it a word.”

Ilya took it. It was cold and it weighed nothing and she was aware, absurdly, of her own hand being steady.

She bit.

The taste was fig, and dust, and then it wasn't a taste. It was a room she had never stood in, low-ceilinged, with the light coming through a shutter and a woman at a table with her sleeves pushed to the elbow, wringing something out over a bowl. Her hands were red from the water. She was speaking to someone off to the side in a voice that went up at the end, the way you speak to a child who is not listening, and the words were nothing, and Ilya understood them anyway, the way you understand a face. The woman turned her head and looked out at a window that had no glass in it, and there was salt drying white on the sill, and her hair was wet, and she was tired, and she was not afraid.

Then the wall, and the rain, and the fig in her hand with one bite out of it.

“Her name was Renata,” Sena said.

Ilya looked at the fruit. The cut flesh was pale and full of seeds and no different from any other fig in the world.

She put the rest of it in her mouth and chewed and swallowed, and it was only fig now, only sweet, and whatever she had been given she had been given once and it was not coming back.

“Your father ate from that tree every year,” Sena said, getting up, taking her bar. “The night before the fast. He never said a word about it to me and I never asked him.”

The lower rows drained slowly into the channel. Somewhere west a bough had come down and lay in the mud with its fruit still on it, a hundred small dark eyes.

Ilya walked to the gate and stood under the almond that leans, and put her hand flat on the bark, which was rough and cold and hers.

## 5. The Buyer

The car came up the coast road at the flat hour, black and slow, the only car on that road in a month. It stopped at the gate because the gate was where the road stopped being a road.

The man who got out had the wrong shoes. He looked at the mud the way a man looks at a bill, then came on anyway, picking his way along the wall.

“Miss Marchetti.” He had a folder under his arm in a plastic sleeve. “Kemp. I wrote.”

“The letter came.”

“You didn’t answer it.”

“The letter didn’t ask a question.”

He smiled at that, briefly, as though marking it. Sena had come up from the rows and stood with her bar across her body, saying nothing, and Kemp looked at her once and then did not look at her again.

“I’ll be plain, then. We’re putting a line in from the point to the main road. Not for the village — for the water. Eight-inch, buried, pumps at this end, a tanker every nine days off the hardstanding. Eleven thousand cubic metres a month to start.” He opened the folder. “Paid by volume, indexed. Plus a cistern for the village, a new roof on that hall, and the road graded twice a year.”

“The aquifer,” Ilya said.

“The aquifer.”

“It isn’t an aquifer.”

He waited, polite, the folder open.

“An aquifer is a body of rock that holds water and gives it up,” she said. “I’ve surveyed intakes from Sines to Klaipeda and I know exactly what a pumping drawdown does to a coastal lens. You pull the fresh off the top, the interface comes up behind it, and in three years you’re lifting brine and paying to dispose of it. I’ll take your money and your cistern and in three years this village will be drinking salt and you’ll be gone.”

“That’s a considered no.”

“It’s a no.”

Kemp closed the folder. He looked past her, at the rows, at the trees standing in the wet with their fruit on them. “Your father said no to me twice,” he said. “Two years running. Then he wrote and asked me to come back after the fast. Said he wanted to talk about it properly.” He put the folder under his arm. “I came back.”

The rain had thinned to nothing. The sea was making its long sound below the cliff.

“He never told me that,” Ilya said.

“He wouldn’t have. He was a man who liked to hold a thing until he was sure of it.” Kemp went back along the wall, and the car turned in the yard and went away down the road, and the sound of it lasted longer than it should have.

Sena drove her bar into the ground and leaned on it. “He’ll be back with paper,” she said.

That night the rain stopped for the first time in nine days and the sky went clear and cold and full of stars, and the water in the lower rows went down and lay flat and took the starlight.

Wick was in the yard when she came out. He had not knocked. He was standing with his boots in the wet and his hands at his sides, and she knew from his stillness.

“The feeder,” he said.

Below the second weir, where the channel ran in under the wall, the bank had been cut. Not washed. Cut, with a clean edge, a spade’s width, and the earth packed back after to hide it until the water found it. In the cut a tin lay on its side, five litres, unlabelled, and the smell coming off the channel was not the sea. It was thin and sharp and it went into the back of her throat like a coin.

The eels were the first thing. A dozen of them, silver sides up, in the channel below the cut. Then the weed going brown at the tips. Then, along the lower row, the leaves beginning to curl at the margins as though something had taken a flame to them and stopped.

Sena came down at first light because Sena always came down at first light. She walked past Ilya without a word, into the water, and put her hand in it and brought it up and tasted it off her fingers. Then she walked to the first tree and put her palm on the trunk and held it there a while.

She said, “Not the roots.” Then her knees went.

They got her up to the house and onto the bed and she was breathing, but her hand would not close when Ilya asked it to, and her mouth had gone slack on one side, and she looked at Ilya and said a word twice that Ilya could not make out and then stopped trying.

By noon the lower row had dropped half its fruit and the village was at the gate. Not angry yet. Waiting, which was worse. Someone said the word poison and it went along the wall like a spark in dry grass.

Ilya stood in the yard with her hands still shaking and did the arithmetic she had been doing all morning. Clean water trucked in was money. New soil, if it came to that, was more money. A lawyer, more again. There was one man on that road in a month and he had a folder and a number and a cistern.

And there was the other account, the one that could not be spent. She could stand in front of the village and tell them what was under the orchard. She could say: you have been drinking the sea, and the sea has been keeping count, and this is what it costs when someone sells it. Say it, and the fast, the book, the tree, the whole long bargain would come apart in an afternoon, and they would be a poor village with poisoned water and nothing else.

Wick was at the gate holding it, saying nothing, watching her.

She went inside past him. On the shelf under the stairs, Sena’s ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, the size of a paving stone. She had been reading it a few pages a night and putting it back. She had told herself it was grief that made her slow.

She set it on the table and opened it at the beginning and turned to the first name, and the second.

Whatever had been done to this orchard had been done by someone who knew the channel and knew the hour. It had been done before, she was certain, and there was no record in the world that went back far enough but this one, and it had not been read to the end in her lifetime.

## 6. Tides

They dug from first light, and the digging was the only thing that helped.

Ilya cut the diversion first — a trench a spade wide, taking the clean water off above the second weir and running it wide of the place where the tin had lain. Wick worked below her in the mud and did not need telling where to put the blade. He knew the fall of the ground. He cut his sods in rectangles and stacked them grass down, the way a man stacks them who intends to put them back.

The eels they gathered in a bucket, a dozen and four more, and Wick carried them up and tipped them into the channel above the weir one at a time, slowly, as though he were saying a name for each.

By the middle of the morning the smell had thinned. Not gone. Thinned. They lifted the upper sluice a notch and let a sheet of water run through the poisoned length and out along the cut and away down the shore, and did it again, and again, and the weed below the wall went from brown to a grey that might, in a week, be green.

Sena's word, the one she had said twice and given up on, turned out to be lime. Ilya found it in the ledger's margin, in Sena's own hand, with a date from eleven years ago and a spade-width cut described in six words. So it had been done before. She turned back a page, and another, and found it again, older, in a hand that looped its tails, and again, and the further back she went the shorter the entries, until she came to the front board and the thread that had been waxed and tied and never cut.

The page was folded into the board itself, sewn in, sealed with a wafer of red wax gone the colour of a dried cut. She worked it out with the point of a knife.

Two columns of names. Eight of them, in a hand so old the ink had gone brown at the edges of the letters. Under them a date and one word, and the word was *drowned*. Marchetti was the fourth. The others she had been reading for nine nights, a few pages at a time, down the long list of the sea's takings — the same names, at the top of the list, at the very beginning of it, before there was an orchard.

Wick came in with the bucket and stood in the doorway and did not put it down.

“You've got to the front,” he said.

“The founders drowned.”

“The founders drowned,” he said. “There was a village on the flat there, and a spring under it that wasn't a spring, and the sea came in one November and took the whole of it, and the eight who were left walked up here and planted trees in the ground the water had made fresh.” He set the bucket down. “That's the bargain. Eight drowned, and the ground remembers them, and it gives back what it took.”

“Gives back,” Ilya said.

He crossed the floor and put his thumb on the last name in the right-hand column and held it there. It was a short name. It went under his thumb entire.

“I was twelve,” he said. “The year the road came up the valley, that was the year after. I ate and I stopped.” He took his thumb away. “I have not had a day older since, and I have not been able to taste a thing but salt since Tuesday, and my teeth ache from here to here.” He touched his jaw, the hinge of it, and dropped his hand. “The trees keep me. I'm the last of the eight. When they're sick, I'm sick. When they go, I go.”

The channel ran behind him, thin and cold and going about its work.

“How long,” she said.

“Ask Sena.” He looked at the bed in the corner, at the slack hand on the blanket. “She's the one that wrote me down. She's the one that's been feeding me the fruit at the fast, every year, and not saying why, and I've not asked her, because a man who asks is a man who's decided he'd rather not know.”

Ilya turned the page in her hand, and the next one, and there it was, three lines above the founders' date, in a hand she knew better than her own because it had taught her her letters — the crossing of the t, the flat foot of the r.

Her own name.

No date after it. Nothing after it but a space and the rest of the page, going brown at the edge.

The rain had held off all day and the light through the window was long and low and lay across the table, and the two ledgers, and the bucket of eels, and Wick standing in it with his hands at his sides.

## 7. The Debt

Sena died in the hour before the light, and Ilya heard it from the table — the long breath that did not come back, and then a smaller sound that was only the blanket settling.

She did not get up at once. She finished the page she was on, because it was the last of the takings and she had promised herself, without ever saying it aloud, that she would read the book to the end before she did anything else in the world. Then she closed it and put both hands flat on the cover and sat while the window went from black to grey.

Wick came in with the empty bucket and stopped.

“Before the light,” she said.

He set the bucket down without a sound. He went to the bed and looked at Sena a while, and then he took the corner of the blanket and drew it up over her face, and his hands were careful, and he said nothing at all.

The end of the ledger was not names. The names were the front of it, the way a house is its door. The back leaves were accounts: a column of years, and against each year a yield — so many crates, so many barrels, so many to the carrier’s cart — and against each year, in a different ink, a short column of figures that did not agree with the first. Ilya had balanced a hundred books in her working life. Sena had balanced hers by writing off, every year, one line, always the same, in the same four words: *to the water*.

The last leaf was newer than the rest. Two lines in Sena’s hand, pressed hard enough to score the paper, and nothing under them but the ruled space and the brown edge.

*What the sea lends, the ground collects.*

*The book stays with the family that keeps it. If the keeping stops, the water comes up.*

At first light they came to the gate, a dozen of them, and the woman who kept the shop had brought the boatman’s boy, whose arms were red to the elbow, and held him out in front of her the way you hold out a lamp.

“She’s been here a week,” the woman said.

“Nine days,” Ilya said.

“Nine days, and the channel’s poached, and my sister’s eels floated, and the boy’s broke out in a rash like a burn. It stood here forty years. Your father never sold a thing and it stood.”

“Say the rest of it,” a man behind her said.

The woman’s mouth worked. “You’re a Marchetti. There’s four of that name in the front of that book and the first three are drowned.”

Wick came and stood in the doorway with his arms crossed and his jaw set, and the ones at the back of the crowd looked at him and then at their feet.

Ilya went to the desk and took out the packet her father had left in the bottom drawer, oilcloth and string, and carried it out into the yard and set it on the mounting block where they could all see it, and opened it, and laid the papers out flat with a stone at each corner because the wind was off the water.

“Nine days ago I told you the water was soured from a buried tin,” she said. “I told you the cut was made by somebody who knew the channel. Here is the somebody.” She pointed. “A price per hectare, payable on vacant possession, and these three words, in the schedule, in the buyer’s own hand. *Sealing of the spring.* Nobody buys an orchard to kill it. They buy it to sell the water under it, and a dead orchard is a cheaper purchase than a living one.”

They crowded in, the ones who could read leaning down, and the ones who could not leaning on them.

“And this.” She took the iron peg out of her coat and set it on the paper, on the small stamped number in the corner of the plan. A survey peg, rusted, its head flattened from being driven. “I pulled it out of the mud at the second weir on the second morning. It was not in the mud before the tin was.” She turned the peg over with one finger. “The number on that plan is the number on this peg. It is a surveyor’s number. I am a surveyor.”

The man behind the woman said, very quietly, “Then it wasn’t her.”

“It was never her,” Wick said.

Nobody spoke for a while. Out on the cut an oystercatcher went over, calling.

“Burn it,” the shop-woman said. “And we say nothing, and no man from the city comes out here again with his plans and his money. The spring stays in the ground where it’s been.” She looked around at them. “We keep it to ourselves, and we get poor together, and we don’t sign.”

“We don’t sign,” the man said. Then the others, one after another, and one old fellow took his cap off and held it against his chest as though something had been read out.

Ilya carried the papers down to the tideline alone. The tide was going out and the sand was cold and firm and the wind took the first match. The second caught, and the flame ran up the plan of the channel and along the schedule and into the corner where the peg had lain, and the ash went out over the water in flakes that the gulls came down to look at and then left.

When she got back the house was full of women and the blanket was straight and someone had put the kettle on, and nobody would meet her eye, and the ledger was where she had left it with its brown edged page.

The fast was three weeks off. In nine days she had not once asked what happened at the fast, and Sena was under the blanket, and Wick was in the doorway with his hands at his sides, watching her.

## 8. Harvest

The fruit came back small and late and hard, and it came back.

She had spent the winter on the sluices, and on the channel, and on the low wall the tide had taken a yard of, and she had gone out every morning with the cold in her wrists and come back with her boots full. The trees stood in it the whole time and gave nothing, and she had begun to think the winter was the answer — that the orchard had spent forty years of Sena and was done.

Then in the fourth week after the turn the buds came, not the way buds come in a mild place, all at once and wasteful, but one here and one there, as if something were counting them. By the fast month the fruit hung green and nubbled and stingy. Nobody said it would be a good year. Nobody said it would be a year at all. But the village came up the track in the evenings to look, and the shop-woman brought her sister, and the boatman's boy stood at the gate with his arms bare and healed and asked whether he could carry a basket, and Ilya said yes.

No one had signed. The buyer's man came out once in a hired boat and stood at the gate in his city shoes, and Wick came and stood beside him, not near enough to touch and not far enough to be ignored, until the man looked at the sky and remembered the tide. That was the last of it.

She read the book in the evenings, and then she read it in the mornings too. The names at the front were not Marchettis only. She had thought they were, because the first four were, and because the woman at the gate had said so. But past the fourth the ink changed and the hand changed and the names were the village's — Lasky, Ondrej, a run of Cohens and then no Cohens, a Fen that went on for a page and a half, one line for a woman with no surname at all. Against each, in the margin, the same two words, cut short, like a breath let out. *To the water.*

She read them all. It took her most of the winter. She read them the way you'd walk a boundary — not for pleasure, not for grief, but because a boundary unwalked is not a boundary, and because Sena had read them and Sena's mother before her, and the book had stayed with the family that kept it.

What happened at the fast, she learned by being asked to do it.

They went down on the ebb with no lamps. The whole village on the shingle, turned out toward the water, and the book in her hands and her coat buttoned wrong because she had dressed in the dark. She read the names of the year's drowned — there were two, both old, neither of them a surprise to anyone — and then she read the year's line, the one the accounts had written off, and said the four words, and the sea took them without comment.

Then Wick, beside her, in his flat voice, said the next name before she had found it. Said it quietly, the way you'd say a thing you'd heard said every year of your life. He did not look at her. She turned the page and there it was.

She wrote her own name on the blank leaf after the keepers' names, under Sena's, and the date, and then she sat with the pen in her hand, because the leaf had room and the room asked a question. She did not write *to the water*. She wrote nothing at all after the date, and then, after a while, in her surveyor's hand, small and level, she wrote *keeps it*, and closed the book.

The fruit came in over three weeks. It was sour. It was sour in a way that made the children laugh and the old men suck their teeth, and it tasted of the ground and then, underneath, of something further out — salt and cold and a great deal of moving water. Ilya ate one down to the core and sat on the mounting block with the stem between her fingers for a long time.

In the last week of the picking she went down to the oldest tree, the one at the low corner where the wall had gone, and knelt to set a stone back under the root where the winter had washed it out. The soil gave more than it should have. She dug with her hands and the hole filled cool, and she dug deeper and it filled again, and then she stopped digging and simply looked.

Under the roots, in the dark of the hole, the water was moving. Not running — rising and falling, slow, patient, the way a chest rises and falls, up through the gravel and back down, and the root hairs moved with it, combed one way and then the other. The ground above went dark and then a shade lighter, dark and then lighter, and it kept time with nothing she could see and with everything she could hear.

She put her hand flat in the water and held it there until it went numb.
