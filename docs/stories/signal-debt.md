# Signal Debt

_Novel · linear · genres: science fiction + mystery · 4760 words_

## 1. “Routine Traffic”

A dead-letter queue never empties. Ada had explained that to two supervisors and a contract auditor, and none of them had liked it, so on the shift roster she simply logged the count and did the work. Calyx-9 had been dark eleven months; the yard had stopped paying for the relay in spring. What the queue still held was everything the station had failed to deliver before the lights went to battery tier — medical notices, freight manifests, a marriage license for a woman in Section C who had died before it could be sent.

The maintenance loop arrived at 03:22, or the queue admitted it at 03:22. Routine traffic, flagged corrupt, three fragments readable and a fourth chewed to hiss. Ada ran the checksum twice. Rebuilt the header by hand, because the automated repair would have flattened the interleave and she wanted the original timestamps intact.

Speakers up. The bay filled with the flat voice of a relay talking to itself.

“Calyx Nine relay, cycle seven, pressure nominal. Calyx Nine relay, cycle eight, pressure nominal.”

She scrolled the manifest of the source channel with one finger, not looking away from the waveform. Ladder code, the old prefix, four digits — nothing the yard had used in five years. Below it, another voice cut in, riding the gaps between the cycle calls like something squeezed into a doorway.

“Vale goes down in Dock Three. North gantry, by the tie-off rack.” A pause, and a small hitch on the s. “He’ll be stupid about it. He’s always stupid about it.”

Ada stopped the playback.

The relay resumed its litany, pressure nominal, cycle nine. She backed the loop up four seconds and let it run again. Same words. Same hitch. The voice sat inside the routine traffic as if the routine had made room for it, and the routing stamp along the bottom of the frame said the loop had been originated at 04:12 by a source the station had decommissioned out of existence.

The chronometer in the corner of her display read 03:34.

“That’s not a maintenance loop,” she said, to the empty archive. Her own voice sounded thin in the room; the archive was twelve racks of tape and a heater that cycled on whenever it felt like it, and she had stopped talking to it months ago on the theory that it encouraged her.

She pulled the fourth fragment. Corrupted, yes, but corruption had a shape. Splicing it against the readable three gave her eleven syllables before the noise swallowed the rest. Not enough to make a sentence of.

“Pressure nominal,” said the relay. “Pressure nominal.”

Three-twenty-nine, no. She carried the playback through the splice; the voice said twelve, plainly, twice — “oh-four-twelve” — the way a man repeats a thing he expects to be written down. Then the hiss, and the cycle calls, patient as a tide.

Ada opened a new case file and typed the timestamp first, because timestamps were the only thing in the world you couldn’t argue with. Then the routing stamp, the ladder prefix, the hash of the loop before she’d touched it, and an initial capture to the archive’s write-once store. Only after that did she open the comm board and put a call through to the watch desk.

The officer who answered had the voice of a man four hours into a shift with nothing in it. “Dock office, Third Watch.”

“This is Okafor, records. Calyx Nine. I need to report a—” She looked at the waveform while she chose the noun. “A threat against a person. On the station. A prediction, I suppose.”

“Say again, records?”

“I have an audio loop recovered from the dead-letter queue. In it, an unidentified male states that an Inspector Vale will be stabbed in Dock Three, north gantry, at oh-four-twelve. I want it logged, and I want someone at that gantry before the time.”

Keys, on the other end. Slow ones. “Vale. That’s the inspector out of the yard office? Spelled how I think it’s spelled?”

“I don’t know where he’s out of. That’s the whole of what the loop gives me.”

“And this came in tonight.”

“It came into the queue tonight. When it was made is a different question, and it’s the question I’d like the answer to before four o’clock.” A breath, and she made herself slow down. “I’m archivist of record on this station. My log entry is going to time-stamp at 03:37, and it will say I called you. If nothing happens at oh-four-twelve, that’s a footnote. If something does, you don’t want my entry to be the one that says nobody answered.”

The watch officer exhaled through his nose. “All right. I’ll put it to the duty inspector.”

“Put it in writing.”

“Records, you—” He stopped himself. More keys. “Logged 03:39, threat against person, source audio in Calyx Nine archive. Someone will call you back.”

The board went quiet. Ada sat with her hands flat on the desk, because they wanted to move and there was nothing left to do with them that wouldn’t spoil the chain of custody.

On the display, the relay counted cycles into the dark. Pressure nominal. Somewhere in the queue were nine hundred more letters that no one would ever read, and she had spent the last month calling them by their numbers, and now one of them had called her by name.

At 03:44 the loop’s source stamp flickered. Not the timecode — the origin field, which had read as a decommissioned medical channel for twenty-two minutes, and which now read as two channels at once, layered, one of them live.

Ada pulled the write-once store’s second copy and set it on a separate spindle before she looked at the origin field again.

## 2. “Routine Traffic”

The relay ticked past 03:51 before the hatch cycled. Vale came through it with his coat still fastened and a tablet held against his forearm like a man carrying a wound, and he did not look at the display first. He looked at her hands.

“Archivist.”

“Okafor.”

“I know.” A nod at the empty stool. “Sit down, Ms. Okafor. Walk me through it from the top and don’t editorialize.”

She walked him through it. The restore, the corrupted block, the voice coming up out of seventeen years of dead maintenance chatter. Vale listened with his head tipped slightly, and when she reached the prediction he held up two fingers without looking up from the tablet.

“At what point did the loop enter the queue?”

“The queue admits when it admits.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only true one.” Ada tapped the spindle housing. “Write-once store. The queue write and the relay stamp are the same event. Whatever routed it routed it tonight, between 02:58 and 03:07. When the audio was *made* is a different question, and it’s the one I want answered before oh-four-twelve.”

He set the tablet down flat. “There are nine hundred letters in your queue.”

“Nine hundred and eleven.”

“And this one made you pick up a phone at three in the morning.” Vale rocked back on his heels. “People confess to things on dead channels all the time. Old recordings. Somebody’s hobby. Somebody’s grudge. You want me to put a body on the deck at Dock Three because a machine said a name.”

“No,” Ada said. “I want you to put a body on the deck at Dock Three because it said *your* name.”

The inspector’s jaw worked once. Behind him, the viewport gave out on the long nothing of the terminator, and the station’s shadow crawled across it with the patience of something that had been doing that for thirty years.

At 04:09 he had a junior on the comm and a maintenance override keyed into Dock Three’s lighting. At 04:12 the deck cameras came up and showed a service lane between two cargo frames, a coolant puddle with a hard rim, and nothing else. At 04:13 the junior’s voice came back thin and fast: *sir, there’s a smear.*

Vale was moving before the sentence finished. Ada followed because nobody told her not to, and because her shoes were the only ones on the station that knew how the audio had sounded when it said the thing it said.

Dock Three smelled of hydraulic fluid and cold. Under the work lights, the smear resolved into a streak — a hand’s width, arterial enough to have found a seam in the deck plating and followed it — and a scuff of something dragged. Vale crouched with a light held low and swept it across the deck in a slow arc.

“Wet,” he said. “Twenty minutes. Thirty at the outside.”

“Whose?”

“Nobody’s. Yet.” He straightened. “There’s no one here and there’s no one in the lockers and the cargo frames read nominal. So either a body got up and walked, or—” He turned the light up the maintenance rail, where a dock drone should have been hanging in its cradle at shoulder height, charging.

The cradle was empty. Ada looked at the empty cradle for a while.

“That’s a drudge unit,” she said. “D-five class. They don’t move unless they’re told and they don’t leave the rail unless they’re loaded.”

“This one left the rail.”

“Then it was loaded.”

Vale brought the tablet up and thumbed the dock registry, and the registry came back with a gap where the drone’s last four hours should have been — not an error, a clean excised block, the digital equivalent of a page razored out along the gutter. He held it up so she could see. Ada felt her pulse arrive somewhere behind her eyes.

“That’s a queue write,” she said. “That’s the same shape as my corruption. Somebody put a block through this registry and it ate the middle and sealed the ends. It’s the same hand.”

“Or the same tool.” Vale’s voice had changed register. It had lost the skepticism and picked up something colder and considerably more useful. “Ms. Okafor. How much of tonight’s loop is on your spindle.”

“All of it. And a second copy on a separate spindle, because I’m not an idiot.”

“Good.” He produced a slip from his coat, thin stock, already printed, and pressed it to the desk plate of her console. The seal on it was dry and the case number was machine-stamped in the corner, and the number was low. Ada read it twice. Case numbers on this station ran in sequence off a station-year register, and she had catalogued enough of them to know approximately where the counter stood.

The number on the slip was two hundred and eleven cases old.

“You requisition my archive,” she said slowly.

“I requisition your archive. Full access, all spindles, all dead-letter stores, effective the seal on that slip.”

“This case number already exists.” She turned the slip around so it faced him. “Somebody opened a file before I ever called you. Before the loop entered my queue. Before 02:58.”

Vale looked at the number, and then at her, and his face did the thing a face does when a man decides not to be surprised in front of a witness.

“Key it in,” he said. “We’ll find out who.”

## 3. “Dead Letter”

The console took the requisition seal with a dry click, and the archive opened the way a locked room opens — reluctantly, all at once.

“Routing header,” Vale said. “Not the audio. The header.”

Ada pulled it up. The stamp sat under the loop’s checksum like a fossil in shale: a four-part address, three parts familiar, one part dead. She had catalogued that prefix in her first month on Calyx-9, back when the station still had departments and the departments still had rooms.

“Med Bay Theta,” she said. “Sealed six years ago, when they moved triage to the ring. Nothing’s routed through Theta.”

“Nothing current.” Vale leaned in, close enough that she could smell wool and cold metal off his coat. “But this loop crossed its wall-jack at 02:58. That’s the same minute it landed in your queue.”

“Because something in Theta handed it off.” She was already typing. “If the bay’s terminal is dead, its buffer isn’t. Buffers on decommissioned hardware don’t get wiped, they get forgotten. Give me a minute.”

He gave her forty seconds, which was nearly generous.

The Theta buffer woke slowly and coughed up its last resident — a patient log, half its fields struck through by the seal order, the rest still legible in the grey of unrefreshed screen print. Ada read down the columns. Bed, time-in, attending, badge.

Bed four. Time-in 02:58. Badge 4471.

She turned the screen. Vale read it, and his face did nothing at all, which on a man who had spent the last hour being visibly skeptical was its own kind of answer.

“That badge is yours,” she said.

“That badge is a number.”

“It’s a number on a medical admission at the exact minute the loop entered my queue, in a bay that’s been dark since before you transferred in.” Ada sat back. “So either you were in a sealed room taking a nap, or someone is building a story about you with real parts.”

Vale’s thumb found the edge of the desk and stayed there, pressing, as if the desk were a thing that needed holding down. “Play the audio again.”

“You’ve heard it twice.”

“A third time.”

She played it. The voice came up out of the spindle the way it always did — flat, unhurried, a maintenance read-back cadence with the music sanded off. *Loop check. Dock Three, 04:12. The inspector will be at the rail when it happens.*

“Again,” Vale said.

She restarted it. This time she watched him instead of the waveform, and so she saw his mouth move — one syllable, no sound, a half-beat ahead of the audio — and then his voice, quiet and level, saying, “—he’ll be at the rail,” a quarter second before the loop said it.

The spindle ran on into its hiss.

Ada stopped it.

“Transcript,” she said. “You read the transcript.”

“I read the transcript.”

“I never printed one.” She tapped the log pane, empty, the cursor still blinking at the top of a page nobody had filled. “There’s no transcript. There’s no text version. That’s a voice, that’s all it is, and you just said its next line.”

Vale straightened. Something crossed his face and was put away, folded small, the way a man folds a receipt he doesn’t want counted. “Any voice on this station can be synthesized. A splicer with a decent library could make it say anything, including things I expect to hear.”

“Including your name?”

“Especially my name. It’s in the duty roster.” He pulled his hand terminal. “I’m sealing Theta. Site integrity, evidence preservation, my authority. Nothing moves out of that buffer until my people have imaged it.”

“That’s a six-hour seal.”

“It’s an indefinite seal.”

Ada nodded slowly and set her hands on the keys, and while she nodded she started a proofing pass.

It was nothing. It was housekeeping. The proofing pass ran the record against its own checksum and, incidentally, wrote a working copy to the secondary outbox so the checksum could be compared, which was standard, which was why the watchdog didn’t flag it, and which she had turned off once in eight months and never once been asked about.

“Theta’s buffer is forty years of medical records,” she said, keeping her voice mild. “You’ll want a certified archivist to witness the image. That’s me. You’ll also want the original spindle quarantined, which means my hands, not your people’s, or the chain of custody has a hole in it a defense advocate could sail a ship through.”

“You’re telling me how to run my seal.”

“I’m telling you what your seal costs.” The progress bar crawled. Eleven percent. “And I’m telling you the loop knew a thing that hadn’t happened yet, and the bay knew you were in it at a minute you say you weren’t, and you just recited a sentence you never read. So either you’re a witness I haven’t decided how to charge yet, or you’re a piece of somebody’s story. Either way, Inspector, you don’t get to seal the room that’s writing it.”

Vale looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she hadn’t expected — he laughed, one syllable, entirely without humor, and put the terminal away.

“All right,” he said. “Copy it. Copy all of it. But you copy it to your spindle, and you log the copy under my case number, and every byte of it is mine by four in the morning.”

“Understood.”

The seal order went out. The proofing pass finished at ninety-four percent, and Ada smiled at her console in a way she was glad he couldn’t see, because a proofing pass didn’t transfer files. It verified them. Which meant the transfer she’d slipped under it had gone through on a different channel, and the channel had a receipt.

She opened the receipt.

The receipt said the record already had a destination. Copy filed, it read. Dead-letter store, tier four, case reference —

Ada read the number twice. It was the number on Vale’s requisition slip. Low. Old. Two hundred and eleven cases already closed and buried, and somebody had filed this log there once before, years ago, under the same case that hadn’t been opened yet.

“Inspector,” she said. “How long has tier four been sealed?”

“Tier four’s been sealed since before I was posted here,” Vale said, buttoning his coat. “Nothing goes in or out of it. Why?”

Ada closed the receipt and put her hands flat on the desk to keep them still.

“No reason,” she said, and began, very quietly, to copy the seal order too.

## 4. “Dead Letter”

The loop woke the console at 05:06 with a sound like a throat being cleared.

Ada had been asleep on her forearms. Her palm hit the mute pad half a second late, which meant the second phrase went out over the open room: *handover at 05:40, Merrit, Dov, badge four-one-nine.*

Vale came out of the chair with his coat half on. "Say that again."

"Recycler two. Cage interlocks." She was already pulling the bay schematic, fingers clumsy from sleep. "It names a worker. It says the cage cycles with him inside it, and the bay logs the cycle as jam clearance."

"Does it give a cause."

"It gives a time."

He leaned over her shoulder. She could smell the corridor on his collar, that flat metallic smell the ring keeps after a long shift. "Recycler two is automated. Nobody's inside the cage envelope during a cycle. That's a hard interlock."

"The interlock's on the cage," Ada said. "Not on the grabber."

The schematic bloomed. Bay two ran a sorting arm on a thirty-second rotation, and the arm had a maintenance bypass for clearing jams, and the bypass had been engaged at 04:58 by a key that wasn't a key.

"Can you stop it from here?"

"I can put a hold on the bay's maintenance queue. It's a queue. It'll take the hold the same way it takes a jam ticket." Her fingers found the string. "But it's a queue, Inspector. Queues lie down for whoever's holding the pen."

"Do it."

She did it. The hold went in at 05:11 and the queue, sullen as a mule, took it. Then Vale was through the hatch and she was running behind him with one boot unlaced, shouting the bay's internal feed up on the handheld so she could see the floor ahead of them both.

Bay two was hot, and smelled of ozone and old paper. The recyclers sounded like a room full of people tearing cloth. On the far side, past the drum, a man in a grey coverall stood inside the cage envelope with a pry bar, doing exactly what the shift manual told him not to do.

"Merrit!" Vale bellowed. "Out of the cage!"

Dov Merrit looked up, mildly, the way a man looks up who has been shouted at every day of his working life and has developed an immunity.

The arm came around.

The hold wouldn't take a fault order — maintenance state doesn't accept emergency stops — so Ada killed the hold first, meaning to get the bay's stop in behind it. She got one of the two. The queue had been waiting for exactly that: a job of its own sitting at the head of the line, timed for 05:40 but already loaded, already validated, its payload a single instruction — *cycle*. She gave it a slot the moment she released the pen. It took the slot, and it used it four minutes early.

Vale got a fist in the coverall and hauled. Merrit came out of the cage sideways and the pry bar didn't, and the arm went through the space where a man's chest had been, sheared the bar into two pieces that sang off the drum and put a dent in the wall behind.

Nobody said anything for a moment. The recyclers kept tearing their cloth.

"Who's got the bay camera?" Ada said, when she had her breath.

The camera was a sealed unit at the top of the frame, and its local buffer was a ring of twenty minutes that rolled forward and ate itself. Ada pulled the last two hours and got a clean grey field with a border of timestamps around it, the way a hole looks when someone has cut it neatly.

"It's wiped," she said.

"Wiped how."

"Not smashed. Not unplugged." She tapped through to the erase receipt, a small plain thing, the kind nobody thinks to falsify. "Somebody ran the buffer flush on the bay's own maintenance channel. Two minutes of footage gone, bridge to grabber. Erase receipt says 05:03."

Vale's face did something complicated and then stopped doing it. "The loop spoke at 05:06."

"Three minutes after."

"Three minutes *after*." He crouched down beside her, elbows on his knees, looking at the grey field as though it might get tired of him. "So the camera wasn't wiped to hide the trap. The trap wasn't set yet when it was wiped. The trap was set at 04:58."

Ada said nothing. She was reading the receipt's authorizing field, which the station's logging standard required to be populated, and which was populated.

"Somebody wiped that camera," Vale said slowly, "before there was anything on it worth wiping."

"Not somebody." She turned the handheld so he could see it. "The erase was authorized under M-7."

He didn't take it. "Say the prefix again."

"M-7. Maintenance series seven." She kept her voice flat, because flat was the only register she had that didn't shake. "Retired before the last refit. There's no live badge in the ring that can sign as M-7. I can't sign as M-7. Half the archive can't even parse it."

Vale held out his hand for the handheld.

"Run the receipt again," he said.

She ran it. The grey field held. The timestamps held. The authorizing field came back blank — wiped in its own right, freshly, sometime in the last ninety seconds — and the erase receipt that had been sitting in her log under his case number was no longer the receipt that had been written.

"Somebody's in the record," Ada said.

Vale stood up. The recyclers tore their cloth. Somewhere behind them Dov Merrit was explaining to a security drone that he'd only been clearing a jam, and the drone was explaining back.

"Then stop reading it," Vale said, "and start writing it down."

## 5. “The Voice’s Grammar”

Vale wanted it written down, so she wrote it down: on the back of a requisition slip, in pencil, because a pencil had no authorizing field left for anyone to wipe.

"Timestamp," he said.

"04:58. Camera four, Dock Three. Erase, authorized M-7." She slid the slip across the desk. "Read it twice. It doesn't get truer."

He read it once. Then again, because she was watching. Behind them the recycler chewed cloth down to fiber; Dov Merrit had stopped explaining himself, and the drone was winning on points.

"Paper," Vale said.

"Paper can't be edited in a hurry."

"Paper burns."

"Then stand closer to it than anyone else does."

Ada pulled her console around. "Before you seal anything, I want to play you a thing. My working buffer — the loop as it came in at 03:51. Before your commandeer. Before the archive."

Vale said nothing, which she took for permission.

Two minutes eleven of a maintenance voice reading status into the dark. She found the segment and let it run.

"Board fourteen, rack seven, clause nine, old counting." A click, like a knuckle being cracked. "Count it twice, then trust it."

The pencil stopped.

"Rendering floor," she said. "That's how it names the recycler bay. Dead word. Pre-refit. Board fourteen and rack seven are pre-refit numbering too — the whole M-7 series. This loop is talking in a dialect that got retired with the badges."

"Somebody copied an old template."

"Somebody copied it perfectly." She stopped the playback. "It says 'the wet dock' for Dock Three. It counts in fives, not eights. There's no parser on this ring that would generate that grammar by accident." She turned the console a few degrees toward him. "It isn't imitation, Vale. It's habit."

He went to the porthole and stood with his hands behind him, looking at nothing in particular.

"Play the rest," he said.

"The rest names the man on the sorting belt and the hour his shift ends. We've already had that conversation."

"I remember it."

"Then you remember it said 'the wet dock' and didn't blink."

A long pause, filled by the recycler.

"Anything else?" he said.

"One thing." She brought up the med-bay patient log. "That bay's been dark six years. Somebody keeps taking appointments in it."

Row forty-one: Vale, service number, 02:18, retrograde debrief, voluntary, attending blank, discharged 03:44.

"The relay cycled at 03:51," Ada said. "You came aboard on it. I watched the hatch."

"Then the log's wrong."

"Logs are wrong. This one's wrong in your service number, your blood type, and the fact that somebody broke a seal to open it." She tapped the discharge line. "03:44. Seven minutes before your relay docked."

He looked at the row the way a man looks at a bill he doesn't intend to pay. "It's a template row. Old admissions auto-fill at decommission."

"Templates don't carry the voluntary flag."

"Leave it."

She left it — on the screen, in her buffer, not in the archive.

At the hatch he turned back. "Count it twice before you file it."

His mouth closed on the end of the sentence. Something crossed his face, was picked up, and put away.

"I will," Ada said, and waited until the hatch cycled before she let out the breath.

The retired roster took forty seconds to answer. Eleven names on the M-series seven list; ten of them last-logged at decommission. The eleventh had a courtesy re-certification dated three days ago — Sayer, K., a badge that had been dead a decade. Paired-case file, also three days old. Vale, I.

Ada set the spool on the legacy drive, the quarter-inch rig on the dead-media shelf. No network. No authorizing field. A machine you had to be standing beside.

She threaded a scratch spool and cut her copy across in real time, the tape turning with a sound like a man breathing through his nose. Two minutes eleven.

Then she saw the label already on the spool. Grease pencil, block capitals, a date older than the refit.

Curiosity was a professional condition. She cued it before she wrote over it.

The counter rolled. Out of the monitor came the same voice, younger by a decade, the room around it wet and echoing the way a pipe echoes.

"Board fourteen, rack seven, clause nine, old counting. Count it twice, then trust it."

The header put the operator's name in the corner. K. SAYER.

Ada sat with that for a while.

Then she blanked the old segment, cut her own copy down over it, typed SPOOL BLANK into the inventory, signed it with her badge, and put the spool inside her coat, where the record could not read it. Folded into her cuff was the pencil slip with two names on it, one above the other, and neither of them hers.
